## Chapter VII — The Same Answer, a Different Question

The morning after the storm, a woman arrives at the shelter and finds the door closed. She asks whether anyone saw it close during the night. Someone answers that it is closed now.

The answer is true. The door is closed. She can see that herself. But the matter she has raised remains unanswered.

She wants to know whether the wind swung it shut, whether someone shut it deliberately, or whether it remained closed throughout the storm. The condition now visible cannot decide which of these occurred. One event may leave a result that several different histories could have produced. The woman does not need a theory of tense or a philosophy of evidence to notice the inadequacy. She asked about an occurrence and received a report of a condition.

A child nearby hears the exchange and looks at the door. The child has learned an expression the adults use when something is finished. He repeats it. One of the adults smiles, then shows him the wet track beneath the frame, the shallow streak where leaves were dragged against the wood by the wind. Another person remembers hearing a sound before dawn. The people begin to fit together what they can establish. They do not all remember the same things. A woman who slept lightly recalls getting up to check the fire. Someone else knows that the door had been left open before everyone settled for the night.

The conversation becomes a small inquiry. No one has to announce that it is an inquiry for its demands to become recognizable. A statement that would have been sufficient for one question does not settle another. A trace on the ground may bear upon what happened without establishing it alone. A remembered sound may be consistent with more than one cause. The people proceed because they can recognize the difference between a possibility and what has been established.

Eventually the person who checked the fire remembers fastening the door before lying down again. The question changes. If the door was closed before the wind grew strong, its present condition is no longer evidence of the movement the woman originally supposed. The woman accepts the account. Nothing has happened to the door at that moment. What has changed is the standing of one possible history within the inquiry.

This is an ordinary kind of completion, and one that language makes unusually visible. A question arises and begins to govern what can count as an adequate answer. Several statements are offered. Some are true but beside the point; others are relevant but insufficient. One eventually makes the history determinate enough for those present to continue their work.

The people do not need every possible fact about the storm. They need an answer to this question. The shelter may still need repairing. The wind may have damaged the path to the spring. Someone may still be concerned about the boat. None of these unsettled matters prevents the particular inquiry from reaching enough.

A philosopher can make the mistake of demanding that a completion be final in every respect before permitting it to be called complete. Ordinary life does not proceed that way. A question can be answered while others remain open; a repair can succeed while the object remains capable of future damage; a route can become usable while the country beyond it remains unexplored. The finitude of a completion is not an embarrassment to it. It is part of its character.

In the island community, this finitude is especially evident because language remains in development. One expression is repeated during the morning inquiry and begins to acquire a use in reports of things that happened earlier. It is not a finished grammatical system. It has not suddenly made every history easy to communicate. But it allows a distinction to be held steadily enough that speakers can return to it in a different circumstance.

Later, a child asks whether the fire went out. An adult uses the expression and gestures toward the extinguished ashes. The child looks again, and the adult discovers that the child is asking something slightly different: not whether the fire has ended, but whether it went out by itself or was put out by someone. The adult offers another expression. The child follows this one.

What has developed here is not an abstract obedience to a predetermined language. The speakers have encountered separate demands and found separate ways of making them sufficiently available. The growing language now lets them preserve distinctions across circumstances that the circumstances themselves no longer display.

And the history of that growth will remain part of the language. Children born on the island may learn the new expressions without knowing the negotiations through which they arose. Their parents may remember the difficulty of communicating an event that had left only a condition behind. The children may simply tell one another what happened yesterday.

For them the expression will be immediate. For their parents it may still carry the memory of a discovery.

The intelligible difference has not changed because it has become easier to say. The life in which it can be shared has changed profoundly.

A woman who once had to gesture at the door and reconstruct the night in fragments now says what she needs to say. Another person understands. They may still disagree about whether her report is correct, but they no longer need to negotiate how to distinguish the occurrence from the resulting condition each time the question arises.

The language has made a form of understanding more freely available. It has not delivered the community from the possibility of error. It has given them better means to recognize, pursue and sometimes correct it.

There is an intimacy between this development and the smallest act of teaching. A child reaches for a word and finds it. Another child hears the word and discovers what can be done with it. What was once a problem of communication becomes part of the ordinary environment in which new problems can be encountered.

The expression has become familiar enough that it can stop calling attention to itself.

This is one of the quiet triumphs of language: it can be newly made, learned, and then forgotten as an object of effort, while continuing to bring intelligible distinctions within reach.

## Chapter VIII — Three Ways Through

The path to the spring has become difficult to use after the storm. Branches lie across it, the ground is soft, and a stone loosened from the hillside has fallen where the track narrows. Two people go to inspect the damage. A third follows with a basket, hoping to bring water back to the settlement.

At the narrow place, the stone blocks the way. It is too large to step around without slipping down the bank. One person tests it with a branch and discovers that it can be moved. Together they push. The stone shifts, turns, and comes to rest below the path. The narrow place is open.

The people feel the familiar satisfaction of an obstacle overcome. One of them steps beyond the place where the stone lay. But a little farther on, the path disappears beneath a fallen tree. Beyond the tree the soil has washed away. They have made one passage possible, yet the route to the spring remains unusable.

There is no contradiction. The removal of the stone succeeded in the respect in which it was attempted. The larger undertaking is not complete. The way to the spring requires a connected passage, not merely the clearing of one decisive point. An achievement can be sufficient for a local difficulty and insufficient for a wider demand into which it enters.

They return to the fallen tree. One person pulls loose branches away. Another lays flat stones across the muddy ground. The work proceeds unevenly. At one point the route looks clear until the basket carrier tries to cross with its weight. The newly laid stones shift. The arrangement that allowed someone to walk through unburdened does not yet serve the purpose for which the route is needed.

So they try again. They find broader stones and set them deeper into the soil. The basket carrier passes with an empty basket, then returns with a container filled at the spring. The stones hold. The path is no longer only visible as a possible line; it can carry the undertaking for which it was cleared.

We can recognize the difference between the first success and the second without bringing a classification to the scene. The stone had occupied one decisive place. Removing it released that point of passage. The route, by contrast, had to become adequate throughout its relevant extent. A clearing here and a firm stone there were insufficient until they belonged to a continuous way through.

The people might have reached the spring by a third means.

Before they began moving the fallen tree, someone might have noticed a broad, dry track running behind the hill. If that track had led safely to the water, they could have abandoned the damaged route. The fallen stone and tree might have remained exactly where the storm left them. The old path would still be blocked. Yet its obstruction would no longer govern the question of obtaining water.

This possibility reveals another kind of change. A condition can cease to determine an undertaking without being physically removed. A route can be released from its necessity. An old plan may remain written down after it ceases to guide the work. An objection may remain true of one proposal after the proposal has been abandoned. Nothing need vanish for its former authority to end.

The third possibility is not simply the first two performed more efficiently. To move the stone is to alter a decisive obstruction. To make the damaged track usable is to achieve adequacy across a connected field. To discover that another way suffices is to change what governs the undertaking. The blocked path remains blocked, but the people need no longer make its repair the condition of their success.

One afternoon can contain all three movements. The people first move the stone, then improve the muddy route, and eventually discover that a different entrance to the spring is better when carrying large loads. What was once necessary becomes unnecessary; what was locally accomplished remains accomplished; what was sufficient for one use may need to be reconsidered for another.

The work has a history, but its history is not one undivided achievement. We can ask which difficulty was answered at each point and how that answer remained available to what followed.

The difference is especially important when an earlier failure loses its force. A person may say that the original path has been solved, although nothing has been done to restore it. The expression may convey that the water problem has been settled, but it risks concealing the way in which that happened. The people did not repair the old path. They found that they could do without it.

Such differences are not ornamental refinements of speech. They belong to the intelligibility of what occurred. We can recognize them before the community possesses fixed words to express them. We can also lose them through careless description after those words become available.

Toward evening the basket carrier returns by the new track. At its entrance, a child is trying to move the large stone that had obstructed the old path. He wants to make the place clear again. The adult watches for a moment, then shows him the other route. The child looks from one path to the other, uncertain whether the stone should still be moved.

The adult does not forbid the work. The stone may be moved another day. The old path may be worth restoring for a different purpose. But for tonight the water has been carried, and the people have what they need.

The child follows the adult up the dry track. In the morning he may return to the stone. There is no need to decide that every obstruction must remain permanently irrelevant simply because another way was found once.

The release was real, and it belonged to a particular undertaking.

The basket reaches the shelter. Someone takes the water and sets it near the fire. The people begin preparing food. The route is no longer their subject of attention. Its sufficiency has become part of the conditions of an ordinary evening.

That may be one of the most revealing signs of an intelligible completion. The matter ceases to demand its own resolution, and what it has made available can be taken up in another life of attention.


## Chapter IX — The Things That Remain

In the morning the woman who carries the water asks which of two baskets was repaired the previous afternoon. They are hanging from the same beam, woven from the same reeds, and nearly identical in size. A child points to the one on the left. The woman takes it down and finds the new binding around its handle.

She could have distinguished it by that binding before the child spoke. The child's gesture made the distinction immediately available to her, and her inspection confirmed it. The basket has not changed because she now knows which one it is. Something has become determinate in her understanding, and the determination can remain available when she turns away from the beam.

She goes to the spring, brings water back, and hangs the basket where it was before. Later someone asks which basket has the strengthened handle. She answers without needing to examine both again. The earlier recognition has become something she can carry forward.

In the shelter next door, a different kind of continuation is under way. A family has agreed that whoever prepares the evening food will leave a covered portion for the person watching the boat. The arrangement was made the previous night, and the conversation in which it was settled has long since ended. Yet when the food is ready, the cook sets a portion aside. She does not reopen the question of whether the watcher's meal belongs within the household's work. The agreement still bears upon her present action.

The arrangement has not remained as an uninterrupted conversation. Nor is its force exhausted by someone remembering the words that were spoken. The people have established a relation that continues to make some actions appropriate, some omissions recognizable, and some requests intelligible. It is possible to forget the agreement, to dispute what its terms were, or to break it. None of these possibilities means that the original settlement was merely a sequence of vanished sounds.

On the hillside, the repaired path has a third kind of afterlife. At first light three people carry water along it. They do not need to pause at every place where stones were removed or the ground was levelled. The work has become effective as an available route. What had required attention yesterday now allows other activity to proceed without making itself the centre of attention.

One achievement leaves a distinction that can stand. Another establishes a relation that continues to bear upon later conduct. Another leaves a capacity available for use. We need not convert these observations into a list before recognizing why they differ. The repaired path is not merely something people know about; it carries them. The agreement is not merely a fact about yesterday's words; it gives shape to today's obligations. The identified basket has not acquired a new function simply because its identity has become clear to someone.

Of course these descriptions can overlap. The path can be identified. The agreement can enable a practical action. The basket's strengthened handle allows it to bear a load. A person may recognize several aspects of the same living situation. What matters is to ask what particular achievement is under examination, what was sufficient for it, and how the result remains available in the continuation that concerns us.

A child watches the people taking water along the path and says that the path is finished. One of the adults replies that it was finished yesterday. The child looks at the walkers and asks why they are still using it if the work is finished. The adult takes a moment to understand the question.

Finishing the path did not finish the journeys that could be made along it. Indeed, it made them possible. The completion of one undertaking became a condition of others. The path's new usefulness is not a contradiction of its completion; it is part of what the completion has brought into the life of the settlement.

This is easy to overlook because we naturally speak as though an ending must mean that nothing more follows. But we know from ordinary activity that the end of a task often marks the beginning of its usefulness. A cup is fired so that it can be used. A letter is written so that it can be read. A bridge is completed so that people may cross. An explanation becomes sufficient so that its listener may understand something further without needing to begin the explanation again.

Nor does every completion leave an operative capacity. A difference may simply remain known. A false suspicion may lose its hold. A question may come to rest with no further action demanded. A friend may finally understand why another friend went away, and remain quietly with that understanding. To insist upon a practical consequence in every case would be to change the question and then mistake the new question for the old one.

The island language is now sufficiently developed for these different continuations to be expressed in ordinary conversation. Someone can say which basket it was, who has undertaken the evening task, and whether the path can be used. Another language might distribute the same work among different verbal forms, customary implications or arrangements of context. We do not need the expressions to resemble one another to recognize the different things they can accomplish.

An old man, visiting the spring by the path for the first time since its repair, pauses where a large stone used to block the way. He remembers struggling to move it before the storm. It lies now beside the track. For him, its new position explains a particular change. Farther along, he notices that the ground has been raised and packed into a continuous surface. Still farther, he sees the dry alternative way around the hill, which some people use when carrying larger loads.

He can recognize three different ways the journey became possible, and he can recognize that what each left available is not simply the same. The moved stone no longer obstructs that point. The prepared track supports travel across its length. The alternative route frees the settlement from dependence upon the old one. Their achievements persist within the same afternoon's walking, but their modes of sufficiency and their continuing significance are not interchangeable.

The old man reaches the spring and fills his vessel. At the water's edge a young woman asks him which way he came. He points toward the hillside. She looks where he is pointing and then asks whether that way is safe after rain.

He pauses again. Yesterday's sufficiency is not automatically an answer to every question tomorrow may raise. A route that carries dry loads today may be impassable in a storm. The completion was real. Its continuing availability can be tested in a new circumstance.

He tells her what he knows. He does not pretend to know what he has not seen. The difference between having completed a work and having established its adequacy under every future condition is allowed to remain a difference.

She thanks him and chooses the higher track. Her choice does not undo the lower path's repair. It settles a new question, within a life already made more spacious by the work of previous days.

## Chapter X — The Question That Arrived Afterward

The child who watched the basket being identified has begun to use a word that the adults employ when something is finished. She says it when a knot has been tied, when a bowl is emptied, when she has reached the last stone in a line she is counting. Sometimes the word is appropriate. Sometimes the adults smile and tell her that the work is not yet done.

One afternoon she brings a small wooden frame to the carpenter and says the word. The frame stands upright. Four pieces have been fitted together, and their corners meet. The carpenter examines it and asks what she means to use it for. She wants it to hold a cloth over a sleeping place.

He touches the upper rail. It gives way under slight pressure. The frame has been assembled, but it is not yet strong enough for the undertaking she has in mind.

The child looks at the fitted corners. All the pieces are there. She points to them one by one. The carpenter nods. He does not deny that she has accomplished the assembly. He shows her that a different question remains: will it stand under the weight it must carry?

The child is disappointed. She thought she had finished. Yet her disappointment is not the discovery that nothing was achieved. The pieces were brought together correctly. That achievement remains. What is unfinished belongs to the frame's ability to serve its intended use.

They reinforce one corner and test it again. This time another corner begins to shift. They work around the frame. A single firm joint cannot establish the sufficiency of the whole structure. The relevant strength must be distributed across the parts whose relation gives the frame its function.

Eventually it stands. A piece of cloth is laid over it. The cloth hangs too low at one end. They adjust the supports, step back, and look again. Now it can shelter the sleeping place in the way the child intended.

The child uses her word for finished once more. This time the carpenter agrees.

But later, in the evening, someone notices that rain has entered through the side of the shelter and dampened one corner of the cloth. The frame may be strong and correctly assembled; the place in which it is being used introduces another demand. They move it away from the opening.

Was the carpenter wrong to call the frame finished earlier? Not simply. The frame was adequate for the question he had asked and the test they performed. The later difficulty concerned its place within a shelter exposed to rain. To treat every new demand as proof that nothing was ever complete would make completion impossible to recognize. To treat an earlier completion as a guarantee against every new circumstance would make it impossible to learn.

We live between these errors. An achieved sufficiency must be allowed to be real, and its scope must be allowed to remain determinate.

The child's growing language is involved in this discipline. Her word for finished does not become useful merely because adults repeat it. She is learning when it can be said with justice, and what remains unsettled when it cannot. Sometimes the same word marks very different achievements. Sometimes another expression is needed to distinguish what has been assembled from what is ready to use, what has been attempted from what has succeeded, what has ended from what can now begin.

A language learns such differences through the lives of its speakers. An individual child acquires the differences through repeated participation in those lives. The developing community and the developing speaker answer to intelligible undertakings that neither has entirely invented. The community must discover what it needs to communicate; the child must discover what its expressions accomplish. In both cases the learning is richer than the accumulation of names.

That evening the carpenter sits beside the doorway and watches the girl explain the frame to another child. She points to the corner that was first strengthened and then to the place where it had to be moved to escape the rain. Her explanation is halting. The listener misunderstands the first part, thinking that the cloth had been too heavy. The girl shakes her head, presses a hand against the joint, and makes it move slightly. Then she holds it firm.

The other child understands.

The words have been few. The gesture has been enough. Yet this little exchange contains a difference between an actual failure and a merely supposed one, between correction at one point and adequacy across a structure, between an object's completed assembly and its suitability to a place. The children do not need to announce these differences as a philosophical inventory. They are learning to recognize them as part of the continuing life of their island.

The carpenter hears them laugh and returns his attention to the door. The hinge has begun to squeak. He may oil it in the morning. There is no need to attend to every unfinished thing tonight simply because he can recognize it as unfinished.

The girl carries the frame into the sleeping place. It stands where they moved it, holding the cloth above the bed. The assembly is over. The sheltering has begun.

And what has been made sufficient continues to make other things possible.

## XI. A Shelter, Three Answers

The first rain of the new season arrives late in the afternoon. It begins with large drops that fall separately enough to be counted against the leaves. Then the distance between the drops disappears, and the island settles beneath a continuous sound.

Inside the shelter, the girl who built the wooden frame watches the cloth above her sleeping place darken at one edge. She moves closer. Water is gathering along a fold and finding its way toward the bedding.

The frame itself has not collapsed. The joints are still firm, and the cloth is still held above the floor. The construction that was completed yesterday has not ceased to be a completed construction merely because rain has introduced another difficulty. Yet the sheltering she hoped it would provide is now inadequate.

She calls for the carpenter. He does not examine the frame's strongest corner. He follows the damp edge of the cloth toward the opening in the wall. There he discovers that the rain has been blown sideways through a gap above the window.

The girl had imagined that the frame needed another support. The carpenter shows her the water's path. They place a strip of material along the opening, and the dripping stops.

They wait.

This waiting is part of the work. A strip of material may look well placed while allowing water to pass behind it. The first disappearance of a drop does not establish that the gap has been sealed. They watch the edge of the cloth until it begins to dry.

The girl says that the problem is finished. The carpenter agrees about the dripping. Then he asks whether the bedding beneath has remained dry.

It has not. They move it nearer the hearth.

Nothing has been withdrawn from the repair of the window. The leak has been stopped. The bedding remains damp. Two matters were involved, and one success did not complete the other.

Later, when the rain has weakened, the girl brings the carpenter back to her frame. She presses the corner that gave way yesterday. It holds. She lifts the cloth and shows him the dry patch beneath the repaired opening. She seems to be asking whether she may now use her word for finished without being corrected.

He asks her what she means.

She points first to the joint, then to the wall, then to the bedding. She has not yet found different expressions for all three achievements. She makes them distinct by pointing.

The carpenter understands. The frame has been made firm. The leak has been stopped. The bedding is being dried. Each achievement answers to a different undertaking. They can be acknowledged separately, without requiring the girl to pretend that the whole afternoon has reached an end.

The next morning they examine the door of the storehouse. Its edge has begun to catch against the frame. The carpenter adjusts the hinge, and the door opens freely when pushed from outside. He demonstrates the movement.

A man inside asks whether the latch can be operated without help. The carpenter closes the door and tries to open it from within. His hand cannot reach the release.

The door can open. The man cannot yet open it from where he must stand.

The first demonstration was accurate. It showed a movement. The second question introduced a condition of use that the first demonstration had not established. They alter the latch and try again.

This time the man closes and opens the door himself. He does it twice, then lets another person try. There is no need to repeat the movement endlessly. They have made the relevant capacity available and tested it under the conditions for which it was required.

But a third question is still possible. Will the swollen door work after another heavy rain? The carpenter cannot answer from this single dry morning. He has allowed extra clearance and expects it to be enough. The next storm will offer a further test.

He does not therefore refuse to call the present work complete. The latch functions, the door moves, and the intended person can operate it. Those achievements are real. Their scope is not the same as a guarantee about every future day.

As they leave the storehouse, the girl stays behind to examine the latch. She has watched it being moved from outside and inside. She can now use it herself. The completion of the adjustment persists not only as a statement that the door was repaired, but as a capacity she can exercise.

The language being developed on the island must learn to carry these differences as the need arises. It must allow people to say what has happened, what is now the case, what remains usable, and what has yet to be established. It need not mark all these differences in the same way. It need not assign each one a separate word. But the speakers will have occasions when confusing them matters, and their efforts to speak together will have consequences for what becomes expressible.

The girl leaves the door unlatched while she carries a basket outside. When she returns, she closes it. The carpenter sees her test the handle and then walk away.

Nothing in her movement announces a theory. She wanted to know that she could open the door when she needed it. Now she knows, and the knowledge has become ordinary enough to use without remark.

## XII. The Word That Finds Its Limit

A few weeks later, a group of survivors travels inland to collect fruit from a stand of trees beyond the ridge. They have made the journey before. The route is familiar to several of them, though a younger man is going for the first time.

Near the ridge, they reach a fork. One path climbs through a narrow gap in the rock. The other descends into denser vegetation. Both were once passable, and the community has begun using a familiar expression for each.

The younger man repeats the word for the climbing path and points uphill. The others nod. They have understood his intention.

But partway through the gap they find that a fallen slab has narrowed the passage. An adult can pass sideways. A basket carried across the shoulders cannot.

One of the travellers says that the path is open. Another answers that it is blocked.

They are not necessarily disagreeing about what is before them. The first has just passed between the rocks. The second is carrying the wide basket that must be brought through. Each has a use of the path in view, and the difference has become consequential.

The travellers set down their loads.

One lifts the basket and measures it against the opening. Another proposes passing the fruit through separately. A third points to the descending path, which takes longer but is wide enough for the baskets.

Their language can already express openness and blockage in many familiar situations. Here the apparent simplicity of those expressions has been interrupted by a more exact demand. Open for whom? Open for what? Open under which conditions?

They do not need to conduct a formal discussion about the meaning of their words. They have to carry the fruit home. They turn the baskets and try the gap. When the contents begin to spill, they stop.

The younger man points to the narrow passage and makes a gesture indicating a person moving through it. Then he holds both arms wide and moves them toward the same opening. The second gesture ends against the rock.

The others understand.

The path is passable for the person and not for the load as it is being carried.

A difference that the word alone had not made sufficiently precise becomes available through the demonstration. In later journeys, the speakers may develop a customary way to express the distinction without repeating the whole performance. They may already have expressions in their different first languages that make the relevant qualification easy. Some of these may enter their developing common speech.

What matters here is that the living situation has given their attempts at expression something to answer to.

The younger man had not been wrong to regard the path as open in every possible respect. He had encountered one way in which it was open. The other traveller had encountered a way in which it failed their task. The group needed a shared determination adequate to the journey they were actually making.

Eventually they choose the lower path. It is longer, and it carries them through mud, but the baskets remain intact.

The obstruction in the upper passage has not been removed. No one claims to have repaired it. Nevertheless, it no longer governs the journey home. The group has found a way through the difficulty by leaving that requirement behind.

When they reach the settlement, someone asks whether the upper passage has been cleared.

The younger man answers no.

Someone else asks whether the fruit has arrived.

Yes, he says, and places a basket on the ground.

Both replies are true. They settle different questions.

The first preserves an unfinished condition that might matter on another day. The second acknowledges an achieved arrival. To treat the successful journey as proof that the upper passage is clear would be a mistake. To treat the blocked passage as proof that the journey could not be completed would be another.

At the evening meal, the travellers recount what happened. A child who has never seen the ridge asks why they did not simply carry the fruit through the narrow opening one piece at a time.

The question is sensible. The travellers had considered that possibility, but darkness was approaching and the fruit would have taken too long to transfer.

The child has supplied another imaginable completion. The actual journey took a different path.

An intelligible possibility can remain open in thought even after a practical decision has been settled. The child has not undone the journey by imagining an alternative. Nor does the fact that the group arrived make the alternative unintelligible.

One of the adults draws the two paths in the earth with a stick. The shorter route has a narrow place marked across it. The longer route curves beneath the ridge and joins the road near the trees.

The child traces the shorter line with a finger. Then she traces the longer one.

The drawing makes both routes available in the same view. It permits the child to compare their lengths and understand why a shorter route might fail to be the more useful one. It also gives the travellers a way to show the difference between a blockage that remained and a demand for passage that had been met otherwise.

When the conversation ends, the drawing is left beside the fire. Before morning, footsteps and wind will scatter it. Its disappearance will not undo the route the people travelled or the understanding that was reached at the meal.

The child may remember the two paths. The younger man may use the new distinction during another journey. Someone may eventually make the distinction customary in the community's speech.

The expression has not merely been stored. It has entered a developing practice in which later situations can call upon it.

The next day a woman carrying a large vessel approaches the storehouse door. She stops and looks at its width. The door opens easily, but the vessel will not pass upright.

She turns it sideways.

Now there is room.

No one thinks to announce that the door has changed. The door's opening is the same. The relevant relation between the opening and what must pass through it has become different.

The woman carries the vessel inside. She sets it down and closes the door behind her.

What had been a difficulty becomes an ordinary movement. The solution is not merely remembered. It works.

## XIII. The Marks the Rain Did Not Keep

By morning the drawing beside the fire has disappeared. Rain has washed one of its lines into the earth, and footsteps have blurred the other. The woman who drew the routes does not look for it. She is preparing to go back toward the ridge, this time to collect leaves that are useful for weaving.

A child asks whether she will take the shorter path.

The woman says no. The fallen slab still narrows the gap, and the baskets will be wide again.

The child looks toward the ground where the drawing used to be. Nothing remains that would allow a stranger to reconstruct the two lines. Yet the woman knows which route will serve the journey. Her knowledge is not the drawing. The drawing made something recognizable that can now be carried into another undertaking.

The child asks for another drawing.

The woman marks two lines on a piece of bark. The shorter one passes between a pair of stones. The longer one follows the slope and curves around them. This time the child draws a basket at the end of each line.

The woman studies the bark. One basket is wide, the other narrow. The child has noticed something that yesterday's account left largely implicit: what can pass through a gap depends upon the thing that must pass. The picture is not merely a copy of the vanished lines. It makes another difference available.

They take the piece of bark to the younger man who had made the journey. He recognizes the route, then points to the narrower basket and indicates that it could probably pass through the rocky gap. The child nods. The route has not changed since yesterday. What is now being considered is a different possible use of it.

The drawing does not answer the practical question completely. They have not measured the basket against the passage. The narrow basket may still catch, or it may pass easily. But a possibility has been made definite enough to investigate. The child's picture has opened a question whose answer will require an encounter with the actual gap.

The woman places the bark beside the doorway and leaves. The child does not accompany her. Instead, she carries a smaller basket to the storehouse and tests it against the door. It passes without turning.

She then tries the larger basket. It must be tilted.

She has begun to find the difference between recognizing that a thing can pass and recognizing what must be done to make it pass. The door is not an obstacle in both cases in the same way. With the small basket, nothing further is required. With the large one, a movement must be discovered.

Later she tells another child how to tilt the large basket. The second child attempts the movement, but its handle catches against the frame. The first child takes the basket back, turns it with the handle facing downward, and tries again. It passes.

The second child repeats the movement and succeeds.

It would be easy to say that a piece of knowledge has been transmitted. That is true enough for ordinary speech. Yet the different achievements are worth remaining close to. The first child discovered a workable orientation. She then attempted to communicate it. The second child recognized enough to reproduce the movement. The practical capacity is now available to both of them.

The door has undergone no alteration.

The basket has not become smaller.

The children have learned how the relation between the two can be made to work.

When the woman returns that afternoon, the children show her what they have learned. She praises them and asks whether the same movement will allow the large basket through the narrow passage in the ridge.

They do not know.

The child who found the movement considers the question. The passage between rocks may be taller than the door, but the rocks do not have a smooth frame. The basket might catch at another place. The useful movement at the storehouse has not automatically become a complete solution at the ridge.

Nothing here diminishes what the children have accomplished. It reveals the limits of its present reach.

The next day they accompany the adults to the passage. The small basket fits. The large one does not pass, even when tilted. Its handle catches between two uneven stones.

The children try again, then set the basket down. One adult unfastens the handle and carries the basket through separately. On the other side, the handle is attached again.

The path can be used for the larger basket, but only through a more complicated activity. The obstacle has not disappeared. The load has been temporarily altered, then restored.

When they return to the settlement, the child draws three pictures on fresh bark: a basket passing through a door, the same basket failing between rocks, and the basket carried through after its handle has been removed.

The pictures are rough, but the sequence can be followed. They do not merely name the objects. They make the course of an undertaking intelligible: an earlier success, a new failure, and a different way of meeting the demand.

Another survivor looks at the pictures and asks whether the rocky passage has been cleared.

No, the child answers.

Can they carry the basket through?

Yes, she says, but the handle must come off.

The difference is now easy to express in the community's developing speech. One sentence acknowledges an enduring obstruction. The other makes an effective procedure available.

The next time someone brings a basket to the ridge, the children need not go with them. They can tell the person what to do. The marks on the bark can help, but the person may understand from the words alone. What was learned by handling a particular basket has entered the common possibilities of the group.

The drawings are later used as kindling. The woman hesitates before putting them into the fire, then finds that they are dry and will burn well.

Nothing about the journey requires the bark to be kept forever. The knowledge is not guaranteed by its disappearance, nor would the bark alone have guaranteed that every future basket could be carried through. But the procedure has already been repeated and understood. It belongs to the community's available ways of meeting a familiar difficulty.

The child watches the fire take hold. She remembers where the handle caught between the rocks.

Tomorrow she may have forgotten the shape of her own drawing. She will still know what she was trying to show.

## XIV. The Word That Can Be Wrong Before It Exists

A boy brings a cooking vessel to the common hearth. Its bottom has cracked. He has poured water into it twice, and each time a little has escaped into the dust.

An older woman asks whether the vessel is empty.

The boy looks inside. There is still water at the bottom. He shakes his head.

The woman asks another question, using a word that several of the islanders have begun using for things that no longer perform the work for which they are needed.

The boy has heard the word applied to a fishing net with a torn mesh and to a roof whose covering had been lifted by wind. He knows the vessel cannot hold water reliably, and he repeats the word.

The woman understands him.

But a younger child standing nearby points to the crack and uses the same word for the narrow opening in the clay. The older woman pauses. The word has been taken to name both the defect and the failure that the defect makes possible.

Sometimes those uses will be close enough for the speakers' purposes. At other times the difference will matter. A vessel may have a hairline crack without leaking. A vessel may leak because its lid is badly fitted, though the body is sound. A net can fail because it is torn, or because it has been set in the wrong place.

The woman does not lecture the children about categories. She fills the vessel with a little water and lets them watch the line of moisture spreading beneath it. Then she points to the crack and draws a narrow line in the dust.

The younger child follows the line with her finger.

The boy points again to the water escaping and repeats the word.

The woman uses another expression that the community has recently begun to share. Its familiar use concerns a thing being unable to accomplish what it is intended to do. She repeats the two expressions in sequence, indicating first the crack and then the loss of water.

The children attempt the distinction.

Their words are still uncertain. The expressions may shift in use during the months that follow. Different speakers may continue to favour the same word for several related circumstances.

Yet the practical difference is there to be encountered. A crack is not the same thing as water escaping through it. The visible defect and the failure of the vessel to hold water stand in a relation, but they are not identical.

The children can now be mistaken in a more definite way. The younger one may point to a harmless mark in an unbroken pot and say that it is leaking. The older woman can test the pot and show that it holds water. The mark is there; the claimed failure is not.

There is a moment in this learning that deserves to be held in view. Before the community possesses an entirely settled expression for the difference, the speakers can already recognize that some attempted uses do not fit what has happened.

They do not have to know the final convention in advance. Their correction need not consist in retrieving a perfect rule from memory. They can return to the vessel, look again, try again, and find an expression that makes the relevant circumstance more precisely available.

The language grows through this work.

Later, the boy visits a family whose shelter has a small opening beneath its roof. When the wind blows from the east, rain comes through. He uses the familiar word for a thing's failure, then gestures toward the opening. The family understands him. Someone has already intended to seal the gap, but the repair has not yet been done.

The boy offers to help.

A strip of cloth is pressed into the opening. They pour water over the roof and wait. No drops appear inside. The repair has succeeded for this test.

The boy repeats the word for failure, then shakes his head.

The family laughs. They have understood that what the word once described no longer governs the situation. He is not denying that the opening was there. He is recognizing that the roof no longer allows water through it in the way that mattered.

The next day, the cloth is still in place. The family has stopped setting a bowl beneath the gap.

The bowl itself remains useful. It can hold fruit, gather seeds, or be placed beneath another leak if one appears. But it is no longer required as protection from this opening. A former necessity has ceased to direct the arrangement of the room.

The boy moves the bowl to a shelf. In doing so he makes space for a small stool.

When he sits there, someone asks him what he changed.

He points to the repaired gap and then to the empty place on the floor. The answer he gives is still awkward. He does not yet have a single expression that carries the whole difference from the old arrangement to the new.

But he understands why the bowl could be moved.

The older woman, visiting that evening, helps him say it. She begins with the fact that water no longer enters. He adds that they no longer need the bowl. Together they find a way to make the relation intelligible to someone who was absent when the repair was made.

The boy listens to their words. One expression is now enough to describe the condition of the roof. Another makes clear that an earlier requirement has been released. A third might tell the story of how the cloth was fitted.

These statements are related, but none is simply a substitute for the others.

Outside, rain begins again.

The boy looks toward the roof. He waits through the first heavy drops, then goes to the corner where the bowl used to stand.

The floor is dry.

He does not say anything. He sits back on the stool.

What has become intelligible to him is not less real because he leaves it unspoken. The language has helped him share and retain the achievement, but the dry floor is already answering the question he came to ask.
