THE FOUNTAINHEAD OF INTELLIGIBILITY II

A Second Beginning

Chapter I — Before the Word

A door closes.

There is a brief movement, a sound, and then stillness. The door, which had stood open, now stands closed.

Someone watching from across the room has seen what happened. Another person, entering a moment later, sees only what is now the case.

The first has witnessed a change. The second encounters a condition.

Neither needs to speak.

Nor is either waiting for a sentence before the difference becomes available.

A child might have watched the door with interest. A dog might have turned at the sound. Someone carrying a tray might have been relieved to see that the draught had stopped. Someone expecting a visitor might have been disappointed.

The same occurrence enters several lives differently. Yet there is nothing obscure about the difference between the door's movement into a closed position and the condition that remains when the movement is over.

We can say that the door closed.

We can say that the door is closed.

These are different things to say because they make different things intelligible.

We should allow ourselves to be surprised by this.

Not by the fact that doors close. Nor by the fact that languages possess ways of speaking about them.

The surprise is that the difference to which these expressions answer is already there for us to encounter.

A language can express it well or badly. A person can describe it accurately or confuse it with something else. A child can learn how to communicate it. A translator can preserve it or lose it.

But the difference is not waiting for any of these activities to bring it into existence.

The door closes before anyone says that it has closed.

And the door remains closed whether anyone mentions its condition or not.

This is where we shall begin.

Not with a definition of intelligibility, since a definition would already require us to recognize the very thing we wish to investigate.

Not with a theory of language, since the possibility of language is among the things we shall need to understand.

Not with a list of categories, however carefully devised, since we have yet to see why there should be categories at all.

We begin with something happening and something becoming available to understanding.

The difference between these two descriptions is itself worth noticing.

A thing may happen without anyone noticing it. A door may close in an empty house. Rain may begin over a field no one is watching. A stone may fall from a wall and lie where it lands.

When someone subsequently encounters what has occurred, the event may become intelligible through its consequences. The fallen stone is found on the ground. The wall has a gap. The earth beneath is disturbed.

A person examines the scene and understands something that was not present to them when it happened.

The understanding has its own occurrence. It arrives.

Perhaps the person sees at once what must have happened. Perhaps there is uncertainty, followed by closer inspection, followed by recognition.

In either case, something becomes determinate for someone.

The movement from uncertainty to recognition is not the same as the stone's movement from the wall to the ground.

Yet both can be understood.

This is already an extraordinary situation.

We inhabit a world in which things happen, conditions persist, and relations obtain. We also inhabit lives in which those happenings, conditions and relations become recognizable.

And then we speak.

We tell another person what we saw. We ask what happened. We answer a question. We correct a mistaken account. We find that a sentence has made something clear.

Speech is not an addition to an otherwise empty life.

It enters a life already filled with differences that matter.

Consider a child who has not yet learned to say again.

Someone lifts the child, lowers them, and lifts them once more. The child laughs. The movement stops. The child reaches upward, anticipating its return.

The word is absent. The expectation is not.

Later the child will learn to ask for the movement to be repeated. Eventually there may be many ways of asking, each carrying a different urgency, playfulness or insistence.

But the acquisition of the word does not mark the first appearance of repetition in the child's life.

The word makes a recognizable possibility available in another way.

It becomes possible to request repetition when the movement has ceased, when the person is farther away, when the circumstances have changed. It becomes possible to talk about repetition rather than merely participate in it.

Language transforms what can be done with an intelligible difference.

It does not follow that language must have created the difference.

The distinction is easily overlooked.

Its consequences extend across languages and their speakers.

For there are thousands of languages in the world, and their speakers do not inhabit thousands of mutually inaccessible orders of intelligibility.

They can meet.

They can misunderstand one another.

They can discover that they have misunderstood.

They can attempt to make something clearer.

And sometimes, without possessing a single word in common, they can succeed.

Imagine two travellers standing beside a damaged bridge.

One points to a broken support. The other looks toward the road beyond the bridge and shakes their head.

The travellers speak different languages.

One draws a line through the air to indicate another route. The other points toward a path through the trees.

They begin to walk.

There may have been considerable uncertainty between them. The first gesture might have been taken to indicate the river rather than the bridge. The path might not lead where either hoped.

Yet their exchange is possible.

Something about the broken support has become recognizable as a reason not to cross. Something about the path has become recognizable as an alternative.

The gestures do not resemble sentences in any elaborate way. They may be improvised and never used again.

Nevertheless, they accomplish something intelligible.

One person makes a difficulty available to another. Together they establish what to do next.

If we call this communication, we should not let the familiar word conceal the achievement.

How did they know there was anything to communicate?

How did either recognize what the other was attempting to make available?

And what allowed their uncertain expressions to become sufficient for the situation?

We are accustomed to asking how communication occurs within a language.

Here we encounter communication at the edge of any shared language.

The distinction is important because we can now ask a different question.

What makes a shared language possible in the first place?

Imagine an aircraft carrying three hundred people from many countries. During a long journey, something goes terribly wrong. The aircraft comes down near a remote island, and those who survive reach its shore.

Their circumstances are extraordinary, and they are far from any settled community that can offer assistance.

They have no common language.

There may be small groups who understand one another, but no language belongs to everyone. Their histories, vocabularies and inherited ways of speaking are different.

Yet they must live together.

They need water.

They need shelter.

They must care for the injured.

They must distinguish food that can be eaten from food that cannot.

They must discover which paths lead inland, which places flood, where the sun gives way to shade, and what changes when the weather turns.

At first, communication may be difficult.

Someone points. Someone imitates. A word from one language is repeated by speakers of another. Gestures acquire customary meanings. A particular sound becomes associated with a particular activity.

The first shared expressions may be awkward.

One expression may have to accomplish many things.

A gesture may be understood only when the object is visible.

A word may mean one thing to the person who introduced it and something slightly different to the person who has begun using it.

But life continues.

A shelter is built.

A fire is maintained.

Water is carried.

Arrangements are made, broken, repaired and made again.

The community's means of expression change under the demands of these activities.

A distinction that was once dependent upon gesture becomes expressible through a word.

A distinction that once required the whole situation to be visible becomes available when the situation is absent.

A distinction that repeatedly caused confusion becomes more reliably expressed.

Children are born.

They grow up among the developing conventions. What their parents assembled from fragments becomes the ordinary environment of their own first speech.

The language is no longer merely an emergency arrangement between strangers.

It has a history, regularities, habits, possibilities of error and ways of putting errors right.

Its speakers tell stories.

They anticipate events.

They describe what has happened.

They ask what remains to be done.

They say that the door closed.

They say that the door is closed.

We should attend carefully to what has occurred.

The islanders have developed a language. But they did not first develop a world in which events differ from conditions, actions from their consequences, possibilities from their fulfilment, or successful attempts from unsuccessful ones.

They arrived in such a world.

Their developing language became a way of inhabiting that intelligible life together.

The distinction between an event and a condition did not have to be imported from any of the languages the survivors once spoke.

It was encountered in the lives they continued to lead.

And when their common expressions proved inadequate to something they needed to distinguish, that inadequacy itself could become recognizable.

A person might try to say that a shelter had collapsed during the night.

Another might understand that it remained in danger of collapsing.

The difference matters. One situation calls for rebuilding. The other may call for strengthening a structure that still stands.

They try again.

One points to the fallen beams.

Another makes a gesture indicating the earlier movement.

An expression begins to acquire a more definite use.

On another occasion, a similar difficulty arises, and the distinction is made more quickly.

With repetition, the community's means of expressing it become increasingly familiar.

We might say that the language is improving.

But this description invites a question that deserves far more attention than it usually receives.

Improving in relation to what?

The speakers are not consulting a grammar of the language they have yet to develop.

There is no completed dictionary against which every attempted expression can be measured.

Nor is there an external committee instructing them which distinctions a proper language must possess.

Nevertheless, they can recognize when communication has failed.

They can discover that one expression has been taken for another.

They can recognize that an intended distinction was lost.

They can try to make that distinction available again.

And they can succeed.

The possibility of recognizing inadequacy is already remarkable.

For an expression cannot simply be inadequate in the abstract. It is inadequate to something.

A warning that does not warn effectively may leave someone exposed to danger.

An instruction that does not distinguish what has been done from what remains to be done may leave a task incomplete.

An explanation that does not establish the relevant relation may leave its listener unable to understand why something happened.

The inadequacy becomes intelligible through what the expression failed to accomplish.

And this failure may become the occasion for developing a better expression.

A language is therefore not merely an accumulation of sounds that a community happens to accept.

It is a developing practice through which people try to make intelligible achievements available to one another.

Its forms are learned, repeated, altered and preserved within that activity.

The intelligibility of the life being articulated gives those alterations their point.

This is not to imagine that every new expression is a step toward perfection. Languages change for countless reasons. People forget, improvise, imitate, joke, borrow and invent. A convention can spread simply because a community takes pleasure in it.

But when speakers encounter an inability to say what they need to say, they encounter a particular kind of pressure.

The difference they need to communicate has not disappeared merely because their present language expresses it poorly.

They must find a way.

And often they do.

The significance of this is immense.

An intelligible distinction can make demands upon its own expression.

We need to understand this carefully.

The distinction is not a little person issuing commands. It does not know that a language is developing. It does not stand outside the community and demand a particular grammatical construction.

Rather, the people encounter situations in which a difference matters, and their attempts to communicate that difference can succeed or fail.

The pressure arises within the encounter between what is intelligible and what their present means of expression can accomplish.

Their language answers to something.

It is this answering that we shall need to investigate.

For the same phenomenon appears in a setting so familiar that we hardly notice its strangeness.

Someone decides to learn a second language.

At first, the undertaking may seem to consist of replacing one set of words with another.

A person learns the names of objects, the forms of verbs, the conventions of greeting, the arrangements of questions and answers.

They recognize a word in the new language and silently supply its equivalent in the familiar one.

An unfamiliar sentence becomes an awkward journey through a known sentence.

The learner hears.

They translate.

They understand.

Then something begins to change.

A familiar expression is heard, and its significance arrives without the intervening sentence.

A question is answered before the learner has consciously converted its words.

A joke becomes funny at the moment it is spoken, rather than after its construction has been examined.

A warning produces an immediate response.

An invitation is recognized in the tone and phrasing through which it is offered.

The learner is no longer continually carrying each expression back into the first language to discover what it means.

They are beginning to understand directly in the second.

And eventually, if fluency develops sufficiently, something extraordinary becomes ordinary.

The new language becomes a place in which the person can think, anticipate, question, remember, correct, explain and settle matters without first performing a translation.

They have not acquired a second world in which doors close.

They have not acquired a second experience of the difference between an event and a condition.

They have acquired another fluent means of encountering, articulating and sharing intelligibility.

Of course, the new language may disclose unfamiliar distinctions. Its expressions may draw attention toward features of experience that the learner previously passed over. Its inherited practices may introduce forms of address, intimacy, obligation, humour and social understanding that require time and participation to recognize.

The learner may discover more than they brought to the undertaking.

But this discovery is itself intelligible.

A new distinction can become recognizable because the learner already lives within the possibility of recognizing differences, encountering unfamiliar relations and understanding more than was understood before.

The acquisition of another language is not a departure from intelligibility into something foreign to it.

It is an enlargement of the ways in which intelligibility can be lived.

The islanders and the language learner appear to be engaged in opposite tasks.

The islanders must develop a shared language where none yet exists.

The learner must enter a language that others already share.

Yet the two activities reveal a common condition.

In each case, intelligibility is already operative.

The islanders can create new expressions because their lives present them with things to distinguish, relate, anticipate, correct and resolve.

The learner can acquire unfamiliar expressions because those expressions can become recognizable as accomplishing things within an intelligible life.

The movement of understanding does not wait until every linguistic difficulty has been overcome.

It is already involved in overcoming them.

And this may be why the difference between speaking a language haltingly and speaking it fluently is so profound.

At first, a person has to work to discover what an expression has accomplished.

Later, the accomplishment becomes directly recognizable.

The sentence is no longer a puzzle standing between the speaker and the matter being understood.

It becomes a way in which that matter is immediately available.

There is a change in fluency, but not a change in the fundamental fact that understanding can occur.

What has changed is the person's access to a particular language's means of making understanding possible.

We should now turn to another familiar wonder.

For thousands of years, human beings have been able to preserve discoveries across changes of language.

Consider the geometrical relation we call Pythagoras' theorem.

The words in which that relation has been taught have varied from one culture and century to another. The notation has varied. Proofs have taken different forms. A person may encounter the relation in a language that did not exist when it was first discovered.

And still the relation is available.

A child can encounter it without knowing the history of its transmission.

The child may draw a right triangle and measure its sides.

At first there may be nothing particularly remarkable about it.

The triangle has three sides.

One angle is a right angle.

Someone marks the lengths and introduces a relation among their squares.

The child follows the calculation.

Then perhaps there is a moment when the significance changes.

The relation is not merely true of the particular triangle in the drawing.

It belongs to every right triangle under the relevant geometric conditions.

The child turns the page and draws another.

The lengths change.

The relation remains.

A third drawing is made.

Again, the relation holds.

The child may initially regard this as an interesting coincidence.

Then an explanation is offered.

Perhaps squares are drawn upon the sides. Perhaps a larger square is partitioned into regions whose areas can be compared. Perhaps the proof proceeds through a different construction.

However it is accomplished, the child comes to see why the relation holds.

There is a new achievement.

Before, the child may have known that several examples worked.

Now the child understands something about what makes the relation necessary within the geometry being studied.

The difference matters.

It is possible to repeat a true statement without understanding why it is true.

It is possible to calculate a correct answer by following a procedure whose significance remains obscure.

It is possible to recognize a pattern without yet seeing what establishes it.

And it is possible for an explanation to change this situation.

The mathematical relation becomes intelligible in a new way.

Not simply encountered.

Not simply remembered.

Understood through what makes it hold.

Consider how extraordinary it is that this can happen so long after the original discovery.

The child does not require the original words.

Nor is the child's understanding limited to reconstructing the thoughts of a particular ancient mathematician.

The child can discover the relation through a proof that did not exist when the relation was first recognized.

A later expression may disclose what an earlier expression left unexplained.

An old claim may become newly intelligible.

Thus the persistence of intelligibility through language is not merely the preservation of an identical possession.

It can include the development of understanding itself.

What one generation recognizes, another may explain.

What one language expresses awkwardly, another formulation may make clearer.

What was once shown by a particular construction may later be shown through a more general relation.

The claim remains answerable to the same geometrical conditions even as the ways of understanding it develop.

And this brings us to a distinction that is easy to miss.

The enduring truth of the relation is not the same thing as the persistence of somebody's understanding of it.

A theorem may be forgotten by an entire community.

A manuscript may be lost.

A mathematical practice may disappear.

Centuries later, another community may discover the relation independently.

The truth has not travelled between the communities through an unbroken chain of words.

The discovery has occurred again.

This possibility is among the strongest invitations to reconsider the relationship between intelligibility and language.

If a relation can be discovered independently, the capacity to recognize it cannot depend upon inheriting the precise linguistic expression in which another person previously stated it.

The discovery requires activity, attention, methods and understanding.

But it does not require the survival of one original sentence.

A relation may be encountered through several histories of recognition.

The histories are different.

The relation remains recognizable.

Now consider what happens when a mathematical claim is mistranslated.

Suppose the original asserts a relation that holds for right triangles.

The translation omits the condition that the triangle must have a right angle.

The resulting sentence may be grammatical.

Its words may be familiar.

Its meaning may be perfectly clear.

But it now makes a different claim.

And the new claim does not hold in the same way.

The error can be discovered.

A reader can draw a triangle that is not right-angled and show that the translated statement is inadequate.

Nothing about the elegance of the translation can rescue it.

The expression is answerable to the relation it purports to state.

And the relation makes the difference between success and failure recognizable.

This is not a special property bestowed upon mathematics by mathematical vocabulary.

The same general phenomenon appears whenever an expression is answerable to something it seeks to make intelligible.

A witness can report that a person left the room.

Another witness can report that the person remained.

The accounts may be compared with what occurred.

A translator can turn a promise into a prediction and thereby alter the commitment conveyed.

A historian can omit the condition under which a decision was made and leave the reader with a misleading understanding of the event.

A friend can repeat another person's explanation while losing the very distinction that made it illuminating.

In each case, a sentence may be intelligible while failing to preserve the intelligible achievement at issue.

The words are not sufficient merely because they are words.

We must ask what they accomplish.

The mathematical example gives this question a particular clarity.

But the question belongs to language more generally.

And perhaps it belongs beyond language altogether.

A carpenter can measure incorrectly.

A musician can enter too early.

A person can mistake the path to a house.

A doctor can misidentify the cause of a symptom.

A child can reach for an object that has been moved.

These are different kinds of mistake, with different ways of being discovered and corrected.

Yet none requires us to suppose that error begins only when a sentence is formed.

A mistake may be made in action.

Its inadequacy can become recognizable through what happens next.

A correction can be achieved without a verbal explanation.

An explanation can then make the correction available to someone else.

Here we begin to see why the study of intelligibility must not be confined to the study of propositions.

Propositions are extraordinary achievements. They allow things to be stated, assessed, remembered and communicated with great precision.

But the field in which correctness and incorrectness, sufficiency and insufficiency, recognition and misrecognition become possible is not exhausted by the sentence.

A sentence is one way of entering that field.

It is not the whole of it.

Return to the child studying the triangle.

At one moment the child is copying symbols.

At another the child is checking a calculation.

At another the child is studying a diagram.

Then the child sees why the relation holds.

There may be no new sentence at the instant of recognition.

The proof may be the same proof that was on the page a moment earlier.

What changes is the child's understanding.

Something previously unrecognized becomes recognizable.

The child may say, "Now I see."

Those words do not constitute the whole of what has happened.

They acknowledge it.

And when the child later explains the proof to someone else, another achievement becomes possible.

One understanding becomes available for another person to acquire.

The explanation may succeed.

It may fail.

It may need to be repaired.

The relation, the proof, the child's recognition, the explanation, and the listener's eventual understanding are not one event.

But they belong together within a life of intelligibility.

And it is precisely their belonging together that makes the passage of understanding across persons and centuries possible.

There is something moving here that is not simply a succession of sentences.

There is the recurring possibility of an intelligible achievement.

A truth can be recognized.

An explanation can be given.

A failure can be discovered.

A correction can be made.

Understanding can arise again.

We should not hurry to reduce this to a single mechanism.

We should instead remain with the extraordinary fact that these achievements can answer to one another.

A proof answers a mathematical question.

A translation answers an earlier expression.

A correction answers an inadequacy.

An explanation answers a difficulty in understanding.

A learner's recognition answers the effort of explanation.

And each achievement may become the occasion for another.

This is not the permanence of language.

It is the continuing availability of intelligibility through language's changing forms.

Chapter II — What Answers to What

The next morning, the door will not open.

Someone pulls it, then pushes. The frame has swollen during the night. The wood presses against the wood, and the simple movement that had been possible yesterday is now prevented.

The person tries again.

A second person comes to help. Together they examine the frame and discover where it catches.

One brings a tool.

A small amount of wood is removed.

The door moves.

There is satisfaction in this, but the satisfaction is not the whole event.

At first, something that ought to have moved could not move. Then the obstruction was discovered. An alteration was made. What had been prevented became possible again.

The door's condition has changed.

The people's understanding has changed.

Their practical situation has changed.

And the work has reached a point at which they can stop.

Nothing about the event requires them to possess a fully developed common language.

They may speak while working. They may gesture. One may take the tool from the other and indicate the place where it should be used.

They can disagree about the cause of the problem.

They can try a solution that fails.

They can recognize the failure and attempt something else.

Finally, the door opens.

That final movement answers the difficulty with which they began.

The relation between the difficulty and its resolution is not supplied retrospectively by a sentence. The sentence may describe it, and do so very well, but the repair has already occurred.

We should pay attention to the word answers.

An answer is not always a sentence.

A bridge across a stream can answer the difficulty of crossing.

A completed meal can answer hunger.

A returned object can answer a search.

A remembered promise can answer an expectation.

A gesture can answer a question.

An alteration can answer a defect.

These answers are not interchangeable. They do not all achieve their sufficiency in the same way.

The bridge does not answer the stream by explaining it.

The meal does not answer hunger by representing it.

The repaired door does not answer the obstruction by denying that the obstruction existed.

In each case, something becomes sufficient in relation to something else.

And the nature of that sufficiency belongs to what has occurred.

We do not decide, simply by choosing words, whether the door can now open.

The person who repaired it discovers whether the work has succeeded by trying it.

If the door remains stuck, the work is unfinished.

If it opens freely, the original difficulty has been answered.

We are approaching another feature of intelligibility.

An achievement may be recognizable not only because something is now the case, but because what is now the case fulfils, resolves or makes good something that was previously outstanding.

This introduces a direction into understanding.

There is something to be done.

There is an attempt.

There may be failure.

There is further attention.

And then, sometimes, there is enough.

The word enough is especially important.

It does not necessarily mean that nothing more could ever be done.

The repaired door may later be painted. Its hinges may be replaced. A better door may eventually be made.

Yet for the present difficulty, the work can be complete.

It opens.

That is enough.

Human life is filled with these local sufficiencies.

A question can be answered without answering every question.

A journey can end without ending movement.

An explanation can become adequate without exhausting its subject.

A disagreement can be settled without the people involved becoming identical in their outlook.

An intelligible completion is not the disappearance of every possible continuation.

Indeed, some completions make continuations newly possible.

Because the door opens, someone can carry water into the shelter.

Because the explanation has become clear, the listener can attempt the work independently.

Because a promise has been fulfilled, another undertaking can begin.

Because a question has been settled, attention can turn elsewhere.

The achievement remains involved in what follows.

Sometimes it remains as an established condition.

Sometimes as a consequence that must still be reckoned with.

Sometimes as a capacity now available for use.

We will eventually need to understand these differences with care.

But here, at the beginning, we should resist the urge to name them too quickly.

The islanders have finished repairing the door.

The person holding the tool puts it down.

Another person opens and closes the door several times, testing the movement.

The child who watched yesterday has come back.

This time the child pushes the door.

It opens.

The child laughs, pulls it shut, and opens it again.

Someone says a word that has become familiar among the people living in the shelter.

The child repeats it.

Nobody present can say exactly when that word became theirs.

Yesterday it was unfamiliar to someone. A week ago it may have belonged to only one of the survivors. A month from now it may be used by everyone.

The word has a history.

The movement has its own conditions.

And the child's delight belongs to the meeting of the two.

The door opens.

The child knows how to make it happen again.

Chapter III — The Language That Was Not Yet There

There is a moment in the life of a child when a sound becomes a word.

It may be difficult to identify the moment precisely. A sound that was once made in play begins to be used when a particular person enters the room. The sound is repeated. Someone responds. The child hears it again, attempts it again, and begins to recognize that it can be used to accomplish something.

The change is not simply that the child has learned to produce a sound.

The child could produce sounds before.

Nor is it simply that the child now associates a sound with an object. There are many associations in a child's life that do not yet amount to speaking.

Something more has happened.

The child has discovered that a sound can be offered to another person as a way of making something available between them.

The sound can be heard, recognized, responded to, and used again.

A new possibility has entered the child's life.

We might say that language has begun.

Yet if we look closely at what has happened, we find that the beginning depends upon a surprising abundance of things that are already available.

The child must be able to distinguish the person from other people, or at least to recognize something about the person's recurring presence. The child must be capable of expectation, of noticing a response, of encountering repetition, and of participating in a relation that extends beyond the immediate sound.

The other person must also recognize something.

They hear the sound and take it as an attempt to make contact. They respond in a way that allows the child to discover what the sound has accomplished.

Sometimes they misunderstand. Sometimes the child is merely playing with the sound. Sometimes what seemed like a word yesterday disappears for weeks.

The development is not uniform.

But amid these uncertainties, a remarkable arrangement begins to take shape.

A difference becomes recognizable.

A sound becomes repeatable.

A relation becomes established.

An attempt becomes successful.

And what has succeeded becomes available for further use.

We should notice how much intelligibility is involved before we can say that the child has successfully learned a word.

The word is not the first intelligible event in the sequence.

It is an achievement within a sequence already rich in intelligibility.

A similar observation can be made about the community on the island.

At the beginning, several survivors discover a spring near the base of a hill. They return to the settlement carrying water, and others accompany them the following morning.

The spring is difficult to find. It lies behind a group of trees, beyond a narrow passage between rocks.

A woman who knows the route attempts to explain it to a man who has not yet been there.

They do not share a language.

She points toward the hill and makes a movement with her hand. He follows her gesture with his eyes but does not understand whether she is indicating the spring, the hill, or the direction in which they must travel.

She tries again.

This time she takes a short branch and draws a rough line in the earth. She makes two marks to indicate the rocks and points between them.

He looks at the drawing.

Something changes.

He now recognizes that the passage between the rocks matters.

She adds another mark beyond the passage and imitates the movement of collecting water.

He nods.

They begin walking.

The drawing has succeeded where the gesture did not.

Why?

The answer cannot simply be that drawings are more intelligible than gestures. Another drawing might have failed. A different gesture might have worked.

Nor is the answer that the woman has transmitted a complete internal picture to the man. We have no reason to suppose that his understanding is identical in every detail to hers.

What matters is that the second attempt has made the relevant arrangement sufficiently available for their undertaking.

He now knows enough to follow the route.

The difficulty has been resolved for the purpose at hand.

And the marks in the earth have played a part in that resolution.

The woman draws the same two rocks the following day when someone else needs directions.

The man recognizes the marks.

He points to them and repeats a sound she had used while drawing.

She repeats the sound.

It begins to belong to their shared activity.

Later, another survivor uses it while discussing a different passage between rocks.

The word's use has widened.

At first it was attached to a particular place. Now it is available for a kind of place.

The speakers have begun to recognize a likeness between different circumstances.

A familiar expression is carried into a new situation because something about the new situation can be encountered in the same way.

The language is growing.

But notice what is growing with it.

The people are not merely acquiring a larger inventory of sounds.

They are discovering that something recognized in one circumstance can be recognized again in another, and that an expression established in the first can become useful in the second.

The development of language brings an enlargement of intelligible reach.

It makes what has been learned in one place available elsewhere.

And this capacity for extension is not wholly created by the expression.

For the people must be able to encounter a relevant likeness in order to find the extension useful.

A word may help them notice the likeness more readily. It may make the likeness easier to remember or communicate. It may allow a distinction that was previously uncertain to become familiar.

But its usefulness depends upon the possibility of recognizing something through it.

The language and the understanding develop together.

They are not identical.

This becomes especially clear when a word is used incorrectly.

A man who has learned the word associated with the passage between rocks begins to use it for every narrow place.

He uses it for a path between trees, a gap in a wall, and finally a crack in a cooking vessel.

Sometimes the others understand him. Sometimes they laugh. Sometimes they correct him by showing what they mean.

The word is not simply either right or wrong in every new circumstance. Its range is being established through use.

The community discovers which differences matter to its speakers.

A narrow passage may be useful to distinguish from an opening that cannot be passed through. A crack in a vessel may demand attention because it allows water to escape rather than permitting someone to enter.

An expression that was useful in one situation may prove inadequate in another.

The people discover this through the consequences of attempting to use it.

And the language acquires greater definiteness.

Something that had been uncertain becomes settled through repeated encounters.

Something that had been settled becomes open again when a new circumstance arises.

The language's developing stability is therefore not the absence of change.

It is the capacity to preserve useful distinctions while remaining available to further experience.

This matters because the language is not being constructed once, from beginning to end, according to a completed plan.

It is being lived into existence.

The people are making arrangements that become familiar enough to be used without constant negotiation. Yet those arrangements may be altered when experience gives them reason to distinguish something further.

In this respect, the developing language has a striking resemblance to the repaired door.

The first door was sufficient for its purpose until the frame swelled.

Then its adequacy was interrupted.

The people discovered the obstruction, changed the arrangement, and restored the movement.

The language, too, may be sufficient for a circumstance until another circumstance reveals what it cannot yet accomplish.

The response is not necessarily to abandon what has already been made.

Often it is to develop the existing arrangement.

A new distinction becomes available.

What was previously difficult to communicate becomes familiar.

What was previously dependent upon the immediate scene can now be expressed when the scene is absent.

A person can say that the water is beyond the rocks without standing beside them.

A child can ask for water without pointing to the spring.

Someone can warn that the passage was blocked yesterday.

Another can say that it is clear today.

An expression can reach beyond the moment in which it is spoken.

This is an immense change.

The language allows the community to make absent things available to one another.

The spring can be discussed by people who are nowhere near it.

An event from yesterday can become part of the understanding of today.

A possible event tomorrow can become a reason for acting now.

The distance between expression and immediate circumstance has increased.

Yet communication remains possible because the speakers have developed ways of making the relevant distinctions recognizable.

A spoken word does not carry the spring into the settlement.

It makes the spring available in another way.

A warning does not bring tomorrow into the present.

It makes an anticipated possibility available for present action.

A story does not restore the past.

It allows something that happened to become present to understanding.

These achievements differ.

We shall need to understand their differences.

For now, what matters is the transformation in the community's life.

The language permits people to share an intelligibility that exceeds the circumstances they can presently see, touch or demonstrate.

And this transformation grows from a life in which recognition, anticipation, relation and correction were already possible.

We might be tempted to say that the community has moved from a prelinguistic world into a linguistic world.

But that description conceals what is most important.

The people have not exchanged one world for another.

They have developed a new capacity within the same continuing life.

The trees remain where they stood.

The water still flows.

The door still opens and closes.

A promise can now be expressed in words that others recognize. A warning can reach someone who has not seen the danger. A discovery can be preserved after the discoverer has died.

Something genuinely new has become possible.

Yet its novelty is intelligible only because it enlarges capacities whose beginnings we have already encountered.

Language does not merely arrive upon the scene.

It changes what the scene can become for those who inhabit it.

And, in doing so, it reveals the intelligibility within which it has come to exist.

Chapter IV — The Claim That Outlived Its Words

A long time before the people on the island learned to speak together, other people had looked at triangles.

They had measured, compared, drawn and calculated.

They had discovered that certain relations remained reliable across differences of size and orientation.

A triangle drawn in sand could be examined. Another could be marked upon stone. A larger one might be used in construction.

The drawings differed.

The relation could be recognized again.

Eventually a claim concerning right triangles became part of the mathematical knowledge transmitted across generations.

Today we commonly associate that claim with Pythagoras.

Its historical passage through cultures is complex. The relation was known in more than one ancient mathematical tradition, and the words in which people first expressed it are not the words in which most people now encounter it.

That complexity is appropriate to our subject.

For what has survived is not a single untouched sentence travelling through time.

Languages have changed.

Mathematical symbols have been introduced.

Methods of demonstration have multiplied.

The knowledge has been taught in schools whose founders could not have imagined the languages in which future generations would study it.

Yet the relation remains available.

A child can encounter it without knowing the history of its transmission.

The child may draw a right triangle and measure its sides.

At first there may be nothing particularly remarkable about it.

The triangle has three sides.

One angle is a right angle.

Someone marks the lengths and introduces a relation among their squares.

The child follows the calculation.

Then perhaps there is a moment when the significance changes.

The relation is not merely true of the particular triangle in the drawing.

It belongs to every right triangle under the relevant geometric conditions.

The child turns the page and draws another.

The lengths change.

The relation remains.

A third drawing is made.

Again, the relation holds.

The child may initially regard this as an interesting coincidence.

Then an explanation is offered.

Perhaps squares are drawn upon the sides. Perhaps a larger square is partitioned into regions whose areas can be compared. Perhaps the proof proceeds through a different construction.

However it is accomplished, the child comes to see why the relation holds.

There is a new achievement.

Before, the child may have known that several examples worked.

Now the child understands something about what makes the relation necessary within the geometry being studied.

The difference matters.

It is possible to repeat a true statement without understanding why it is true.

It is possible to calculate a correct answer by following a procedure whose significance remains obscure.

It is possible to recognize a pattern without yet seeing what establishes it.

And it is possible for an explanation to change this situation.

The mathematical relation becomes intelligible in a new way.

Not simply encountered.

Not simply remembered.

Understood through what makes it hold.

Consider how extraordinary it is that this can happen so long after the original discovery.

The child does not require the original words.

Nor is the child's understanding limited to reconstructing the thoughts of a particular ancient mathematician.

The child can discover the relation through a proof that did not exist when the relation was first recognized.

A later expression may disclose what an earlier expression left unexplained.

An old claim may become newly intelligible.

Thus the persistence of intelligibility through language is not merely the preservation of an identical possession.

It can include the development of understanding itself.

What one generation recognizes, another may explain.

What one language expresses awkwardly, another formulation may make clearer.

What was once shown by a particular construction may later be shown through a more general relation.

The claim remains answerable to the same geometrical conditions even as the ways of understanding it develop.

And this brings us to a distinction that is easy to miss.

The enduring truth of the relation is not the same thing as the persistence of somebody's understanding of it.

A theorem may be forgotten by an entire community.

A manuscript may be lost.

A mathematical practice may disappear.

Centuries later, another community may discover the relation independently.

The truth has not travelled between the communities through an unbroken chain of words.

The discovery has occurred again.

This possibility is among the strongest invitations to reconsider the relationship between intelligibility and language.

If a relation can be discovered independently, the capacity to recognize it cannot depend upon inheriting the precise linguistic expression in which another person previously stated it.

The discovery requires activity, attention, methods and understanding.

But it does not require the survival of one original sentence.

A relation may be encountered through several histories of recognition.

The histories are different.

The relation remains recognizable.

Now consider what happens when a mathematical claim is mistranslated.

Suppose the original asserts a relation that holds for right triangles.

The translation omits the condition that the triangle must have a right angle.

The resulting sentence may be grammatical.

Its words may be familiar.

Its meaning may be perfectly clear.

But it now makes a different claim.

And the new claim does not hold in the same way.

The error can be discovered.

A reader can draw a triangle that is not right-angled and show that the translated statement is inadequate.

Nothing about the elegance of the translation can rescue it.

The expression is answerable to the relation it purports to state.

And the relation makes the difference between success and failure recognizable.

This is not a special property bestowed upon mathematics by mathematical vocabulary.

The same general phenomenon appears whenever an expression is answerable to something it seeks to make intelligible.

A witness can report that a person left the room.

Another witness can report that the person remained.

The accounts may be compared with what occurred.

A translator can turn a promise into a prediction and thereby alter the commitment conveyed.

A historian can omit the condition under which a decision was made and leave the reader with a misleading understanding of the event.

A friend can repeat another person's explanation while losing the very distinction that made it illuminating.

In each case, a sentence may be intelligible while failing to preserve the intelligible achievement at issue.

The words are not sufficient merely because they are words.

We must ask what they accomplish.

The mathematical example gives this question a particular clarity.

But the question belongs to language more generally.

And perhaps it belongs beyond language altogether.

A carpenter can measure incorrectly.

A musician can enter too early.

A person can mistake the path to a house.

A doctor can misidentify the cause of a symptom.

A child can reach for an object that has been moved.

These are different kinds of mistake, with different ways of being discovered and corrected.

Yet none requires us to suppose that error begins only when a sentence is formed.

A mistake may be made in action.

Its inadequacy can become recognizable through what happens next.

A correction can be achieved without a verbal explanation.

An explanation can then make the correction available to someone else.

Here we begin to see why the study of intelligibility must not be confined to the study of propositions.

Propositions are extraordinary achievements. They allow things to be stated, assessed, remembered and communicated with great precision.

But the field in which correctness and incorrectness, sufficiency and insufficiency, recognition and misrecognition become possible is not exhausted by the sentence.

A sentence is one way of entering that field.

It is not the whole of it.

Return to the child studying the triangle.

At one moment the child is copying symbols.

At another the child is checking a calculation.

At another the child is studying a diagram.

Then the child sees why the relation holds.

There may be no new sentence at the instant of recognition.

The proof may be the same proof that was on the page a moment earlier.

What changes is the child's understanding.

Something previously unrecognized becomes recognizable.

The child may say, "Now I see."

Those words do not constitute the whole of what has happened.

They acknowledge it.

And when the child later explains the proof to someone else, another achievement becomes possible.

One understanding becomes available for another person to acquire.

The explanation may succeed.

It may fail.

It may need to be repaired.

The relation, the proof, the child's recognition, the explanation, and the listener's eventual understanding are not one event.

But they belong together within a life of intelligibility.

And it is precisely their belonging together that makes the passage of understanding across persons and centuries possible.

There is something moving here that is not simply a succession of sentences.

There is the recurring possibility of an intelligible achievement.

A truth can be recognized.

An explanation can be given.

A failure can be discovered.

A correction can be made.

Understanding can arise again.

We should not hurry to reduce this to a single mechanism.

We should instead remain with the extraordinary fact that these achievements can answer to one another.

A proof answers a mathematical question.

A translation answers an earlier expression.

A correction answers an inadequacy.

An explanation answers a difficulty in understanding.

A learner's recognition answers the effort of explanation.

And each achievement may become the occasion for another.

This is not the permanence of language.

It is the continuing availability of intelligibility through language's changing forms.

Chapter V — When Another Language Becomes One's Own

A woman sits in a café in a city where she has lived for six months.

When she arrived, she knew enough of the local language to purchase food, ask directions and exchange the simplest greetings.

Every conversation demanded attention.

She listened carefully, searching for familiar words. Sometimes she understood an entire sentence except for the one word upon which its meaning depended.

A question could arrive faster than she could interpret it.

She smiled, asked people to repeat themselves, and occasionally answered a different question from the one they had asked.

Her days were filled with small corrections.

She learned that a phrase she had taken as a refusal was often a polite hesitation.

She learned that an expression which appeared to ask for information might instead function as an invitation.

She discovered that a particular tone could turn an apparently neutral remark into a complaint.

None of this was accomplished simply by memorizing a dictionary.

She had to recognize what people were doing with their words.

And at first, her familiar language stood between her and the new one.

She heard a sentence.

She formed an approximate translation.

She considered what the translated sentence meant.

Then she decided how to answer.

The procedure worked often enough for ordinary life.

But it was slow.

By the time she had understood a joke, the others were already laughing.

By the time she had formed a reply, the conversation had moved elsewhere.

Sometimes she understood the words individually and still failed to recognize what the speaker intended to accomplish.

The new language was present to her, but not yet fully available.

Then, one morning in the café, the person behind the counter asks whether she wants her usual order.

She answers immediately.

Only afterward does she realize that she has not translated the question.

She understood it.

The realization surprises her.

Nothing dramatic has happened. She has ordered breakfast.

Yet something in her relation to the language has changed.

The words reached her without requiring an equivalent sentence in the language she already knew.

They were no longer merely signs from which she extracted a more familiar expression.

They were directly intelligible.

We might say that she has begun to think in the new language.

But let us attend more closely to what this means.

She has not acquired an entirely new capacity for recognizing a question.

She was able to recognize questions before she arrived.

Nor has she newly discovered what breakfast is, or what it means to have a usual order, or how a person can offer something to another.

Those possibilities were already available in her life.

What has changed is that the new language has become capable of making them immediately present to her.

An expression now accomplishes something without the familiar language having to intervene.

She recognizes the question as a question.

She recognizes the offered choice.

She answers.

The movement is continuous.

And when this becomes possible across more of the language, her life within it changes.

She begins to anticipate what someone is saying.

A sentence may be unfinished, yet its likely direction is already available.

A familiar phrase carries an implication she no longer needs to explain to herself.

She recognizes when a statement is tentative and when it is decisive.

She notices when a person is gently correcting another.

She can hear that someone has raised a possibility without committing to it.

She can distinguish an answer that settles a question from one that leaves the matter open.

She begins to participate in these movements herself.

What has become fluent is not simply the production of words.

It is the participation in intelligible achievements through the new language.

This distinction matters because fluency is often measured from the outside.

Can the person speak without long pauses?

Can they use the appropriate grammatical forms?

Can they understand rapid conversation?

Can they pronounce words intelligibly?

These are important questions, and the answers tell us something substantial.

But there is also an internal transformation that the measurements do not fully describe.

The language ceases to be an obstacle continually requiring conscious passage.

It becomes a medium through which the person can move.

A child running across familiar ground does not calculate every step.

A skilled musician does not deliberate over every movement of the fingers.

A fluent speaker does not need to reconstruct the conditions of each ordinary utterance before recognizing what it accomplishes.

The capacities remain active.

But their activity has become sufficiently integrated that attention can be directed toward what is being said rather than toward the separate effort of saying or understanding it.

This does not eliminate thought.

It changes where thought is needed.

The woman in the café can now devote her attention to whether she wants to accept an invitation, rather than to working out whether an invitation has been made.

She can consider whether an explanation is convincing, rather than merely whether she has understood the words.

She can disagree.

She can make a joke.

She can change her mind in the middle of a sentence and find another way of expressing herself.

The language is no longer simply something she is learning.

It has become something through which she can live.

And there is a particularly revealing moment in this development.

She hears a sentence in the new language and understands it completely.

A friend then asks her to translate it.

She hesitates.

The meaning is clear to her, but no immediate equivalent in her first language presents itself.

She tries one expression and rejects it.

She begins another.

Finally she explains the sentence in several words rather than offering a direct translation.

What has happened?

The new language has become intelligible to her without first having to become the old language.

The difficulty now lies not in understanding the sentence, but in finding a second expression that will preserve what the first accomplished.

This reverses the situation with which she began.

At first, translation was her path to understanding.

Now understanding is what makes translation possible.

And the adequacy of the translation has become a separate question.

This reversal is important.

It reveals that knowing what an expression means and knowing how to reproduce its achievement in another language are not identical capacities.

A person may understand an expression perfectly well and still struggle to translate it.

A translation may require explanation, circumlocution, or a change in grammatical construction.

One language may make a distinction through a familiar everyday form while another requires the speaker to specify something that is ordinarily left to context.

Yet the translator can recognize the task.

They know what must be preserved, even when they have not yet found how to preserve it.

That knowledge is not a dictionary entry.

It is an understanding of the intelligible achievement for which a new expression must be found.

The woman now speaks two languages fluently.

She does not inhabit two sealed worlds.

Nor does she always find exact correspondence between their expressions.

She sometimes prefers one language for a particular thought.

An experience may come to mind with a phrase that belongs naturally to one language and feels awkward when carried into the other.

A memory may retain the sounds of the language in which it occurred.

A joke may depend upon a form that cannot be reproduced without explanation.

The differences are real.

Yet they do not prevent her from understanding both languages.

She can recognize when a distinction has been preserved.

She can recognize when it has been altered.

She can discover a new way of expressing something she previously understood only imperfectly.

She can also discover that one language has made her attentive to something she had not previously learned to notice.

Fluency is therefore not merely access to the same intelligibility through different labels.

It is a growing capacity to participate directly in the intelligible life carried by another language, including the distinctions and possibilities that language has developed through its own history.

The unity beneath languages is not impoverished by this diversity.

It is disclosed through it.

For the possibility of acquiring another language demonstrates that the person who begins outside its conventions is not thereby outside the possibility of understanding what its speakers understand.

The learner can enter.

They can discover.

They can become proficient.

And eventually they can encounter the language not as a foreign system continually requiring conversion, but as another immediate way in which things become intelligible.

Consider the final change.

One evening the woman receives a telephone call.

A friend speaks quickly and with some distress.

Something has happened.

The woman listens, asks a question, understands the answer, and responds with reassurance.

They speak for several minutes.

When the call ends, she sits quietly for a while.

Her concern is for her friend.

Only much later does she remember that the entire conversation took place in the language she once struggled to understand.

The language has done its work.

It has allowed another person's difficulty to become present to her.

It has allowed her to recognize what matters, distinguish what has happened from what may yet happen, and offer a response.

The effort of understanding has not disappeared.

But it is now directed toward her friend's circumstances rather than toward the language as an unfamiliar object.

Perhaps this is one of the most revealing marks of fluency.

The language becomes sufficiently available that something other than the language can once again occupy the centre of attention.

The person can simply listen.

And in listening, understand.

Chapter VI — The Demand That No One Issued

The next question concerns the force of the distinction itself.

We have observed that languages can develop ways of expressing distinctions that their speakers already have reason to recognize.

We have observed that a learner can acquire a second language because expressions in that language can become intelligible within an existing life.

We have observed that translation can succeed or fail, and that a truth may remain discoverable through changes in the words used to express it.

But there is something more to understand.

Why does an inadequate expression call for correction?

Why is one translation better than another?

Why does a developing language acquire more definite ways of distinguishing what its speakers need to distinguish?

We might answer that people want to communicate successfully.

That is certainly part of the matter.

But it does not yet tell us what makes an attempt successful.

Suppose a person asks whether the boat has returned.

Another answers that it will return.

The answer is understandable.

It may even be reassuring.

But the question has not been answered.

The speaker has introduced a future expectation where the listener was asking about a completed occurrence.

The difference is not merely that the listener wanted one sentence and received another.

The difference concerns what needed to become determinate.

Has the boat returned?

The listener may be waiting for someone aboard it. They may need supplies from it. They may be deciding whether to begin unloading its cargo.

The question seeks to settle whether the relevant event has occurred.

A prediction of its eventual occurrence does not settle that question.

The inadequacy can be recognized because the answer has failed to accomplish the needed determination.

The listener asks again.

This time the speaker understands and replies that the boat has not yet returned.

The question is answered.

The listener may be disappointed.

But disappointment does not make the answer inadequate.

Indeed, the answer's sufficiency may be precisely what allows the disappointment to become definite.

The listener now knows what is the case.

We should linger over this because it separates two things that are easily confused.

An intelligible achievement need not be pleasant.

A correct answer may be unwelcome.

A successful diagnosis may establish that something is seriously wrong.

The discovery of a mistake may bring embarrassment.

An accurate warning may produce fear.

Yet each can accomplish an intelligible determination that was previously unavailable.

The sufficiency of understanding is not the same as the satisfaction of every desire.

A thing can become clear without becoming good.

It can become settled without becoming easy.

And a person can understand why something has failed without thereby possessing the means to put it right.

The demand for intelligible adequacy therefore cannot simply be identified with a demand that experience turn out as we wish.

It concerns whether what is being attempted has been accomplished in the relevant way.

In the case of the boat, the listener needed to know whether it had returned.

The second answer provided that determination.

The first did not.

This difference is available whether the answer is spoken in English, French, Arabic, Mandarin, or the developing language of our island community.

The particular expressions may differ.

What the question seeks to establish remains recognizable.

And the question gives the answer its task.

This is where the idea of a mandate begins to acquire substance.

The mandate need not be imagined as a command sent from a hidden authority.

It appears within the intelligible relations of the situation itself.

A question calls for an answer.

An attempted explanation calls for sufficient understanding.

A broken mechanism calls for an appropriate repair if its function is to be restored.

A promise calls for fulfilment if it is to be kept.

A translation calls for fidelity to what is being translated.

These are not identical demands, and their differences matter.

But in each case, the nature of what is undertaken determines what would count as bringing it to an intelligible completion.

A promise cannot be kept merely by uttering the words I have kept my promise.

A bridge cannot be repaired merely by declaring it repaired.

A question cannot be answered merely by producing any sentence in response.

What counts as completion is answerable to the undertaking.

This is elementary.

And perhaps its very elementariness has prevented us from noticing how profound it is.

Our lives are composed of countless situations in which we recognize not only that something has happened but whether what needed to happen has happened.

We distinguish an attempt from its success.

We distinguish a preparation from its fulfilment.

We distinguish the appearance of resolution from a resolution that actually settles the matter.

And we can make these distinctions even when we have difficulty putting them into words.

A child tries to fit a shape into a space.

It will not go.

The child turns it.

Again, it will not go.

The shape is turned once more.

This time it fits.

The child releases it.

The task is complete.

No one needs to explain what makes the final movement different from the earlier attempts.

The difference is present in the fit itself.

The shape occupies the space as intended.

The child recognizes enough to stop trying.

This is not a linguistic judgment subsequently imposed upon an otherwise meaningless movement.

It is an intelligible achievement within the child's activity.

And the possibility of stopping matters.

If the child were unable to distinguish the successful fit from the unsuccessful attempts, the activity could not proceed in the same way.

The recognition of sufficiency is part of what allows the activity to become complete.

The same holds in situations of far greater complexity.

A scientist examines a result and realizes that an apparent anomaly has a different cause from the one originally suspected.

A musician listens to a passage and recognizes that a misplaced note has finally been corrected.

Two friends struggle to understand a misunderstanding until one says something that makes the other's action comprehensible.

A person searches for a missing document and finds it beneath a stack of papers.

The modes of completion differ.

But each has a point at which a relevant demand is answered.

We can now see something more clearly about language.

A language does not have to be told, from outside, that it must distinguish every conceivable feature of experience.

Speakers encounter circumstances in which certain differences matter to their undertakings.

Their expressions become answerable to those undertakings.

When an expression proves inadequate, the inadequacy is not arbitrary.

It arises because something has failed to become sufficiently available.

A person who meant to warn another about a falling branch discovers that the warning was taken as an instruction to approach the tree.

The failure matters because the practical direction of the utterance has been reversed.

The speaker tries another expression.

The listener moves away.

The warning succeeds.

The language has not been corrected by consulting an ideal language hidden somewhere behind it.

It has been corrected through the intelligible relation between the intended warning and the response it needed to make possible.

The pressure toward adequacy arises within the activity itself.

This is what allows an expression to be both conventional and answerable.

The sounds used by the community may be contingent upon its history.

Another community might use different sounds.

But the possibility of recognizing whether the warning has succeeded is not established merely by agreeing upon those sounds.

It involves the intended achievement and the actual response.

A shared linguistic convention provides the means.

The intelligible undertaking provides the measure of its sufficiency.

And this is why the forms of intelligibility, if we can discover them, may reveal something more fundamental than recurring linguistic structures.

They may show how achievements become answerable to the differences, relations and demands through which they acquire their significance.

They may show why certain kinds of incompletion remain recognizable even when expressions change.

They may show how repair is possible because an inadequacy is not merely a blank absence, but a determinate failure in relation to something that can be attempted again.

They may also show why completion does not always consist in adding something.

Sometimes understanding becomes complete because a distinction is finally made.

Sometimes because a possibility is opened.

Sometimes because a relation is established.

Sometimes because an inadequacy is corrected.

Sometimes because separate elements become a coherent whole.

And sometimes because a live demand comes to rest.

We should not yet assume that these descriptions exhaust the possibilities.

But they are already visible within the ordinary lives we have been following.

The door that was stuck has been repaired.

The route to the spring has been explained.

The mathematical relation has been proved.

The translation has been corrected.

The woman in the café has understood the invitation.

The child has fitted the shape into its space.

These events do not merely resemble one another.

They also differ in precisely how something becomes sufficient.

The differences invite investigation.

For if there is an order within these modes of intelligible achievement, that order may help us understand why language can emerge, change, translate, correct itself and become fluent in new speakers.

It may explain why a developing language is never simply an arbitrary collection of conventions, even though so much about its particular expressions is conventional.

And it may reveal why people who do not share a language can nevertheless recognize enough of what matters to begin building one.

The mandate, then, is perhaps best approached not as an authority standing behind intelligibility but as something disclosed wherever intelligibility becomes answerable to its own fulfilment.

A demand arises within an undertaking.

The undertaking makes a kind of completion possible.

Its success or failure becomes recognizable.

And recognition permits what follows.

This is not a command imposed upon the world.

It is part of how an intelligible life unfolds.

Let us return to the island.

A storm has passed during the night.

The people emerge from their shelters and begin examining the damage.

One roof has lost several branches. The path to the spring is covered by fallen leaves. A cooking vessel has cracked. Near the shore, a small boat has been pulled high enough above the water to remain undamaged.

Different tasks become apparent.

Someone gathers branches.

Someone clears the path.

Someone tests the vessel and discovers that it cannot hold water.

Someone else goes to inspect the boat.

The people communicate as they work.

They point, request, warn, promise, correct and explain.

Some expressions have become familiar enough to be understood immediately. Others are still uncertain.

A child hears two adults disagreeing about whether the boat moved during the storm.

One points to its present position.

The other indicates where it had been left the previous evening.

The distinction matters.

The boat is above the water now.

But did it move?

A third person comes over and notices a furrow in the sand.

The mark begins near the place where the boat had stood and ends beneath its present position.

The adults examine it.

One gestures along the furrow.

The other nods.

Something has been established.

The boat moved during the storm.

It is now safely above the water.

The community will need ways of saying both.

The child watches the adults, then looks back at the mark in the sand.

Later, while playing beside the shelter, the child drags a piece of wood across the ground.

It leaves a line behind it.

The child stops and looks at the line.

Then the child moves the wood again.

A second line appears.

Nobody has asked for an explanation.

Nobody has issued an instruction.

Yet something that occurred on the shore has become available again, in a different place, through a child's activity.

The mark remains after the movement ends.

The child notices.

And in that recognition, the same elementary difference with which we began has appeared once more.

Something happened.

Something is now the case.

The language of the island is still developing.

The child has not yet learned all its words.

But the difference is already there to be understood.

And the child is beginning to make something of it.

Chapter VII — The Same Answer, a Different Question

The morning after the storm, a woman arrives at the shelter and finds the door closed. She asks whether anyone saw it close during the night. Someone answers that it is closed now.

The answer is true. The door is closed. She can see that herself. But the matter she has raised remains unanswered.

She wants to know whether the wind swung it shut, whether someone shut it deliberately, or whether it remained closed throughout the storm. The condition now visible cannot decide which of these occurred. One event may leave a result that several different histories could have produced. The woman does not need a theory of tense or a philosophy of evidence to notice the inadequacy. She asked about an occurrence and received a report of a condition.

A child nearby hears the exchange and looks at the door. The child has learned an expression the adults use when something is finished. He repeats it. One of the adults smiles, then shows him the wet track beneath the frame, the shallow streak where leaves were dragged against the wood by the wind. Another person remembers hearing a sound before dawn. The people begin to fit together what they can establish. They do not all remember the same things. A woman who slept lightly recalls getting up to check the fire. Someone else knows that the door had been left open before everyone settled for the night.

The conversation becomes a small inquiry. No one has to announce that it is an inquiry for its demands to become recognizable. A statement that would have been sufficient for one question does not settle another. A trace on the ground may bear upon what happened without establishing it alone. A remembered sound may be consistent with more than one cause. The people proceed because they can recognize the difference between a possibility and what has been established.

Eventually the person who checked the fire remembers fastening the door before lying down again. The question changes. If the door was closed before the wind grew strong, its present condition is no longer evidence of the movement the woman originally supposed. The woman accepts the account. Nothing has happened to the door at that moment. What has changed is the standing of one possible history within the inquiry.

This is an ordinary kind of completion, and one that language makes unusually visible. A question arises and begins to govern what can count as an adequate answer. Several statements are offered. Some are true but beside the point; others are relevant but insufficient. One eventually makes the history determinate enough for those present to continue their work.

The people do not need every possible fact about the storm. They need an answer to this question. The shelter may still need repairing. The wind may have damaged the path to the spring. Someone may still be concerned about the boat. None of these unsettled matters prevents the particular inquiry from reaching enough.

A philosopher can make the mistake of demanding that a completion be final in every respect before permitting it to be called complete. Ordinary life does not proceed that way. A question can be answered while others remain open; a repair can succeed while the object remains capable of future damage; a route can become usable while the country beyond it remains unexplored. The finitude of a completion is not an embarrassment to it. It is part of its character.

In the island community, this finitude is especially evident because language remains in development. One expression is repeated during the morning inquiry and begins to acquire a use in reports of things that happened earlier. It is not a finished grammatical system. It has not suddenly made every history easy to communicate. But it allows a distinction to be held steadily enough that speakers can return to it in a different circumstance.

Later, a child asks whether the fire went out. An adult uses the expression and gestures toward the extinguished ashes. The child looks again, and the adult discovers that the child is asking something slightly different: not whether the fire has ended, but whether it went out by itself or was put out by someone. The adult offers another expression. The child follows this one.

What has developed here is not an abstract obedience to a predetermined language. The speakers have encountered separate demands and found separate ways of making them sufficiently available. The growing language now lets them preserve distinctions across circumstances that the circumstances themselves no longer display.

And the history of that growth will remain part of the language. Children born on the island may learn the new expressions without knowing the negotiations through which they arose. Their parents may remember the difficulty of communicating an event that had left only a condition behind. The children may simply tell one another what happened yesterday.

For them the expression will be immediate. For their parents it may still carry the memory of a discovery.

The intelligible difference has not changed because it has become easier to say. The life in which it can be shared has changed profoundly.

A woman who once had to gesture at the door and reconstruct the night in fragments now says what she needs to say. Another person understands. They may still disagree about whether her report is correct, but they no longer need to negotiate how to distinguish the occurrence from the resulting condition each time the question arises.

The language has made a form of understanding more freely available. It has not delivered the community from the possibility of error. It has given them better means to recognize, pursue and sometimes correct it.

There is an intimacy between this development and the smallest act of teaching. A child reaches for a word and finds it. Another child hears the word and discovers what can be done with it. What was once a problem of communication becomes part of the ordinary environment in which new problems can be encountered.

The expression has become familiar enough that it can stop calling attention to itself.

This is one of the quiet triumphs of language: it can be newly made, learned, and then forgotten as an object of effort, while continuing to bring intelligible distinctions within reach.

Chapter VIII — Three Ways Through

The path to the spring has become difficult to use after the storm. Branches lie across it, the ground is soft, and a stone loosened from the hillside has fallen where the track narrows. Two people go to inspect the damage. A third follows with a basket, hoping to bring water back to the settlement.

At the narrow place, the stone blocks the way. It is too large to step around without slipping down the bank. One person tests it with a branch and discovers that it can be moved. Together they push. The stone shifts, turns, and comes to rest below the path. The narrow place is open.

The people feel the familiar satisfaction of an obstacle overcome. One of them steps beyond the place where the stone lay. But a little farther on, the path disappears beneath a fallen tree. Beyond the tree the soil has washed away. They have made one passage possible, yet the route to the spring remains unusable.

There is no contradiction. The removal of the stone succeeded in the respect in which it was attempted. The larger undertaking is not complete. The way to the spring requires a connected passage, not merely the clearing of one decisive point. An achievement can be sufficient for a local difficulty and insufficient for a wider demand into which it enters.

They return to the fallen tree. One person pulls loose branches away. Another lays flat stones across the muddy ground. The work proceeds unevenly. At one point the route looks clear until the basket carrier tries to cross with its weight. The newly laid stones shift. The arrangement that allowed someone to walk through unburdened does not yet serve the purpose for which the route is needed.

So they try again. They find broader stones and set them deeper into the soil. The basket carrier passes with an empty basket, then returns with a container filled at the spring. The stones hold. The path is no longer only visible as a possible line; it can carry the undertaking for which it was cleared.

We can recognize the difference between the first success and the second without bringing a classification to the scene. The stone had occupied one decisive place. Removing it released that point of passage. The route, by contrast, had to become adequate throughout its relevant extent. A clearing here and a firm stone there were insufficient until they belonged to a continuous way through.

The people might have reached the spring by a third means.

Before they began moving the fallen tree, someone might have noticed a broad, dry track running behind the hill. If that track had led safely to the water, they could have abandoned the damaged route. The fallen stone and tree might have remained exactly where the storm left them. The old path would still be blocked. Yet its obstruction would no longer govern the question of obtaining water.

This possibility reveals another kind of change. A condition can cease to determine an undertaking without being physically removed. A route can be released from its necessity. An old plan may remain written down after it ceases to guide the work. An objection may remain true of one proposal after the proposal has been abandoned. Nothing need vanish for its former authority to end.

The third possibility is not simply the first two performed more efficiently. To move the stone is to alter a decisive obstruction. To make the damaged track usable is to achieve adequacy across a connected field. To discover that another way suffices is to change what governs the undertaking. The blocked path remains blocked, but the people need no longer make its repair the condition of their success.

One afternoon can contain all three movements. The people first move the stone, then improve the muddy route, and eventually discover that a different entrance to the spring is better when carrying large loads. What was once necessary becomes unnecessary; what was locally accomplished remains accomplished; what was sufficient for one use may need to be reconsidered for another.

The work has a history, but its history is not one undivided achievement. We can ask which difficulty was answered at each point and how that answer remained available to what followed.

The difference is especially important when an earlier failure loses its force. A person may say that the original path has been solved, although nothing has been done to restore it. The expression may convey that the water problem has been settled, but it risks concealing the way in which that happened. The people did not repair the old path. They found that they could do without it.

Such differences are not ornamental refinements of speech. They belong to the intelligibility of what occurred. We can recognize them before the community possesses fixed words to express them. We can also lose them through careless description after those words become available.

Toward evening the basket carrier returns by the new track. At its entrance, a child is trying to move the large stone that had obstructed the old path. He wants to make the place clear again. The adult watches for a moment, then shows him the other route. The child looks from one path to the other, uncertain whether the stone should still be moved.

The adult does not forbid the work. The stone may be moved another day. The old path may be worth restoring for a different purpose. But for tonight the water has been carried, and the people have what they need.

The child follows the adult up the dry track. In the morning he may return to the stone. There is no need to decide that every obstruction must remain permanently irrelevant simply because another way was found once.

The release was real, and it belonged to a particular undertaking.

The basket reaches the shelter. Someone takes the water and sets it near the fire. The people begin preparing food. The route is no longer their subject of attention. Its sufficiency has become part of the conditions of an ordinary evening.

That may be one of the most revealing signs of an intelligible completion. The matter ceases to demand its own resolution, and what it has made available can be taken up in another life of attention.

Chapter IX — The Things That Remain

In the morning the woman who carries the water asks which of two baskets was repaired the previous afternoon. They are hanging from the same beam, woven from the same reeds, and nearly identical in size. A child points to the one on the left. The woman takes it down and finds the new binding around its handle.

She could have distinguished it by that binding before the child spoke. The child's gesture made the distinction immediately available to her, and her inspection confirmed it. The basket has not changed because she now knows which one it is. Something has become determinate in her understanding, and the determination can remain available when she turns away from the beam.

She goes to the spring, brings water back, and hangs the basket where it was before. Later someone asks which basket has the strengthened handle. She answers without needing to examine both again. The earlier recognition has become something she can carry forward.

In the shelter next door, a different kind of continuation is under way. A family has agreed that whoever prepares the evening food will leave a covered portion for the person watching the boat. The arrangement was made the previous night, and the conversation in which it was settled has long since ended. Yet when the food is ready, the cook sets a portion aside. She does not reopen the question of whether the watcher's meal belongs within the household's work. The agreement still bears upon her present action.

The arrangement has not remained as an uninterrupted conversation. Nor is its force exhausted by someone remembering the words that were spoken. The people have established a relation that continues to make some actions appropriate, some omissions recognizable, and some requests intelligible. It is possible to forget the agreement, to dispute what its terms were, or to break it. None of these possibilities means that the original settlement was merely a sequence of vanished sounds.

On the hillside, the repaired path has a third kind of afterlife. At first light three people carry water along it. They do not need to pause at every place where stones were removed or the ground was levelled. The work has become effective as an available route. What had required attention yesterday now allows other activity to proceed without making itself the centre of attention.

One achievement leaves a distinction that can stand. Another establishes a relation that continues to bear upon later conduct. Another leaves a capacity available for use. We need not convert these observations into a list before recognizing why they differ. The repaired path is not merely something people know about; it carries them. The agreement is not merely a fact about yesterday's words; it gives shape to today's obligations. The identified basket has not acquired a new function simply because its identity has become clear to someone.

Of course these descriptions can overlap. The path can be identified. The agreement can enable a practical action. The basket's strengthened handle allows it to bear a load. A person may recognize several aspects of the same living situation. What matters is to ask what particular achievement is under examination, what was sufficient for it, and how the result remains available in the continuation that concerns us.

A child watches the people taking water along the path and says that the path is finished. One of the adults replies that it was finished yesterday. The child looks at the walkers and asks why they are still using it if the work is finished. The adult takes a moment to understand the question.

Finishing the path did not finish the journeys that could be made along it. Indeed, it made them possible. The completion of one undertaking became a condition of others. The path's new usefulness is not a contradiction of its completion; it is part of what the completion has brought into the life of the settlement.

This is easy to overlook because we naturally speak as though an ending must mean that nothing more follows. But we know from ordinary activity that the end of a task often marks the beginning of its usefulness. A cup is fired so that it can be used. A letter is written so that it can be read. A bridge is completed so that people may cross. An explanation becomes sufficient so that its listener may understand something further without needing to begin the explanation again.

Nor does every completion leave an operative capacity. A difference may simply remain known. A false suspicion may lose its hold. A question may come to rest with no further action demanded. A friend may finally understand why another friend went away, and remain quietly with that understanding. To insist upon a practical consequence in every case would be to change the question and then mistake the new question for the old one.

The island language is now sufficiently developed for these different continuations to be expressed in ordinary conversation. Someone can say which basket it was, who has undertaken the evening task, and whether the path can be used. Another language might distribute the same work among different verbal forms, customary implications or arrangements of context. We do not need the expressions to resemble one another to recognize the different things they can accomplish.

An old man, visiting the spring by the path for the first time since its repair, pauses where a large stone used to block the way. He remembers struggling to move it before the storm. It lies now beside the track. For him, its new position explains a particular change. Farther along, he notices that the ground has been raised and packed into a continuous surface. Still farther, he sees the dry alternative way around the hill, which some people use when carrying larger loads.

He can recognize three different ways the journey became possible, and he can recognize that what each left available is not simply the same. The moved stone no longer obstructs that point. The prepared track supports travel across its length. The alternative route frees the settlement from dependence upon the old one. Their achievements persist within the same afternoon's walking, but their modes of sufficiency and their continuing significance are not interchangeable.

The old man reaches the spring and fills his vessel. At the water's edge a young woman asks him which way he came. He points toward the hillside. She looks where he is pointing and then asks whether that way is safe after rain.

He pauses again. Yesterday's sufficiency is not automatically an answer to every question tomorrow may raise. A route that carries dry loads today may be impassable in a storm. The completion was real. Its continuing availability can be tested in a new circumstance.

He tells her what he knows. He does not pretend to know what he has not seen. The difference between having completed a work and having established its adequacy under every future condition is allowed to remain a difference.

She thanks him and chooses the higher track. Her choice does not undo the lower path's repair. It settles a new question, within a life already made more spacious by the work of previous days.

Chapter X — The Question That Arrived Afterward

The child who watched the basket being identified has begun to use a word that the adults employ when something is finished. She says it when a knot has been tied, when a bowl is emptied, when she has reached the last stone in a line she is counting. Sometimes the word is appropriate. Sometimes the adults smile and tell her that the work is not yet done.

One afternoon she brings a small wooden frame to the carpenter and says the word. The frame stands upright. Four pieces have been fitted together, and their corners meet. The carpenter examines it and asks what she means to use it for. She wants it to hold a cloth over a sleeping place.

He touches the upper rail. It gives way under slight pressure. The frame has been assembled, but it is not yet strong enough for the undertaking she has in mind.

The child looks at the fitted corners. All the pieces are there. She points to them one by one. The carpenter nods. He does not deny that she has accomplished the assembly. He shows her that a different question remains: will it stand under the weight it must carry?

The child is disappointed. She thought she had finished. Yet her disappointment is not the discovery that nothing was achieved. The pieces were brought together correctly. That achievement remains. What is unfinished belongs to the frame's ability to serve its intended use.

They reinforce one corner and test it again. This time another corner begins to shift. They work around the frame. A single firm joint cannot establish the sufficiency of the whole structure. The relevant strength must be distributed across the parts whose relation gives the frame its function.

Eventually it stands. A piece of cloth is laid over it. The cloth hangs too low at one end. They adjust the supports, step back, and look again. Now it can shelter the sleeping place in the way the child intended.

The child uses her word for finished once more. This time the carpenter agrees.

But later, in the evening, someone notices that rain has entered through the side of the shelter and dampened one corner of the cloth. The frame may be strong and correctly assembled; the place in which it is being used introduces another demand. They move it away from the opening.

Was the carpenter wrong to call the frame finished earlier? Not simply. The frame was adequate for the question he had asked and the test they performed. The later difficulty concerned its place within a shelter exposed to rain. To treat every new demand as proof that nothing was ever complete would make completion impossible to recognize. To treat an earlier completion as a guarantee against every new circumstance would make it impossible to learn.

We live between these errors. An achieved sufficiency must be allowed to be real, and its scope must be allowed to remain determinate.

The child's growing language is involved in this discipline. Her word for finished does not become useful merely because adults repeat it. She is learning when it can be said with justice, and what remains unsettled when it cannot. Sometimes the same word marks very different achievements. Sometimes another expression is needed to distinguish what has been assembled from what is ready to use, what has been attempted from what has succeeded, what has ended from what can now begin.

A language learns such differences through the lives of its speakers. An individual child acquires the differences through repeated participation in those lives. The developing community and the developing speaker answer to intelligible undertakings that neither has entirely invented. The community must discover what it needs to communicate; the child must discover what its expressions accomplish. In both cases the learning is richer than the accumulation of names.

That evening the carpenter sits beside the doorway and watches the girl explain the frame to another child. She points to the corner that was first strengthened and then to the place where it had to be moved to escape the rain. Her explanation is halting. The listener misunderstands the first part, thinking that the cloth had been too heavy. The girl shakes her head, presses a hand against the joint, and makes it move slightly. Then she holds it firm.

The other child understands.

The words have been few. The gesture has been enough. Yet this little exchange contains a difference between an actual failure and a merely supposed one, between correction at one point and adequacy across a structure, between an object's completed assembly and its suitability to a place. The children do not need to announce these differences as a philosophical inventory. They are learning to recognize them as part of the continuing life of their island.

The carpenter hears them laugh and returns his attention to the door. The hinge has begun to squeak. He may oil it in the morning. There is no need to attend to every unfinished thing tonight simply because he can recognize it as unfinished.

The girl carries the frame into the sleeping place. It stands where they moved it, holding the cloth above the bed. The assembly is over. The sheltering has begun.

And what has been made sufficient continues to make other things possible.

XI. A Shelter, Three Answers

The first rain of the new season arrives late in the afternoon. It begins with large drops that fall separately enough to be counted against the leaves. Then the distance between the drops disappears, and the island settles beneath a continuous sound.

Inside the shelter, the girl who built the wooden frame watches the cloth above her sleeping place darken at one edge. She moves closer. Water is gathering along a fold and finding its way toward the bedding.

The frame itself has not collapsed. The joints are still firm, and the cloth is still held above the floor. The construction that was completed yesterday has not ceased to be a completed construction merely because rain has introduced another difficulty. Yet the sheltering she hoped it would provide is now inadequate.

She calls for the carpenter. He does not examine the frame's strongest corner. He follows the damp edge of the cloth toward the opening in the wall. There he discovers that the rain has been blown sideways through a gap above the window.

The girl had imagined that the frame needed another support. The carpenter shows her the water's path. They place a strip of material along the opening, and the dripping stops.

They wait.

This waiting is part of the work. A strip of material may look well placed while allowing water to pass behind it. The first disappearance of a drop does not establish that the gap has been sealed. They watch the edge of the cloth until it begins to dry.

The girl says that the problem is finished. The carpenter agrees about the dripping. Then he asks whether the bedding beneath has remained dry.

It has not. They move it nearer the hearth.

Nothing has been withdrawn from the repair of the window. The leak has been stopped. The bedding remains damp. Two matters were involved, and one success did not complete the other.

Later, when the rain has weakened, the girl brings the carpenter back to her frame. She presses the corner that gave way yesterday. It holds. She lifts the cloth and shows him the dry patch beneath the repaired opening. She seems to be asking whether she may now use her word for finished without being corrected.

He asks her what she means.

She points first to the joint, then to the wall, then to the bedding. She has not yet found different expressions for all three achievements. She makes them distinct by pointing.

The carpenter understands. The frame has been made firm. The leak has been stopped. The bedding is being dried. Each achievement answers to a different undertaking. They can be acknowledged separately, without requiring the girl to pretend that the whole afternoon has reached an end.

The next morning they examine the door of the storehouse. Its edge has begun to catch against the frame. The carpenter adjusts the hinge, and the door opens freely when pushed from outside. He demonstrates the movement.

A man inside asks whether the latch can be operated without help. The carpenter closes the door and tries to open it from within. His hand cannot reach the release.

The door can open. The man cannot yet open it from where he must stand.

The first demonstration was accurate. It showed a movement. The second question introduced a condition of use that the first demonstration had not established. They alter the latch and try again.

This time the man closes and opens the door himself. He does it twice, then lets another person try. There is no need to repeat the movement endlessly. They have made the relevant capacity available and tested it under the conditions for which it was required.

But a third question is still possible. Will the swollen door work after another heavy rain? The carpenter cannot answer from this single dry morning. He has allowed extra clearance and expects it to be enough. The next storm will offer a further test.

He does not therefore refuse to call the present work complete. The latch functions, the door moves, and the intended person can operate it. Those achievements are real. Their scope is not the same as a guarantee about every future day.

As they leave the storehouse, the girl stays behind to examine the latch. She has watched it being moved from outside and inside. She can now use it herself. The completion of the adjustment persists not only as a statement that the door was repaired, but as a capacity she can exercise.

The language being developed on the island must learn to carry these differences as the need arises. It must allow people to say what has happened, what is now the case, what remains usable, and what has yet to be established. It need not mark all these differences in the same way. It need not assign each one a separate word. But the speakers will have occasions when confusing them matters, and their efforts to speak together will have consequences for what becomes expressible.

The girl leaves the door unlatched while she carries a basket outside. When she returns, she closes it. The carpenter sees her test the handle and then walk away.

Nothing in her movement announces a theory. She wanted to know that she could open the door when she needed it. Now she knows, and the knowledge has become ordinary enough to use without remark.

XII. The Word That Finds Its Limit

A few weeks later, a group of survivors travels inland to collect fruit from a stand of trees beyond the ridge. They have made the journey before. The route is familiar to several of them, though a younger man is going for the first time.

Near the ridge, they reach a fork. One path climbs through a narrow gap in the rock. The other descends into denser vegetation. Both were once passable, and the community has begun using a familiar expression for each.

The younger man repeats the word for the climbing path and points uphill. The others nod. They have understood his intention.

But partway through the gap they find that a fallen slab has narrowed the passage. An adult can pass sideways. A basket carried across the shoulders cannot.

One of the travellers says that the path is open. Another answers that it is blocked.

They are not necessarily disagreeing about what is before them. The first has just passed between the rocks. The second is carrying the wide basket that must be brought through. Each has a use of the path in view, and the difference has become consequential.

The travellers set down their loads.

One lifts the basket and measures it against the opening. Another proposes passing the fruit through separately. A third points to the descending path, which takes longer but is wide enough for the baskets.

Their language can already express openness and blockage in many familiar situations. Here the apparent simplicity of those expressions has been interrupted by a more exact demand. Open for whom? Open for what? Open under which conditions?

They do not need to conduct a formal discussion about the meaning of their words. They have to carry the fruit home. They turn the baskets and try the gap. When the contents begin to spill, they stop.

The younger man points to the narrow passage and makes a gesture indicating a person moving through it. Then he holds both arms wide and moves them toward the same opening. The second gesture ends against the rock.

The others understand.

The path is passable for the person and not for the load as it is being carried.

A difference that the word alone had not made sufficiently precise becomes available through the demonstration. In later journeys, the speakers may develop a customary way to express the distinction without repeating the whole performance. They may already have expressions in their different first languages that make the relevant qualification easy. Some of these may enter their developing common speech.

What matters here is that the living situation has given their attempts at expression something to answer to.

The younger man had not been wrong to regard the path as open in every possible respect. He had encountered one way in which it was open. The other traveller had encountered a way in which it failed their task. The group needed a shared determination adequate to the journey they were actually making.

Eventually they choose the lower path. It is longer, and it carries them through mud, but the baskets remain intact.

The obstruction in the upper passage has not been removed. No one claims to have repaired it. Nevertheless, it no longer governs the journey home. The group has found a way through the difficulty by leaving that requirement behind.

When they reach the settlement, someone asks whether the upper passage has been cleared.

The younger man answers no.

Someone else asks whether the fruit has arrived.

Yes, he says, and places a basket on the ground.

Both replies are true. They settle different questions.

The first preserves an unfinished condition that might matter on another day. The second acknowledges an achieved arrival. To treat the successful journey as proof that the upper passage is clear would be a mistake. To treat the blocked passage as proof that the journey could not be completed would be another.

At the evening meal, the travellers recount what happened. A child who has never seen the ridge asks why they did not simply carry the fruit through the narrow opening one piece at a time.

The question is sensible. The travellers had considered that possibility, but darkness was approaching and the fruit would have taken too long to transfer.

The child has supplied another imaginable completion. The actual journey took a different path.

An intelligible possibility can remain open in thought even after a practical decision has been settled. The child has not undone the journey by imagining an alternative. Nor does the fact that the group arrived make the alternative unintelligible.

One of the adults draws the two paths in the earth with a stick. The shorter route has a narrow place marked across it. The longer route curves beneath the ridge and joins the road near the trees.

The child traces the shorter line with a finger. Then she traces the longer one.

The drawing makes both routes available in the same view. It permits the child to compare their lengths and understand why a shorter route might fail to be the more useful one. It also gives the travellers a way to show the difference between a blockage that remained and a demand for passage that had been met otherwise.

When the conversation ends, the drawing is left beside the fire. Before morning, footsteps and wind will scatter it. Its disappearance will not undo the route the people travelled or the understanding that was reached at the meal.

The child may remember the two paths. The younger man may use the new distinction during another journey. Someone may eventually make the distinction customary in the community's speech.

The expression has not merely been stored. It has entered a developing practice in which later situations can call upon it.

The next day a woman carrying a large vessel approaches the storehouse door. She stops and looks at its width. The door opens easily, but the vessel will not pass upright.

She turns it sideways.

Now there is room.

No one thinks to announce that the door has changed. The door's opening is the same. The relevant relation between the opening and what must pass through it has become different.

The woman carries the vessel inside. She sets it down and closes the door behind her.

What had been a difficulty becomes an ordinary movement. The solution is not merely remembered. It works.

XIII. The Marks the Rain Did Not Keep

By morning the drawing beside the fire has disappeared. Rain has washed one of its lines into the earth, and footsteps have blurred the other. The woman who drew the routes does not look for it. She is preparing to go back toward the ridge, this time to collect leaves that are useful for weaving.

A child asks whether she will take the shorter path.

The woman says no. The fallen slab still narrows the gap, and the baskets will be wide again.

The child looks toward the ground where the drawing used to be. Nothing remains that would allow a stranger to reconstruct the two lines. Yet the woman knows which route will serve the journey. Her knowledge is not the drawing. The drawing made something recognizable that can now be carried into another undertaking.

The child asks for another drawing.

The woman marks two lines on a piece of bark. The shorter one passes between a pair of stones. The longer one follows the slope and curves around them. This time the child draws a basket at the end of each line.

The woman studies the bark. One basket is wide, the other narrow. The child has noticed something that yesterday's account left largely implicit: what can pass through a gap depends upon the thing that must pass. The picture is not merely a copy of the vanished lines. It makes another difference available.

They take the piece of bark to the younger man who had made the journey. He recognizes the route, then points to the narrower basket and indicates that it could probably pass through the rocky gap. The child nods. The route has not changed since yesterday. What is now being considered is a different possible use of it.

The drawing does not answer the practical question completely. They have not measured the basket against the passage. The narrow basket may still catch, or it may pass easily. But a possibility has been made definite enough to investigate. The child's picture has opened a question whose answer will require an encounter with the actual gap.

The woman places the bark beside the doorway and leaves. The child does not accompany her. Instead, she carries a smaller basket to the storehouse and tests it against the door. It passes without turning.

She then tries the larger basket. It must be tilted.

She has begun to find the difference between recognizing that a thing can pass and recognizing what must be done to make it pass. The door is not an obstacle in both cases in the same way. With the small basket, nothing further is required. With the large one, a movement must be discovered.

Later she tells another child how to tilt the large basket. The second child attempts the movement, but its handle catches against the frame. The first child takes the basket back, turns it with the handle facing downward, and tries again. It passes.

The second child repeats the movement and succeeds.

It would be easy to say that a piece of knowledge has been transmitted. That is true enough for ordinary speech. Yet the different achievements are worth remaining close to. The first child discovered a workable orientation. She then attempted to communicate it. The second child recognized enough to reproduce the movement. The practical capacity is now available to both of them.

The door has undergone no alteration.

The basket has not become smaller.

The children have learned how the relation between the two can be made to work.

When the woman returns that afternoon, the children show her what they have learned. She praises them and asks whether the same movement will allow the large basket through the narrow passage in the ridge.

They do not know.

The child who found the movement considers the question. The passage between rocks may be taller than the door, but the rocks do not have a smooth frame. The basket might catch at another place. The useful movement at the storehouse has not automatically become a complete solution at the ridge.

Nothing here diminishes what the children have accomplished. It reveals the limits of its present reach.

The next day they accompany the adults to the passage. The small basket fits. The large one does not pass, even when tilted. Its handle catches between two uneven stones.

The children try again, then set the basket down. One adult unfastens the handle and carries the basket through separately. On the other side, the handle is attached again.

The path can be used for the larger basket, but only through a more complicated activity. The obstacle has not disappeared. The load has been temporarily altered, then restored.

When they return to the settlement, the child draws three pictures on fresh bark: a basket passing through a door, the same basket failing between rocks, and the basket carried through after its handle has been removed.

The pictures are rough, but the sequence can be followed. They do not merely name the objects. They make the course of an undertaking intelligible: an earlier success, a new failure, and a different way of meeting the demand.

Another survivor looks at the pictures and asks whether the rocky passage has been cleared.

No, the child answers.

Can they carry the basket through?

Yes, she says, but the handle must come off.

The difference is now easy to express in the community's developing speech. One sentence acknowledges an enduring obstruction. The other makes an effective procedure available.

The next time someone brings a basket to the ridge, the children need not go with them. They can tell the person what to do. The marks on the bark can help, but the person may understand from the words alone. What was learned by handling a particular basket has entered the common possibilities of the group.

The drawings are later used as kindling. The woman hesitates before putting them into the fire, then finds that they are dry and will burn well.

Nothing about the journey requires the bark to be kept forever. The knowledge is not guaranteed by its disappearance, nor would the bark alone have guaranteed that every future basket could be carried through. But the procedure has already been repeated and understood. It belongs to the community's available ways of meeting a familiar difficulty.

The child watches the fire take hold. She remembers where the handle caught between the rocks.

Tomorrow she may have forgotten the shape of her own drawing. She will still know what she was trying to show.

XIV. The Word That Can Be Wrong Before It Exists

A boy brings a cooking vessel to the common hearth. Its bottom has cracked. He has poured water into it twice, and each time a little has escaped into the dust.

An older woman asks whether the vessel is empty.

The boy looks inside. There is still water at the bottom. He shakes his head.

The woman asks another question, using a word that several of the islanders have begun using for things that no longer perform the work for which they are needed.

The boy has heard the word applied to a fishing net with a torn mesh and to a roof whose covering had been lifted by wind. He knows the vessel cannot hold water reliably, and he repeats the word.

The woman understands him.

But a younger child standing nearby points to the crack and uses the same word for the narrow opening in the clay. The older woman pauses. The word has been taken to name both the defect and the failure that the defect makes possible.

Sometimes those uses will be close enough for the speakers' purposes. At other times the difference will matter. A vessel may have a hairline crack without leaking. A vessel may leak because its lid is badly fitted, though the body is sound. A net can fail because it is torn, or because it has been set in the wrong place.

The woman does not lecture the children about categories. She fills the vessel with a little water and lets them watch the line of moisture spreading beneath it. Then she points to the crack and draws a narrow line in the dust.

The younger child follows the line with her finger.

The boy points again to the water escaping and repeats the word.

The woman uses another expression that the community has recently begun to share. Its familiar use concerns a thing being unable to accomplish what it is intended to do. She repeats the two expressions in sequence, indicating first the crack and then the loss of water.

The children attempt the distinction.

Their words are still uncertain. The expressions may shift in use during the months that follow. Different speakers may continue to favour the same word for several related circumstances.

Yet the practical difference is there to be encountered. A crack is not the same thing as water escaping through it. The visible defect and the failure of the vessel to hold water stand in a relation, but they are not identical.

The children can now be mistaken in a more definite way. The younger one may point to a harmless mark in an unbroken pot and say that it is leaking. The older woman can test the pot and show that it holds water. The mark is there; the claimed failure is not.

There is a moment in this learning that deserves to be held in view. Before the community possesses an entirely settled expression for the difference, the speakers can already recognize that some attempted uses do not fit what has happened.

They do not have to know the final convention in advance. Their correction need not consist in retrieving a perfect rule from memory. They can return to the vessel, look again, try again, and find an expression that makes the relevant circumstance more precisely available.

The language grows through this work.

Later, the boy visits a family whose shelter has a small opening beneath its roof. When the wind blows from the east, rain comes through. He uses the familiar word for a thing's failure, then gestures toward the opening. The family understands him. Someone has already intended to seal the gap, but the repair has not yet been done.

The boy offers to help.

A strip of cloth is pressed into the opening. They pour water over the roof and wait. No drops appear inside. The repair has succeeded for this test.

The boy repeats the word for failure, then shakes his head.

The family laughs. They have understood that what the word once described no longer governs the situation. He is not denying that the opening was there. He is recognizing that the roof no longer allows water through it in the way that mattered.

The next day, the cloth is still in place. The family has stopped setting a bowl beneath the gap.

The bowl itself remains useful. It can hold fruit, gather seeds, or be placed beneath another leak if one appears. But it is no longer required as protection from this opening. A former necessity has ceased to direct the arrangement of the room.

The boy moves the bowl to a shelf. In doing so he makes space for a small stool.

When he sits there, someone asks him what he changed.

He points to the repaired gap and then to the empty place on the floor. The answer he gives is still awkward. He does not yet have a single expression that carries the whole difference from the old arrangement to the new.

But he understands why the bowl could be moved.

The older woman, visiting that evening, helps him say it. She begins with the fact that water no longer enters. He adds that they no longer need the bowl. Together they find a way to make the relation intelligible to someone who was absent when the repair was made.

The boy listens to their words. One expression is now enough to describe the condition of the roof. Another makes clear that an earlier requirement has been released. A third might tell the story of how the cloth was fitted.

These statements are related, but none is simply a substitute for the others.

Outside, rain begins again.

The boy looks toward the roof. He waits through the first heavy drops, then goes to the corner where the bowl used to stand.

The floor is dry.

He does not say anything. He sits back on the stool.

What has become intelligible to him is not less real because he leaves it unspoken. The language has helped him share and retain the achievement, but the dry floor is already answering the question he came to ask.
