# SOCRATIA

*A Socratic journey into the heart of intelligibility*

---

# Book I — Before the Word

## Chapter I — The Door

A door closes.

What has happened?

The door has moved from being open to being closed.

And afterward?

It is closed.

Are these the same thing?

No. One concerns what happened. The other concerns what is now the case.

Must anybody speak before this difference can be encountered?

No.

And must we possess words for it before we can recognize it?

No.

Then let us begin with that.

A door closes. Something happens. The movement ends. A condition remains.

A child may watch the movement without having learned to describe it. A person arriving afterward may discover the condition without having witnessed the movement. Another person may hear a description and understand what occurred without having been present.

These are different encounters.

And the differences matter.

Suppose I tell you that I saw the door close, when all I actually know is that I found it closed.

Have I said something I cannot establish?

Yes.

Could my sentence nevertheless be perfectly grammatical?

Yes.

What, then, is wrong with it?

It makes an occurrence intelligible that my experience did not establish.

We have discovered something elementary.

There is a difference between an occurrence, a condition, and what an expression makes intelligible about either.

And that difference can determine whether an expression is adequate.

We have not needed a theory to recognize this.

We have merely attended to something ordinary.

Now let us consider something else.

## Chapter II — Before a Common Language

Imagine that you and I meet beside a path.

We have no language in common.

Ahead of us, a fallen tree blocks the way.

Can I make you aware of the obstruction?

I might point toward it.

Will you necessarily understand me?

No. I might be indicating the tree itself, the road beyond it, or something else entirely.

What can we do?

We can try again.

I might gesture toward another route. You might look where I am pointing. We might move a little way together and discover whether we have understood enough to proceed.

If the first gesture fails, can we recognize that failure?

Yes.

Can we attempt to correct it?

Yes.

But we do not yet share a language.

No.

Then the possibility of recognizing an inadequate expression and attempting a better one is already present.

Yes.

What are we trying to make available to each other?

Something about the obstruction and the possibility of another route.

We should notice what has happened.

We have not yet established a shared vocabulary. Nevertheless, we can attempt to communicate, recognize misunderstanding, correct it and arrive at sufficient understanding.

Someone might choose to call our gestures a language.

Very well.

But calling them a language does not answer the question.

What made one gesture inadequate and another sufficient?

What allowed us to recognize the difference?

And how did we know there was something still to communicate before we found a way of communicating it?

These questions remain.

## Chapter III — The Translation That Failed

Now imagine that we have learned one another's languages.

I say, in mine, that the door is closed.

You tell someone else, in yours, that the door closed.

Have you translated me accurately?

Not necessarily.

Why not?

Because I stated a condition, while you may have described an occurrence.

Are both sentences intelligible?

Yes.

Could both be grammatical?

Yes.

Then grammatical correctness alone cannot establish that the translation succeeded.

No.

What has failed?

A difference made intelligible by the first expression has not been adequately preserved in the second.

Now consider what we have admitted.

Two languages may have different words, different sounds and different grammatical constructions.

Yet we can recognize when an expression in one has accomplished what an expression in the other accomplished.

We can also recognize when it has failed.

Indeed, mistranslation is especially revealing.

A mistranslation may be a perfectly intelligible sentence.

It simply makes the wrong thing intelligible.

What allows us to recognize this?

We can compare what the expressions accomplish.

And what is that, if not a recognition that intelligibility cannot be identified simply with the particular words through which it is expressed?

Let us remain with this question.

For translation is so familiar that we scarcely find it astonishing.

But perhaps we should.

## Chapter IV — The Relation That Did Not Age

Consider a right triangle.

The relation we call Pythagoras' theorem has been expressed across thousands of years in different languages, notations and forms of demonstration.

Has the relation changed each time its expression changed?

No.

Could someone discover it without knowing the original words in which earlier people expressed it?

Yes.

Could someone misunderstand it?

Yes.

Could a translation misstate it?

Yes.

How would the mistake be recognized?

By attending to the relation that the expression purports to establish.

Then there is something extraordinary here.

The relation remains discoverable even when the words of earlier discoveries have changed or disappeared.

Different people, separated by centuries, may arrive at recognition of the same relation through entirely different linguistic paths.

What persists is not a single sentence.

And what makes the sentences correct or incorrect is not merely that they belong to an accepted language.

There is something to which they answer.

Do we have reason to believe that only mathematical language can behave this way?

We have established no such restriction.

A promise can be mistranslated.

A warning can be distorted.

An explanation can omit the very difference upon which its success depends.

A story can be retold in another language while preserving what made its events intelligible.

Mathematics offers a particularly vivid encounter with the phenomenon.

It does not give us grounds to declare the phenomenon exclusive to mathematics.

So we should ask a larger question.

**What is it about intelligibility that permits different linguistic expressions to answer to the same recognizable achievement?**

## Chapter V — The Island

Imagine an aircraft carrying three hundred people of many nationalities.

It crashes near an isolated island.

The survivors reach shore. They have no common language. They must live together, and in time their children will grow up among them.

What do they need?

Water. Food. Shelter. Ways to cooperate, warn, request, remember and explain.

Do they possess a shared vocabulary for all these things?

No.

Do they nevertheless encounter differences between what has happened and what remains to be done?

Yes.

Between a safe path and an unsafe one?

Yes.

Between an injury that has been treated and one still requiring attention?

Yes.

Between a shelter that fell during the night and one that remains standing?

Yes.

Then they are already living amid intelligible distinctions.

What happens when they cannot communicate those distinctions reliably?

They try.

They gesture. They repeat sounds. They borrow words from one another. They establish conventions. They correct misunderstandings.

Over time their common means of expression develop.

Children encounter those means as the beginnings of an ordinary language.

Now suppose the developing language has no dependable way to distinguish between a door that closed and a door that is closed.

Does that difference disappear from the island?

No.

Can the difference become important?

Certainly.

And if their available expressions are inadequate?

They may develop new ways of expressing it.

What prompts them?

The difference they need to communicate, and the inadequacy of their present means.

Who issued the command?

No one need have done so.

Then perhaps we have discovered something more remarkable than the persistence of intelligibility across languages.

**Intelligibility may participate in the development of language itself.**

Not simply as something language contains once it exists.

But as something already operative in the lives from which a shared language emerges.

What the people need to make intelligible may give them reason to develop expressions they do not yet possess.

And their recognition of linguistic inadequacy can become part of the process by which that inadequacy is repaired.

## Chapter VI — The Mandate

Someone on the island asks whether the boat has returned.

Another person answers, "It will return."

Has the question been answered?

No.

Is the answer intelligible?

Yes.

Then why is it inadequate?

Because the question asked whether an occurrence had taken place. The reply offered an expectation about the future.

Suppose the person answers instead, "It has not returned yet."

Has the question now been answered?

Yes.

Even if the listener is disappointed?

Yes.

What determined the adequacy of the answer?

What the question sought to establish.

And what determines whether a repair succeeds?

Whether the relevant difficulty has been overcome.

And whether a promise has been kept?

Whether the undertaking has been fulfilled.

And whether a translation is faithful?

Whether it has adequately preserved what it undertook to translate.

We have encountered a recurring feature.

An intelligible undertaking gives an attempted completion something to answer to.

A question is not answered by just any sentence.

A repair is not completed by merely declaring it complete.

A translation is not accurate simply because its words are grammatical.

Something must have become sufficient in the relevant way.

Where is this requirement coming from?

It is encountered within the intelligible character of the undertaking.

It need not be issued as a command by an external authority.

And it need not be invented by language before it can matter to the people whose language is developing.

This brings us to the possibility we have been approaching.

**The demands of intelligibility may become recognizable through the inadequacy of our attempts to express, enact or complete them.**

A failed expression can be repaired because there is something determinate it has failed to accomplish.

The people invent the new expression.

But the success of their invention is answerable to more than its mere existence.

## Chapter VII — Becoming Fluent

Now consider someone learning a second language.

At first, they hear a sentence in the unfamiliar language.

They translate it into their familiar language.

Only then do they understand.

After months or years of learning, something changes.

They hear the unfamiliar sentence again.

This time they understand it immediately.

What has happened?

The second language has become directly intelligible.

Must the person learn for the second time what it means for something to happen?

No.

Or what it means for something to remain the case?

No.

Or what it means to ask a question, recognize a failure or understand that a promise has been fulfilled?

Not from the beginning.

What, then, are they learning?

How another language makes such achievements available, together with the distinctions, practices and possibilities that language has developed through its own history.

At first, translation is the learner's path toward understanding.

Eventually understanding may be what allows the learner to translate.

A fluent speaker can understand a sentence perfectly and still struggle to translate it into another language.

Why?

Because understanding what an expression accomplishes and finding another expression that accomplishes it are different achievements.

And fluency itself?

It is not merely the rapid substitution of one vocabulary for another.

It is the capacity to participate immediately in an intelligible life through another language.

The fluent speaker can recognize a question as a question, a warning as a warning, a correction as a correction.

They can anticipate what is being said.

They can understand what has been left unsaid.

They can notice a difference that a beginner cannot yet hear.

And they can discover new ways of making things intelligible that their first language did not habitually bring to their attention.

The second language enlarges their possibilities of expression and understanding.

Yet the possibility of understanding was already involved in their acquiring it.

What, then, have they crossed?

Not from a world without intelligibility into one that possesses it.

They have acquired another fluent means of participating in intelligibility.

## Chapter VIII — The Question We Have Earned

Let us gather what we have recognized.

A difference can be encountered before it is spoken.

People without a shared language can recognize and correct misunderstandings.

Translation can preserve an intelligible achievement across different expressions.

Mistranslation can be recognized because such preservation has failed.

An intelligible relation can remain discoverable across centuries of linguistic change.

A shared language can emerge through the attempts of people who already encounter differences, relations and demands in their lives.

A second language can become fluent because understanding is not confined to the first language a person learns.

And an expression can be recognized as inadequate before a better expression has been found.

What follows?

We are entitled to ask whether intelligibility possesses an order that is not reducible to the grammatical structures of any particular language.

But what kind of order?

We do not yet know.

We must discover it.

We should not begin by demanding that experience fit a list of predetermined categories.

Instead, we can ask what changes when something becomes intelligible.

What is the difference between making something distinct and making something possible?

Between establishing a relation and repairing a failure?

Between bringing separate things into coherence and bringing an unsettled matter to rest?

What makes an achievement sufficient?

And how does what has become intelligible remain available in what follows?

These questions are not technical inventions.

They arise from the ordinary achievements we have already encountered.

The door closes.

Someone asks whether it is closed.

Someone answers.

The question is settled.

But then someone asks why the door closed.

The same answer no longer suffices.

What has changed?

And what would count as answering the new question?

**Here our investigation properly begins.**

---

## Chapter IX — What Makes an Answer Enough?

The door closes.

Someone asks, "Is it closed?"

Another person looks and replies, "Yes."

Has the question been answered?

Yes.

Suppose someone else asks, "Why did it close?"

Would the same answer suffice?

No.

What has changed?

The door has not changed. What is being asked has changed.

Then the adequacy of an answer does not depend upon its words alone.

No. It depends upon what those words accomplish in relation to the question.

Can a correct statement fail to answer a question?

Certainly. "The door is made of wood" may be true, but it does not tell us why the door closed.

And can an answer be sufficient without telling us everything about the door?

Yes. If the question is whether the door is closed, a look and a nod may be enough.

Enough for what?

For the question asked.

Let us remain here. It is easy to overlook what we have discovered because none of it is difficult to recognize. A matter can come to rest without every matter coming to rest. We may know that the door is closed and not know why it closed. We may know why it closed and not know who made it. The unanswered questions do not undo the answer already given.

Suppose I insist that no question has been answered until every possible question about its subject has been answered. What follows?

Then no ordinary answer would ever be enough.

And would that help us understand the ordinary fact that questions are answered?

No. It would obscure it.

So sufficiency is not the same as total knowledge.

No.

Does it follow that sufficiency is merely whatever someone decides to accept?

Consider a person who asks whether a bridge can carry a cart. Someone says, "Yes," without examining it. The speaker may be satisfied with the sound of the answer. But can the cart cross safely merely because the answer has been accepted?

No.

Then we must distinguish the ending of an exchange from the achievement that the exchange purports to establish.

Yes.

The same words can end a conversation without answering its question. A correct answer can settle the question and leave other questions alive. An answer can be unwelcome and still sufficient. The question of enoughness must be approached through the particular undertaking, not through the wish that nothing further ever remain to be known.

Now imagine that the question is not "Is the door closed?" but "Which of these doors is closed?" There are three doors along a corridor.

Would a reply of "Yes" answer it?

No.

Would pointing to one door answer it?

It might.

What has the pointing accomplished?

It has made one door distinct from the others for the purpose at hand.

And if I ask instead, "Can we get through?"

Then identifying the closed door may not be enough. We need to know whether a route is available.

Can the same physical scene admit different completed understandings?

Yes.

Do those understandings all become complete in the same way?

Not obviously.

Good. We need not yet name their forms. We need only recognize that one question asks for an identification, another for an available passage, another for an explanation, and another for the correction of an obstacle. The difference between their demands is already intelligible to us.

And now an unexpected question arises.

If different questions make different kinds of completion possible, is the difference something we have introduced by inventing words for them, or something we can discover by examining what the questions require?

We can discover it by examining what they require.

Then let us examine.

## Chapter X — The Door That Can Be Opened

The door is closed.

Someone asks, "Can we pass through?"

What would answer the question?

We might try the handle.

The handle turns, but the door does not move.

What have we learned?

That turning the handle is not sufficient to open the door.

Have we learned that passage is impossible?

Not yet. The door may be stuck. There may be another entrance.

Someone discovers a small bolt at the bottom of the door and withdraws it. The door opens.

What changed?

The bolt no longer prevents the movement.

Was the bolt the only thing in the scene?

No. There was a door, a frame, a handle, hinges, a wall, a passage and people trying to get through.

Yet what made the difference at the decisive moment?

The withdrawal of the bolt.

We can recognize a completion that gathers around one decisive point without pretending the rest of the situation has disappeared.

But suppose the bolt is withdrawn and the passage beyond the door is filled with fallen timber.

Is the original question settled?

Not if the question was whether we could get through.

Why not?

Because opening the door did not make a complete route available.

What must now be accomplished?

A passable way must exist from here to the place we need to reach.

Could one cleared piece of timber be enough?

Not if other pieces still block the way.

Then this completion does not gather around a single decisive obstruction.

No. The passage as a whole must become usable.

Here are two recognizably different shapes of sufficiency. In the first, one bolt is decisive. In the second, the adequacy of a route depends upon the condition of a connected extent. We have not imposed a distinction from a list; the undertaking has made the difference visible.

Now imagine something else. Before the storm, everyone believed that the old door was the only entrance. A person discovers a wide opening in the opposite wall, with a clear path beyond it.

Must the old door be repaired in order for the people to pass through?

No.

Does it remain stuck?

Yes.

Then has the obstacle been removed?

Not physically.

And yet?

It no longer governs the undertaking. They can pass by another way.

Could we describe this as a successful repair of the old door?

No. The door remains unrepaired.

Could we describe it simply as another blocked route becoming unblocked?

Not exactly. What changed was the relevance of the old route's obstruction.

Something that had governed the undertaking has ceased to govern it, although the thing itself remains.

Yes.

And we recognized this without first being given a technical name for it.

Yes.

Let us now look back at the three events.

One bolt was withdrawn, and the door opened.

A whole passage was cleared, and the way through became usable.

A different entrance was found, and the unrepaired door ceased to determine whether the journey could continue.

Are these merely three sizes of the same event?

No. Their different ways of becoming sufficient are part of what makes them intelligible.

Would our descriptions be adequate if we said of each only, "The problem was solved"?

They might report a general success. But they would conceal how that success occurred.

And if our inquiry is concerned with the forms in which things become intelligibly complete, can we afford to conceal those differences?

No.

Then we have begun to discover an order.

Not a table we have been instructed to memorize. Not an inventory of words. An order encountered through the different ways in which a question can come to rest.

There is still something missing.

After the bolt is withdrawn, the door can open again tomorrow. After the passage is cleared, people can carry supplies along it. After the alternative entrance is found, the old obstruction no longer directs their plans.

Do these achievements remain available in the same way?

That is our next question.


## Chapter XI — What Remains After the Answer?

Suppose I ask which of two keys opens the storehouse.

You try the first. It does not turn. You try the second. The lock opens.

Has my question been answered?

Yes.

If I ask again tomorrow, must we repeat the experiment before we can know which key it was?

Not necessarily. We can remember what was established.

What remains from yesterday's achievement?

The distinction between the keys remains available. We know which one opened the lock.

Is that the same as saying that the lock is opening now?

No. Yesterday's opening has ended. What we learned can still stand.

Then an intelligible completion need not remain present as a continuing occurrence in order to remain available.

Yes.

Consider another case. Two people agree that the storehouse key will be kept with the person who distributes food.

They make the agreement and go their separate ways.

Is the agreement over when their conversation ends?

The conversation is over. The arrangement still bears upon what each is entitled or expected to do.

Does it remain in the same way as the discovered difference between the two keys?

Not quite. The agreement continues to govern a relation between people.

Suppose someone now asks for the key. Why does the earlier agreement matter?

Because the present request is to be understood in light of what was settled before.

The earlier achievement has not merely left a fact to be remembered. It has established a continuing bearing within which later acts can be understood.

Now a third case.

A path through the trees has been cleared from the shelter to the spring.

The work is finished. The people leave their tools and return home.

What remains?

A route that can be used.

Is its significance exhausted by the proposition that it is clear?

No. People can now carry water along it.

So the completion has left a capacity effective in subsequent activity.

Yes.

Let us compare the three.

The key was identified. That determination can stand.

The agreement was made. Its terms can continue to bear upon what people do.

The route was opened. It can now be used.

Have we described three successive stages through which every achievement must pass?

No. Each case brings a different aspect of what remains to the foreground.

Could the identified key also be used, and could the route also be described as an established fact?

Certainly.

Then our task is not to place objects into separate boxes. It is to recognize the manner in which a particular intelligible achievement remains available.

And that manner can matter to whether our account is adequate.

Yes.

Now consider the person who found the alternative path while the original entrance remained blocked.

What remains there?

The old entrance no longer has to govern the journey to the spring.

Does the blockage cease to exist?

No.

Could the blockage matter again if the community later needed that particular entrance?

Yes.

Then what was released was not the existence of the blockage, but its authority over a particular undertaking.

Exactly.

And can that release remain available afterward?

Yes. People may continue to use the alternative path without returning to the former obstruction.

We now have two questions to ask of a completion.

First, how did the matter become sufficient?

Second, how does what was accomplished remain available?

Are those questions identical?

No.

Can both be answered by attending to the same event?

Yes.

And if we neglect one of them?

We may describe a success without understanding its structure.

Then we must go on.

## Chapter XII — A True Sentence, an Unanswered Question

A visitor comes to the island's storehouse and asks, "Where is the key?"

The keeper answers, "The door is locked."

Is what the keeper said true?

It may be.

Has the visitor's question been answered?

No.

Suppose the keeper adds, "The storehouse was built last month."

Another true statement?

Perhaps.

An answer?

No.

And if the keeper says, "The key is with Mara"?

Now the visitor knows where to look.

What has made the third sentence sufficient when the others were not?

It answers what was being asked.

Then truth and adequacy to a particular question cannot simply be substituted for one another.

Yes.

But let us be careful. If the question had been "Is the door locked?" could the first sentence have been enough?

Yes.

And if it had been "When was the storehouse built?" might the second have been enough?

Yes.

So the same sentence can complete one inquiry while leaving another untouched.

Yes.

What changed?

The question to which the sentence was being asked to answer.

Did the sentence's words change?

No.

Then the intelligible achievement of a sentence in a living exchange is not given by its words considered in isolation.

It depends also on what the sentence is doing there.

Now suppose the visitor misunderstands "with Mara" as meaning that Mara is standing beside the key, when the keeper meant that Mara has taken responsibility for keeping it.

Can the visitor's misunderstanding become apparent?

Yes. The visitor may go looking for Mara near the storehouse and not find her.

What might the keeper do?

Explain further: "Mara has it. She took it home."

Has anything changed in the physical location of the key while the explanation is being repaired?

Not necessarily.

What has changed?

The visitor can now understand the relevant arrangement more adequately.

Is the correction merely the addition of more words?

No. The additional words succeed because they remove a particular misunderstanding.

And when the visitor understands, what can follow?

The visitor can go to Mara and ask for the key.

So an intelligible completion can become effective in action without being itself a physical alteration of the object concerned.

Yes.

We have now encountered a fact standing, an arrangement bearing, and an understanding becoming effective.

But the achievement in each case is still only properly described by noticing what question was alive and what made it enough.

Then perhaps we are ready for another question.

When we recognize these different ways of completion and persistence, are we discovering differences that belong to the world we encounter, or merely inventing differences among our descriptions?

Let us test it rather than answer too soon.

## Chapter XIII — Before the Sentence Is Ready

Imagine a child looking at two baskets of fruit.

One contains three oranges. The other contains two.

The child has not yet learned the number words used by the adults.

Can the child nevertheless notice that one basket has an orange the other lacks?

Yes, by comparing or pairing the fruit.

Suppose the child takes an orange from the first basket and places it in the second.

The arrangement changes.

Does the change wait for a sentence?

No.

If the child now pauses, looking at the baskets, must we know the child's unspoken words before acknowledging that something can have become recognizable?

No.

What have we seen?

An encounter with a difference, an action, and a changed arrangement.

Now the adults begin teaching number words.

Does the child suddenly acquire oranges, baskets, differences, and the possibility of arranging them?

No.

What does the new language make possible?

The child can state the numbers, ask how many there are, recount the change, and communicate about absent baskets.

Can the words also help the child make distinctions that were previously difficult?

Yes.

Then language can enlarge and refine intelligible life without being the creator of every distinction it enables us to express.

Yes.

Suppose a new language is forming among the islanders. One speaker has not yet found the word that will distinguish a completed task from a task still being attempted.

Can the distinction be present to that speaker as something they are struggling to say?

Yes.

Can another person recognize from a gesture that their first interpretation was wrong?

Yes.

And can the shared expression become more definite through these attempts?

Yes.

Then an inadequacy in expression can be encountered from within intelligibility, rather than from a place outside it.

Yes.

Is this why language can be repaired?

It is at least part of what makes repair possible.

And if we wish to understand that possibility, where must we look?

Not merely at the words that eventually succeed, but at the intelligible undertaking in which their success becomes recognizable.

Good. We have no need to announce the whole of our inquiry yet.

We have earned another question, and it is enough for now:

**What kinds of difference must we be able to recognize in order to know what has become intelligibly complete?**

## Chapter XIV — One Answer, Three Questions

A carpenter on the island has made a wooden door for the storehouse.

Someone asks, "Is the door closed?"

The carpenter looks and says yes.

Has the question been answered?

Yes.

Someone else asks, "Will the door keep the rain out?"

Can the carpenter give the same answer?

Not merely by saying that it is closed.

Why not?

Because a closed door may still admit rain through a gap.

And if a third person asks whether the door can be opened from inside?

That is yet another question.

What must we now recognize?

The door can be closed, weatherproof, and openable from within, but these are not the same accomplishment.

Suppose the carpenter presses a strip of cloth into the gap. Water no longer enters during the next shower.

Has the door been repaired?

In the respect that allowed rain through, yes.

Suppose the latch still cannot be reached from inside. Is that repair undone?

No. The weatherproofing remains. Another requirement has not been satisfied.

Then a new demand does not necessarily cancel a completed achievement.

No.

Now imagine the carpenter replacing the latch. It can be operated from inside, but only while the wood is dry. When the frame swells, it jams again.

Was the replacement useless?

No. It answered some of the difficulty, but not the full demand of reliable operation under the conditions in which the door will be used.

What must be done?

The carpenter must examine more than the latch alone: the frame, the clearance, and the way the door moves when the weather changes.

Could a single adjustment be enough?

Perhaps. But if the relevant demand concerns the door's reliable operation across the whole movement and across changing conditions, success at one point may not suffice.

Then what decides whether one point or a larger field must be attended to?

What the undertaking requires.

Let us consider a different case.

The storehouse was once entered through the door on its western side. After a storm, that entrance is blocked by a fallen tree. The people discover that a second entrance on the eastern side can serve their needs.

Must the fallen tree be removed before they can say that access to the storehouse has been restored?

No.

What has happened?

The old blockage remains, but it no longer governs whether they can enter.

Has a missing piece been installed in the old entrance?

No.

Has the entire western route been repaired?

No.

Yet a difficulty has ceased to govern the undertaking.

Yes.

Then there are different ways an undertaking can become sufficient.

A single place may settle the matter. A whole arrangement may have to hold together. Or a condition that once governed the undertaking may cease to govern it at all.

Are these merely different quantities of work?

No. They are differences in what counts as enough.

And if we were to call every one of them simply a repair?

We might lose what distinguishes them.

So before choosing a name, we must ask: what was the live demand, where did sufficiency arise, and what stopped being required?

Yes.

Now another question.

Did the door have to be mentioned in a sentence for any of these differences to occur?

No.

Can a sentence nevertheless complete a separate undertaking by making those differences clear to someone who was absent?

Yes.

Then we have to attend to the world, to the sentence, and to the person's understanding without treating them as one event.

Exactly.

The distinctions are beginning to multiply, but they do not do so arbitrarily. Each appears because one account that was sufficient for something becomes inadequate to something else.

That is how we shall continue.

## Chapter XV — What We Cannot Yet Say

A young child has been helping to carry water.

Two containers stand beside the shelter. One has a small crack. The child pours water into it, notices the leak, and moves the water to the other.

Has the child recognized something?

The child has acted in a way that makes the crack's consequence relevant.

Must we suppose the child has already formed a sentence about leakage?

No.

An adult asks which container is good.

The child points to the uncracked one.

Would it be accurate to say that the child has no intelligible access to the difference simply because no explanation has been spoken?

No.

Would it be accurate to claim that we know every detail of the child's understanding from the pointing alone?

No.

Then what may we say?

The action and gesture make a relevant distinction available, while leaving some matters about the child's understanding undecided.

Good. We have no need to pretend to know more than the encounter establishes.

Now the child begins learning the community's words for leaking, cracked, full, and empty.

Does this learning merely decorate what the child already knows?

No. It makes new comparisons, questions and explanations possible.

Does it create the crack?

No.

Does it create the difference between containing water and losing it?

No.

Can it make that difference more readily available across other situations?

Yes.

The child may now tell another person about the cracked container without carrying it into view.

May the listener understand a difficulty that they have never seen?

Yes.

May they ask whether the container is empty because the water leaked out, or whether it was never filled?

Yes.

And in answering, can the child discover that several differences once folded together in experience now need to be kept apart?

Yes.

Then the learning of language can itself occasion further intelligible achievements.

It can.

Here is a difficult-looking question with an elementary beginning.

When the child learns to say "the container leaked," what, exactly, has become complete?

The child may have acquired the expression, or correctly described an event, or enabled another person's understanding.

Are those one and the same?

No.

Could the expression be grammatical while the description is false?

Yes.

Could the description be true while the listener still does not understand which container is meant?

Yes.

Could the listener understand which container is meant while remaining uncertain whether the leak has been repaired?

Yes.

Then each achievement is answerable to a different demand.

And we must not take the completion of one as evidence that all the others are complete.

Yes.

Suppose the child finally says, "That one leaked yesterday, but it holds water now."

What might the listener understand?

That an earlier failure and a present condition differ, and that the failure may have been corrected.

Could the listener ask how the repair was done?

Yes.

And would that new question show that the earlier statement accomplished nothing?

No. It would show that another question has become available.

This, too, is a way completed intelligibility persists.

It can furnish the ground from which another question arises.

We have therefore learned not to fear what remains open.

An answer can be enough without being the last answer.

A word can be learned without completing the child's education.

A repair can succeed without preventing every future problem.

A translation can be faithful without reproducing every feature of the original language.

What matters is not the fantasy of a world in which no question can ever arise again.

It is the recognition of what has become sufficiently determinate, how it has done so, and what that determination makes possible.

Are we ready, then, to count the forms of intelligibility?

Not yet.

Why not?

Because a count would tell us how many positions our account proposes, whereas we still need to recognize why each difference deserves a position.

What should we do next?

Take an achievement that appears simple, follow what actually changes, and ask what would make our description of it exact.

That is enough for now.

## Chapter XVI — The Mark That Remains

A child draws a circle in sand.

Has something happened?

Yes. The child has moved a finger and left a mark.

When the finger is lifted, does the drawing stop being present?

No. The circle remains.

Could the mark be erased?

Yes.

If another child traces the same circle before it is erased, has the first child's movement occurred again?

No. The second movement is new.

And yet?

The second movement follows a form made available by the first.

Suppose the first child says, "That is where the stones must go."

Has the circle acquired another place in the activity?

Yes. It can now guide the placement of the stones.

Must the circle remain in the sand forever for the stones to be placed correctly?

No.

Could the stones, once arranged, make the mark unnecessary?

Yes.

Then an intelligible achievement may remain available without the material trace that first helped bring it about.

It may.

But let us avoid saying too much too soon. If the child forgets the arrangement and the mark has been erased, can we simply declare that the plan is still effective?

No. We must look at what remains available and how.

So when we ask what persists, what must we distinguish?

The mark in the sand, the arrangement it helped establish, the person's recognition of that arrangement, and the possibility of acting upon it.

Suppose the stones have been placed, and the children now use them as the boundary of a small garden. Has the original circle merely survived as a remembered picture?

No. The arrangement is doing something.

It is keeping a place distinct and helping the children decide where to plant.

And if the stones are subsequently moved, leaving a garden whose boundary is marked by young plants?

The earlier arrangement has helped make another one possible, even though the stones no longer occupy their original positions.

Could we call everything here a memory?

We could, but we would lose the difference between remembering where the stones were and possessing a garden whose organization arose through the earlier work.

What, then, is the danger?

That a single word for persistence will conceal the different ways an achievement remains available.

Now imagine that one stone sits crookedly. A child straightens it.

What has changed?

One particular place in the boundary.

Suppose every stone is crooked because the ground slopes sharply. Is straightening one enough?

No. The whole arrangement may need reconsidering.

Suppose instead that the children abandon the stone boundary because they discover that the growing plants themselves mark the garden adequately.

What has changed then?

The demand that the stones must mark the boundary has ceased to govern the work.

Have the stones vanished?

Not necessarily.

Could they remain where they are?

Yes.

Then we are again looking at several kinds of achievement whose differences are not merely differences of size.

One position is made right.

An arrangement is made right across its extent.

A former condition loses its authority over what is now required.

Must we name these differences now?

Only after recognizing them.

And when we do name them, what should the names preserve?

The actual structure of what has become sufficient.

The children finish for the afternoon.

One looks at the garden and says, "Now we can plant."

Has planting already taken place?

No.

What has been accomplished?

A possibility has been made available.

And how does it remain?

In the ground that has been prepared, the places that have been marked, and the capacity to begin the next activity.

The children have not completed the planting.

They have completed something that makes planting possible.

Should we describe their work as incomplete because another activity can begin?

No.

Nor should we describe the planting as completed simply because the preparation is finished.

No.

An intelligible achievement must be allowed its own end.

And that end may be precisely the beginning of something else.

## Chapter XVII — The Word That Has No Equivalent

Two speakers who know different languages look at the finished garden.

One says a word from her childhood language.

The other asks what it means.

She answers, "It is what you feel when the work is ready, but you don't want to leave it yet."

Has she translated the word?

She has begun to explain what it makes intelligible.

Could she simply have supplied a word in the listener's language?

Perhaps, if there were a suitable one.

And if there is not?

She may use several sentences, an example, or a gesture. She may invite the other person to remember a similar experience.

Suppose the listener says, "I know that feeling."

Have they necessarily acquired the exact word?

No.

Has some understanding nevertheless become possible?

Yes.

What has crossed between them?

Not necessarily one word for another. Something of the experience and the distinction the first speaker was trying to make available.

And if the listener responds, "I know the feeling, but I would not use it in that situation"?

Then they may understand each other sufficiently to discover a further difference.

Could such a difference itself become the subject of conversation?

Yes.

Then the absence of an exact equivalent has not shown that translation or understanding is impossible.

No. It has disclosed something the speakers must attend to.

Suppose the learner later uses the word at an inappropriate moment.

Can the first speaker correct the usage?

Yes.

How?

By showing which circumstance matters, what the attempted use missed, or how the word's place in the language differs from what the learner assumed.

What makes correction possible if no single word in the learner's first language corresponds perfectly?

The speakers can examine what is being accomplished by the expression and where the attempted use fails.

Then fluency cannot mean possession of a dictionary of exact equivalents.

No.

It means becoming able to participate in the distinctions, implications and undertakings through which a language is lived.

But is there only one achievement in this conversation?

No. The first speaker is attempting an explanation. The learner is attempting recognition. Later the learner attempts an appropriate use.

Could the explanation succeed while the learner's later use fails?

Yes.

Could the learner understand the word and still be unable to explain it concisely in another language?

Yes.

Then the difficulty of translating need not be the difficulty of understanding.

No.

And the difficulty of understanding need not prove that the relevant experience is absent.

No.

What, then, is the shared ground?

The possibility of encountering, making, and correcting intelligible distinctions together.

Now the first speaker asks the learner to use the word in a new sentence.

The learner begins, hesitates, and stops.

Has anything intelligible happened in that hesitation?

Yes. The learner recognizes that the intended sentence is not yet adequate.

Does the learner need to know the correct sentence already in order to recognize that the first attempt will not do?

No.

Then what follows?

The very recognition of inadequacy can guide the search for a more sufficient articulation.

A new sentence is attempted.

The first speaker smiles and says, "Yes, that is how we say it."

Has the learner mastered the whole language?

No.

Has a particular achievement become complete?

Yes.

What remains available?

A newly acquired expression, a more precise distinction, and a capacity to use it again.

Is the completion a final silence?

No. It allows conversation to continue.

And now we should return to the garden.

The second speaker looks at the young plants and says the unfamiliar word without translating it.

The first understands.

No further explanation is needed.

What has happened?

Two people have found a shared expression for something one could recognize before the other could say.

And does the newly shared word replace the earlier experience?

No.

It gives the experience another way to be present between them.

Let that be enough for this chapter.

## Chapter XVIII — What the Answer Makes Possible

The two speakers are still in the garden.

The second has used the newly learned word. The first has understood without explanation.

Have they finished something?

Yes. The word no longer needs to be carried across by a translation.

And now?

The first speaker points toward the stones around the garden.

“What have we made?”

“A garden,” says the second.

“Is that all?”

The second looks again. “A boundary. Places for planting. A path between the beds.”

Was the first answer false?

No.

Why, then, did the speaker answer again?

Because another question had become visible within the first.

Did the first speaker already know that the answer would lead there?

Perhaps not.

The second kneels to straighten a stone.

“Who put this one here?”

“You did.”

“When?”

“Yesterday, before the rain.”

“And why did I put it here?”

“To keep the edge from moving.”

The first smiles. “We have scarcely moved, yet we have gone from the garden to the stone, from the stone to yesterday, and from yesterday to the work it was meant to do.”

Have they discovered different things?

Yes.

Did the stones change while they were asking?

No.

Then what changed?

What they understood about the stones.

But did every question come from nowhere?

No. Each was made possible by something already understood.

Let us examine that.

Before anyone knew which stone was meant, could “Who put this one here?” have been asked with the same precision?

No. They first needed to know which one.

Once they knew who placed it, could they ask when that person did so?

Yes.

And once they knew when, was the reason for placing it established?

No.

Then an answer may complete one question and leave another open.

We have recognized that before.

But look more closely. Does it merely leave an old question waiting, or can it make a new one possible?

The second looks at the stone. “I did not think to ask why I had placed it there until you reminded me that I had.”

So what has the answer done?

It has not only finished a question. It has changed the questions that can now be asked.

The first considers this.

“Then why did you put it there?”

“To keep the earth in place.”

“Does it?”

The second touches the soil beside it. Rain has washed a thin line of earth beneath the stone.

“Not entirely.”

Was the earlier answer mistaken?

“I meant to keep the earth in place.”

“And you did put it there for that reason.”

“Then that answer was true.”

“But the stone has not entirely done what you intended.”

Is the truth of why a person acted the same as the success of the action?

No.

What has their next question disclosed?

A difference between the reason for an action and what the action achieved.

Would they have reached that difference if they had stopped after discovering who placed the stone?

Not in this conversation.

Does that mean the answer about who placed it was incomplete?

No. It answered what had been asked.

Then the earlier completion need not disappear because another question reveals something further.

The second lifts the stone.

“Shall I put it back more firmly?”

“Would that answer the difficulty?”

“It might.”

“What if the rain continues to carry the soil away beneath the others?”

The second looks along the boundary. Several stones show the same narrow gap.

“Then perhaps it is not one stone.”

What has changed in their understanding?

They thought one place needed correction. Now they see that several places may be involved.

Has their looking outward from the first stone brought them back to examine the first answer more closely?

Yes.

Is that not a curious movement?

They looked inward at one stone. Its condition led them outward toward the others. What they found among the others changed how they understood the first.

The second says, “Then we must ask whether the whole edge will hold.”

Will lifting this stone answer that question?

No.

Will straightening all the stones necessarily answer it?

“Perhaps the rain would still pass beneath them.”

Then what must they learn?

How the water moves through the garden.

And have they learned it?

Not yet.

Has their inquiry therefore accomplished nothing?

The second shakes her head.

“We know it is not enough merely to make one stone straight.”

“And is that the answer to how the rain moves?”

“No.”

“What, then, have we discovered?”

“What we need to ask next.”

Is that itself an achievement?

Consider the beginning. They stood beside the finished garden, pleased to have found a word for an experience they shared. They were not yet asking about water beneath the boundary.

Now they know where the uncertainty lies.

Could they have begun by asking how the whole boundary would survive another storm?

They could have spoken those words. But would the question have had the same place in their understanding?

“No,” says the second. “We would not yet have seen why it mattered.”

Then a question can be available as words before its necessity becomes recognizable within an undertaking.

The first sits on the path.

“Must every question be answered before we can go home?”

“No.”

“Must every answer lead us to another question?”

“Not always. Sometimes nothing further matters to what we are doing.”

“And if something further does matter?”

“Then what we have learned gives us somewhere to begin.”

What is guiding the next question?

Not the mere fact that another sentence can be spoken. The difference now encountered in the garden and the work they wish the garden to perform.

“Then there is a question within the work itself,” says the second.

“Does the work speak?”

“No.”

“Do the stones ask?”

“No.”

“Yet can the difficulty be there before we find the words?”

“Yes. The rain can wash the soil away whether or not we ask why.”

“And finding the question?”

“That is our achievement.”

The first looks again at the line of stones.

“Earlier we asked what we had made. That answer brought one stone to our attention. Asking who placed it brought yesterday into view. Asking why it had been placed there exposed the purpose of the boundary. Asking whether it fulfilled that purpose brought us back to the soil. And now the soil has made us ask how the rain travels.”

“Each answer has given us another place to look.”

“Did we know at the beginning what the last question would be?”

“No.”

“Have we become less certain of everything?”

“No. We know more, and know more exactly what we do not yet know.”

“Then perhaps we should not think of inquiry as a heap of questions.”

“What should we think of it as?”

The second takes a little soil in her palm.

“I cannot yet say. But I can see what happened. We asked about what was before us. What we learned changed what we could ask. Then what we learned next changed the meaning of what we had noticed before.”

“And this can happen again?”

“Yes.”

“Without making what we discovered earlier disappear?”

“Yes, unless the new discovery shows that we were wrong about it.”

The first stands.

“How shall we discover where the rain goes?”

The second looks up toward the shelter.

“We could bring a little water tomorrow and watch.”

Has the question about the boundary been answered?

No.

Has the question about how to begin investigating it acquired an answer?

Yes.

The speakers leave the stones as they are.

The garden is still a garden. The gaps beneath the boundary remain. The rain has not yet returned.

But now the next visit has a purpose that was not clear when they first stood there.

A question has come from an answer. A finer answer has come from looking again. The finer answer has disclosed another question.

Is this merely what they are saying about understanding?

No. It is what they have been doing.

And how much must they understand before this moment can be complete?

Only enough for what has now become clear.

Tomorrow may disclose more.

Let that be enough for today.

## Chapter XIX — What Tomorrow Receives

The next morning, the two speakers return to the garden.

One carries a small jug of water.

The other carries nothing.

“What shall we do?” asks the first.

“Watch where the water goes.”

“Why?”

The second looks surprised.

“We discovered yesterday that the rain was passing beneath the stones. We wanted to know how.”

“But yesterday has ended.”

“Yes.”

“Then why does yesterday's question still concern us?”

The second sets down the jug.

“Because we did not answer it.”

“Must we remember every word we said yesterday to know what we came to do?”

“No.”

“Must the rain fall again exactly as it did yesterday?”

“No. We can pour water and watch.”

“Then what has remained?”

The second considers the stones.

“The difficulty. The question. Perhaps what we learned about the boundary.”

Are those the same thing?

No.

The gap beneath one stone might still be there whether they remembered it or not.

The question could remain in their minds even if the gap had been repaired overnight.

And what they learned yesterday could be true even if they no longer recalled the moment in which they learned it.

Let us look more closely.

The first pours a little water beside the nearest stone.

It runs down the side and disappears into the earth.

“Is that what happened yesterday?”

“Not exactly. Yesterday we saw where the soil had been washed away. Today we can see where the water goes.”

“Then today has given us something yesterday did not.”

“Yes.”

“And yesterday?”

“It gave us the reason to look here.”

Could today's observation have been made without yesterday's question?

It could. Anyone might have poured water on the soil.

Would it necessarily have meant the same thing to them?

No. They might only have been watering the bed.

What makes this pouring an investigation?

The question to which it is being asked to answer.

The first moves to the next stone.

“Then something from yesterday is here with us.”

“Not yesterday itself.”

“No. Yesterday is gone.”

“Nor the words exactly as we spoke them.”

“No.”

“Yet we have not begun again from nothing.”

The second smiles.

“That is why I brought the jug.”

They pour water along the boundary.

At one place the water sinks almost immediately. At another it runs along the surface and carries away a thread of soil.

They watch until the jug is empty.

“What have we learned?”

“That the water does not travel the same way all along the edge.”

“Did we know that yesterday?”

“No.”

“Does that make yesterday's discovery false?”

“No. We knew that soil had been carried away beneath some of the stones. We did not yet know how the water moved.”

“Then today's discovery has added something.”

“Yes.”

“Has it changed yesterday's event?”

“No.”

“Has it changed what yesterday's observation means to us?”

The second looks again at the gap.

“Yes. What seemed to be a problem with one stone may belong to the slope of the whole bed.”

Can the meaning of an earlier discovery grow while the earlier event remains what it was?

It appears so.

But let us not confuse the things involved.

The rain that fell yesterday is not falling again now.

The speaker who noticed the gap yesterday is not repeating that first noticing merely by looking again today.

And the soil carried away yesterday has not returned to its former place.

Yet the earlier discovery has not vanished from their work.

How is that possible?

The second answers slowly.

“Because what we found out can still guide us.”

“Is that the same as remembering how you felt when you first found it?”

“No. I hardly remember how I felt.”

“Is it the same as remembering every sentence?”

“No. We might tell the story differently.”

“Then what must be preserved if we are to continue the inquiry?”

“That the soil was washed away, that the boundary may not hold, and that we still need to understand what the water is doing.”

The first thinks for a moment.

“Suppose I tell someone tomorrow that we discovered the stones were crooked.”

“Would that preserve what we discovered?”

“No. Some are straight. Our concern is how the water moves beneath them.”

“Suppose I say that we discovered the garden could never hold its soil.”

“That would be too much. We have not established that.”

“So what we carry forward cannot be just any story about yesterday.”

“No. It has to answer to what we actually found.”

They sit on the dry path.

“Now imagine that someone else comes here tomorrow,” says the first. “Someone who has never seen the garden.”

“We could show them the gaps and how the water runs.”

“Would that person have been with us yesterday?”

“No.”

“Could they nevertheless understand what we have established?”

“Yes.”

“Would their understanding be our understanding occurring again?”

The second hesitates.

“They would be understanding it themselves.”

“Could they discover something we missed?”

“Certainly.”

“Would what we learned cease to be available because they learned more?”

“No. Unless their discovery showed that we had misunderstood what we saw.”

Then the same determination may be taken up in different acts of understanding.

And those acts need not be identical.

The first points to the water darkening the soil.

“Can two people see this at different times?”

“Yes.”

“Can they both understand that water travels toward the lower ground?”

“Yes.”

“Must their experiences be the same experience?”

“No.”

“Then perhaps what becomes intelligible to them cannot be identified simply with either person's moment of recognizing it.”

The second remains quiet.

“Is that what you meant when you said yesterday had not returned?”

“Yes. Yesterday's moment has passed. But what became clear in it can still matter.”

“Then what is a moment?”

“An instant?”

“Was yesterday's discovery an instant?”

The second thinks.

“We looked at the stones, talked, remembered the rain, and changed our question. It took time.”

“Could you say precisely when you first understood that one stone might not be the whole difficulty?”

“Not precisely.”

“Yet did that understanding become available?”

“Yes. It brought us here today.”

“Then perhaps the moment we are speaking of is not merely a very small piece of clock time.”

“What is it?”

“The occasion in which something becomes clear enough to matter in what follows.”

“Even if the becoming clear takes a while?”

“Yes.”

“Even if no one can point to the exact second?”

“Yes.”

The second looks toward the shelter.

“Then the moment is gone, but its work is not necessarily gone.”

What does that mean?

Not that the moment continues forever.

Not that remembering preserves every part of it.

And not that whatever we once believed must remain true.

It means that an achievement can belong to one occasion and remain available to another.

They rise and examine the edge of the bed.

The first asks, “Have we resolved the problem now?”

“Not yet. We have seen how water travels in two places.”

“Have we understood anything?”

“Yes.”

“Has something become intelligible that was not clear yesterday?”

“Yes. The slope affects the way the water carries the soil.”

“Then why do you say the problem is unresolved?”

“Because knowing what happens is not the same as having made the boundary hold.”

“Could we understand the problem without having repaired it?”

“We do.”

“Could we repair it without knowing every reason why the repair works?”

“We might.”

“Then understanding and resolution are not always one achievement.”

“No.”

“And what about the water's path? Did it wait until we understood it before it could be there to be discovered?”

“No.”

“What, then, happened here this morning?”

“We came to understand something about it.”

“And when did that understanding become enough?”

“Enough for which question?”

The first laughs.

“That is the question I ought to have asked.”

They consider what they now know.

For today's inquiry into the route of the water, their observation has given an answer in the places they tested. For the larger undertaking of keeping the soil in the garden, more work remains.

Is the morning incomplete because tomorrow may require more?

No.

Would it be complete to say that the whole boundary is now repaired?

No.

Then one achievement has an end of its own, even as it becomes the beginning of another.

The second gathers the empty jug.

“Tomorrow we can change the slope beside the stones and try again.”

“Will we need to repeat everything we said today?”

“No.”

“Will we need to remember what today's water showed us?”

“We will need what we learned, whether we carry it in memory, in a mark, or in a way we have changed the bed.”

“And if someone else does the work?”

“They can take up what we found.”

The first looks over the garden.

“Yesterday taught us what to ask. Today taught us something about the answer. Tomorrow we may learn what to change.”

“Three days,” says the second.

“Three different occasions.”

“And one continuing undertaking.”

“Does that make the three days one moment?”

“No. Each has happened in its own way.”

“Then what connects them?”

The second puts a hand upon the nearest stone.

“What became clear did not have to become unclear when the day ended.”

The stone is still in its place. The soil is still damp where the water passed. The speakers begin to walk home.

The first asks, “And if we teach another person what we have learned, what must remain the same for that person to understand the very thing we understood?”

The second looks back at the garden.

“That,” she says, “we have not yet answered.”

But they know where to begin.

Let that be enough for today.

## Chapter XX — The Same Thing, Understood Again

The following morning, a third person comes to the garden.

She has not seen the earlier rain. She did not help place the stones. She has not heard the conversations of the previous two days.

One speaker shows her the gaps beneath the boundary.

“We discovered that water was carrying the soil away.”

The visitor looks carefully.

“Who discovered that?”

“We did, two days ago.”

“And must I have been here then to understand what you discovered?”

“No. Look beneath this stone.”

The visitor kneels.

“I can see where the soil has gone.”

“Then do you understand what we understood?”

“Perhaps. But how do you know that what I understand is the same thing?”

What would answer her?

Could they say that they have spoken the same words?

They could.

But suppose she heard the words and imagined that the soil had been removed by someone digging beneath the stones.

Would she have understood the same occurrence?

No.

Could they say that she has seen the same stones?

She has.

But someone might look at the stones and notice their colours, their shapes or their arrangement without understanding anything about the soil being carried away.

Then neither the repetition of words nor the presence of the same objects is sufficient.

What else is needed?

The visitor points toward the lower end of the garden.

“The water runs downhill. It carries the loose soil through the spaces beneath the stones.”

The first speaker smiles.

“That is what we discovered.”

But the second speaker asks another question.

“Is it exactly what we discovered?”

The first hesitates.

“We discovered that soil had been carried away. Only yesterday did we discover how the water moved through the gaps.”

“Then she has understood something we did not understand on the first day.”

“Yes.”

“Does that mean she has not understood our earlier discovery?”

“No. She has understood it, and something further.”

How can that be?

If the new understanding contains more, why is it not simply another understanding of another thing?

The visitor takes a small stone from the path.

“Suppose I tell you that this stone is heavy.”

“It is.”

“And tomorrow I discover that it is heavier than another stone of the same size.”

“Then you have learned more about it.”

“Have I ceased to know that it is heavy?”

“No.”

“Has my earlier discovery become a different discovery merely because I now understand a further relation?”

“No.”

“Then perhaps understanding can become richer without every determination within it losing its identity.”

Let us examine that.

The stone remains the stone.

Its weight has not changed merely because another weight has been compared with it.

And the earlier recognition can remain available within the later comparison.

But does this happen with every new discovery?

Suppose the visitor now says, “This stone is hollow.”

The first speaker picks it up.

“How do you know?”

“I thought it sounded hollow when I tapped it.”

They examine the stone and discover that it is solid throughout.

Has the later examination merely added another fact to the earlier understanding?

“No,” says the visitor. “It has shown that I was wrong.”

“Then what happened to your earlier claim?”

“I must withdraw it.”

“Must you withdraw your recognition that the stone made a particular sound?”

“No. I heard the sound. I was mistaken about what caused it.”

Then correction need not destroy everything in the earlier encounter.

It can preserve what was genuinely established while removing an unwarranted determination.

Yes.

And does that not require us to distinguish what was encountered from what was inferred?

It does.

The visitor replaces the stone.

“I see something now. If I merely repeat your words, I may not understand what you meant. But if I discover more, I need not cease to understand what you discovered.”

“Then what makes an understanding faithful?”

The visitor considers the question.

“It must preserve what was actually established.”

“And if that was not yet clearly distinguished?”

“Then we must return to it and find out.”

“Return to the words?”

“Sometimes.”

“To the place?”

“Sometimes.”

“To the question?”

“Perhaps especially to the question.”

Why the question?

“Because it tells us what the earlier answer was trying to establish.”

The first speaker looks toward the boundary.

“On the first day, we asked whether the stone had kept the soil in place.”

“And what did we establish?”

“That some soil had passed beneath it.”

“On the second day, what did we ask?”

“How the water travelled.”

“And today?”

“Whether another person can understand what we found without having been present.”

Have they been asking the same question three times?

No.

Have they been investigating three entirely unrelated things?

No.

Then what connects the inquiries?

The garden, the boundary, the earlier discoveries and the demands those discoveries make available.

But is it enough to say that they all concern the garden?

The visitor shakes her head.

“Someone could investigate the flowers, the insects or the colour of the stones. That would still concern the garden.”

“Then what must remain connected?”

“The particular difference we are trying to understand.”

“And what is that difference?”

“That the boundary was meant to hold the soil, but soil has passed beneath it.”

The second speaker says, “Now I think we have found something.”

“What?”

“We have been speaking of the same garden. But that alone did not make our understandings the same. We had to recognize what was established about it.”

And can this recognition survive different descriptions?

Yes.

Could someone describe the water's movement in another language?

Yes.

Could someone draw it instead of describing it?

Yes.

Could someone discover it without knowing we had discovered it?

Yes.

Would these be the same spoken sentence?

No.

The same drawing?

No.

The same personal experience?

No.

And yet?

They could concern the same determinate relationship.

The visitor looks at the stream of water moving beneath the stones.

“Then what we understand is not necessarily made different every time another person understands it.”

That seems right.

But the first speaker is not satisfied.

“Suppose you draw the water passing over the stones instead of beneath them.”

“Then my drawing would be wrong.”

“Why?”

“Because it would not preserve what we observed.”

“And if you draw the water passing beneath them, but show it moving uphill?”

“That would also be wrong.”

“Then the difference between a faithful account and a mistaken one is not decided merely by whether the account resembles our words.”

“No. It must answer to what we are trying to make intelligible.”

What has become recognizable?

The visitor answers:

“That the same intelligible determination can be approached in different ways, and that the ways can be judged by whether they preserve it.”

The first speaker considers this.

“Then sameness is not merely the repetition of an expression.”

“No.”

“Nor is it the repetition of an experience.”

“No.”

“Nor is it any resemblance we choose to notice.”

“No.”

“What, then, is it?”

The visitor looks again at the water.

“Perhaps it is what remains answerable to the same determination, even when our ways of understanding it change.”

Has she established everything that such sameness requires?

Not yet.

But has she distinguished something important?

Yes.

For an account can change its words and preserve what it makes intelligible.

Another account can retain familiar words while changing what it claims.

And an earlier understanding can be made richer without ceasing to contain what was correctly established.

The second speaker walks toward the far end of the garden.

“Then could someone discover the same thing a hundred years from now?”

“If the relevant relationship can still be established.”

“Even if our words have disappeared?”

“Yes.”

“Even if no one remembers us?”

“Yes.”

“Then what is it that survives?”

The visitor looks toward the first speaker.

“Perhaps we should be careful with that word.”

“Survives?”

“Yes. The stones may disappear. The garden may disappear. Our memories certainly will not last forever. Yet someone could discover the same kind of relationship elsewhere.”

“Would that be the same particular occurrence?”

“No. It would be another occurrence.”

“Could it make the same relationship intelligible?”

“Yes.”

“Then we have another difference to investigate.”

“What difference?”

“Between discovering again the very same intelligible relation and discovering another instance of a similar relation.”

The visitor looks toward the water.

“Is that not what we have been asking all morning?”

“Partly. But now we can ask it more exactly.”

Have they completed their investigation?

They have established that faithful understanding cannot be identified simply with repeated words, shared objects, or identical experiences.

They have also recognized that later discoveries can preserve, enrich or correct what was established before.

But another question has become available.

**What makes the intelligible identity of a relationship independent of the particular occasions in which it is encountered?**

The water continues moving beneath the stones.

No one needs to speak for it to do so.

The three stand looking at the same place.

They are not having one and the same experience.

But they have learned how to examine whether they are understanding the same thing.

Let that be enough for today.


## Chapter XXI — What No Longer Decides

The following morning, the three return to the garden.

A child has placed a small board across the damp ground.

“Don't step there,” the child says. “The soil is too soft.”

The visitor steps back.

“Was the child right?”

The first speaker kneels and presses a hand against the ground.

“Yesterday, certainly. We could scarcely stand here without leaving deep marks.”

“And now?”

“The surface still yields. We should go around.”

They carry the water along the longer path.

Later, after the sun has warmed the garden, the child returns. The board is still there, but the soil beside it is firm.

“Can we cross now?” the visitor asks.

The child removes the board, presses a heel into the ground and crosses without sinking.

“Yesterday you told us not to step there,” the visitor says.

“I know.”

“Were you mistaken?”

The child looks at the dry earth, then at the visitor.

“No. It was wet yesterday.”

“And are you mistaken to cross it now?”

“No. It isn't wet like that now.”

The visitor turns to the others.

“We have heard two directions from the same child: do not cross, and now you may cross. Must one of them be wrong?”

“Only if they were given for the same ground under the same conditions,” says the second speaker.

“So the earlier direction can remain correct?”

“Yes.”

“Yet it no longer tells us what to do?”

“Not today.”

They watch the child carry the board to the edge of the garden.

“I have been calling such a change a correction,” says the visitor. “But that cannot be quite right.”

“What would you correct?”

“Perhaps the earlier instruction.”

“Would you tell the child that yesterday the ground was not soft?”

“No. We saw it ourselves.”

“Would you tell the child it was wrong to warn us?”

“No.”

“Then what has changed?”

“The ground. And with it, what the warning requires of us.”

The first speaker picks up a stone and lays it beside the path.

“Suppose we kept the board there even after the ground had dried, merely because the warning was once needed. What would we be doing?”

“Treating the earlier condition as though it still governed the crossing.”

“And if we threw the board away, declaring that the warning had always been foolish?”

“We would lose the reason for it.”

“Can we do neither?”

“Yes. We can remember why the crossing was unsafe and recognize why that reason no longer decides this crossing.”

The child returns with a handful of seeds.

“May I plant here?”

The first speaker examines the soil.

“Not too close to the stones. The water passed beneath them yesterday.”

The child stops.

“Will it always pass there?”

“That is what we are still learning.”

The visitor smiles.

“So we have not finished every question about this place.”

“No.”

“But we have finished the question of whether we may cross it now?”

“For the conditions before us, yes.”

“And yesterday's warning has not been erased by today's answer?”

“No.”

The visitor is quiet for a while.

“I think I see something that troubled me when we spoke of the same understanding passing from one moment to another. I imagined that remaining faithful meant continuing to obey every earlier conclusion.”

“Can an earlier conclusion remain faithful to its own occasion without commanding every later occasion?”

“Here it can.”

“And how do you know?”

“Because the warning answered to the softness of the ground. That was what made it appropriate. When the ground changed, the warning's reason ceased to decide what we should do. The reason did not cease to have been real.”

The second speaker sets down the jug.

“Now suppose someone had told us yesterday that the ground was soft when it was actually dry. Would today's crossing be the same sort of change?”

“No. We would have discovered that the earlier warning was mistaken.”

“What if yesterday the ground was soft, but today a bridge had been built above it?”

“The old difficulty would still be there beneath the bridge, but it might no longer prevent us from passing.”

“Must the obstacle disappear for the undertaking to be complete?”

“No.”

“And if we call all three cases a correction?”

“We lose the difference between a false judgment, a changed condition, and a way forward that makes the old obstacle no longer decisive.”

The first speaker nods toward the child, who has started arranging seeds in small groups.

“Can this distinction help us understand more than paths?”

The visitor thinks of the untranslatable word they learned in the garden.

“A word may be suitable in one exchange and unsuitable in another, even when the earlier use was good.”

“And a rule?”

“It may be needed for one undertaking but not another.”

“And a discovery?”

“It can remain established while a new question demands more than it established.”

“Then what must we preserve if we want to understand yesterday faithfully?”

“Not merely the instruction. We must preserve what it was answering to.”

“And what must we examine before carrying it into today?”

“Whether those conditions still matter to what we are doing now.”

The child calls out.

“The small seeds should go nearer the fence. The others need more space.”

“Why?” the visitor asks.

“Because those will grow wider.”

“Has the child answered every question about the garden?”

“No.”

“Has the child discovered something that may govern the planting?”

“Yes.”

“Will it govern every planting, in every garden?”

“Not without looking at what is being planted and where.”

The visitor takes up the jug.

“Yesterday's understanding has not vanished. Nor must we surrender today's observation to yesterday's instruction. Perhaps to remain faithful is to know what an achievement was enough for, and what it is enough for now.”

The first speaker looks at the path where the board had been.

“Then we have found something more exact than either repetition or rejection.”

“What?”

“An earlier condition may stop governing without having been false. And what it once made intelligible can remain available to us.”

They cross the path together.

The crossing does not repair yesterday.

It does not make yesterday happen again.

It belongs to today, and yesterday has helped them understand why.
 
## Chapter XXII — What We Can Begin From

The garden is nearly planted.

The child has left a small space unfilled.

“Why not put seeds there?” asks the visitor.

“I don't know whether the water will gather there when it rains again.”

“Then something remains unfinished?”

“The planting does, in that place.”

“And the work already done?”

The child looks across the rows.

“That is finished.”

The visitor turns to the first speaker.

“When we began, I thought that if another question remained, our understanding must not yet have come to rest.”

“Does the unplanted patch undo the planted rows?”

“No.”

“Does a planted row answer the question of what the next rain will do?”

“No.”

“So what have we learned?”

“To let each achievement answer for itself.”

The second speaker has taken a scrap of paper from a pocket. On it is a drawing of the garden from the previous day, with arrows showing the movement of water.

“Will the child understand this drawing?” the visitor asks.

“Ask.”

The child studies the arrows.

“Those show where the water went.”

“Do they show where it must go next time?”

“Not by themselves. It might rain harder. We might move the stones.”

The visitor takes the drawing.

“Then it holds something from yesterday, but does not command tomorrow.”

“It makes yesterday's finding available,” says the second speaker.

“Is that the same as knowing why yesterday's conclusion was justified?”

The child shakes their head.

“I remember the puddle. And we watched the soil move.”

“If you had not been here?”

“I would have to ask how they knew.”

“So the drawing gives you one thing, and the account of how it was observed gives you something else.”

“The result, and the way to check it,” says the first speaker.

“And both may matter when we use it again.”

They place the drawing beside the empty patch.

The visitor asks, “Suppose tomorrow a neighbour arrives. She has never seen the water move and knows nothing of our conversation. Can she begin from what we have learned?”

“Yes, if we can show her what we learned and what it depended upon.”

“Must she have the very same experience?”

“No.”

“Must she believe us merely because we were here?”

“No.”

“What might she do?”

“Look at the stones. Read the drawing. Ask how the water was observed. Wait for another rain, if she needs to.”

“And if she discovers a different cause?”

“We must examine what that changes.”

The visitor looks around the garden.

“The earlier finding may survive. Its explanation may need repair. And the neighbour may discover something none of us saw.”

“Yes.”

“Then perhaps our work is not a chain in which each link merely follows the last.”

“What else is it?”

“A later step takes its possibilities from an earlier achievement. But it must still answer its own question.”

The child raises a hand.

“Does that mean we cannot plant the last seeds?”

“Not yet,” says the first speaker. “It means we have a question worth asking before we do.”

“Then I know what to do,” says the child.

“What?”

“Watch that place when it rains.”

The visitor laughs.

“We began with a question, found an answer, and the answer has made another question possible.”

“Is the first answer therefore incomplete?”

“No.”

“Is the last question already answered?”

“No.”

“And can the discovery of the right question sometimes be an achievement?”

“Yes. We now know what to look for and why.”

They stand by the unplanted patch.

The visitor speaks slowly.

“I once thought clarity was having more answers. Then I thought it was finding one answer that would settle everything. But neither is what I find here.”

“What do you find?”

“That I can say what we know, what made it enough, what we have only supposed, and what we still need to discover. I can see why yesterday's instruction was right then and why it does not decide every choice today.”

“Has the world become simpler?”

“Not necessarily.”

“Have we learned to distinguish it more exactly?”

“Yes.”

The first speaker looks at the rows of seeds.

“Then what do you now understand by clarity?”

“Not the end of all questions. The ability to recognize what is being asked, what has actually been achieved, what continues to hold, and what must be examined next.”

“And can understanding grow in this way without beginning again from nothing?”

“It has done so here.”

“Must every achievement remain in force forever?”

“No. But a change in what governs need not destroy what was rightly established.”

“Must we name every form of achievement before recognizing any of them?”

“No. We began with doors, words, stones, water, and the difficulty of getting something right.”

“Were the distinctions already waiting in those encounters?”

“They became recognizable because we attended to what each encounter required.”

The second speaker folds the paper carefully.

“Someone may come tomorrow and understand this garden differently.”

“Then she may teach us something.”

“Someone may find that we were mistaken.”

“Then we should correct what was mistaken.”

“And someone may ask a question that none of us has yet imagined.”

“Then today's understanding may help make it possible.”

The child has already gone to put away the tools.

“Are you coming?” the child calls.

The visitor looks once more at the unplanted space.

“What shall we tell someone who asks whether we finished?”

“That we finished what we finished,” says the first speaker. “And we know what remains to be done.”

The visitor considers the answer, then smiles.

“That will do.”

They walk away from the garden.

Nothing requires the moment to last.

Nothing requires the completed work to be repeated before it can matter again.

The garden will meet another morning, another rain, perhaps another question.

What has become clear does not have to end there.

It gives them somewhere to begin.

## Chapter XXIII — Before Anyone Said It

The rain comes in the night.

By morning the garden path is scattered with leaves. A shallow channel has formed beside the stones, and the unplanted patch is filled with water.

The child arrives first.

“I knew it,” the child says.

The visitor, coming behind, takes a look.

“What did you know?”

“That the water would gather there.”

“You knew it would happen,” says the visitor, “or you knew that we ought to watch?”

The child scowls a little. “I thought it would happen.”

“And now?”

“Now I can see it.”

The first speaker arrives with a spade. She stands at the edge of the water and sees the channel running from the higher ground to the unplanted patch.

“No one was here when the rain began,” she says.

“Not even the child?” asks the visitor.

“I was asleep,” the child answers. “But the puddle came anyway.”

“Then perhaps we should ask something,” the visitor says. “When did the water find its way down here?”

“During the night.”

“When did we discover the way it took?”

“This morning.”

“Are those the same event?”

“No.”

The visitor kneels to examine the channel. There are places where the water has carried fine soil, leaving the larger stones exposed. A small twig has lodged against one of them.

“How can we tell which way the water went?”

The child points from the higher ground to the lower patch.

“It came from there. It left the little stones behind and brought the loose soil here.”

“Is that all we know?”

The first speaker shakes her head. “We can see where soil was displaced. We have good reason to think the water came along this channel. We did not watch every drop.”

The child looks up. “But you can still know something about it.”

“Yes,” says the speaker. “We can know what the marks are enough to tell us, and leave aside what they do not tell us.”

The visitor lifts the twig.

“Suppose the channel had formed in a part of the garden no one ever visited. Would the water have needed us in order to run down the slope?”

“No.”

“And if a person came a year later and found the marks?”

“They might work out what happened,” says the child.

“Would their discovery make the water run at that earlier time?”

The child laughs. “It would be rather late for that.”

The visitor smiles, but does not let the matter rest.

“Then we must distinguish the event, the traces it leaves, and the understanding someone may later achieve.”

“We have distinguished those before,” says the first speaker.

“We have. But there is something further here. We speak as though a relationship could be understood long after the occurrence from which we learn of it.”

The second speaker, who has been watching the little pool, answers.

“Sometimes it can. But we must not pretend every explanation found afterward was already waiting in the stones.”

“What do you mean?”

“We can say the ground sloped, the rain fell, and water moved downhill. The movement did not wait for our account. But the words we use to describe it, the questions we choose, and the explanation we put together are our work.”

“Does that make the movement depend upon the explanation?”

“No. Nor does the movement tell us everything we might wish to explain.”

The child begins clearing leaves from the lower end of the channel.

“Wait,” says the first speaker. “What are you trying to do?”

“Let the puddle drain.”

“Then you have begun a new undertaking. Has the investigation of the water's earlier movement finished?”

“Enough to know where to make a way out,” says the child.

“Is the way out made?”

“Not yet.”

The visitor points to the child's muddy hands.

“A moment ago you were looking at traces. Now you are trying to change the ground. Did the earlier discovery itself drain the water?”

“No.”

“Did it help you choose where to work?”

“Yes.”

“So what was achieved this morning?”

The child pauses. “We found where the water went. And now we know where it might go if we clear the leaves.”

“Those are two things,” says the visitor.

“And neither is the rain itself,” adds the second speaker.

The child looks from one adult to another. “Are we going to talk about it all day, or can I move the leaves?”

“You can move the leaves,” says the first speaker, and picks up the spade.

They work for a while.

The water begins to trickle into a shallow bed beyond the stones. The puddle falls, slowly enough that they can watch the wet line withdraw.

The visitor stands.

“What we discovered was not produced by the words we found for it,” she says. “Yet without understanding what mattered in the marks, we might have dug in the wrong place.”

“And without the words?” asks the first speaker.

“We might still have noticed and acted. But the words have let us show the child what we noticed, ask whether we were right, and explain it to someone absent.”

The second speaker takes up the twig and sets it beside the channel.

“Then let us keep the distinction. Something may happen without being understood. Something may be understood without being spoken. And something spoken may still be mistaken.”

“Yes.”

“Does the same follow for every thing we might call intelligible?”

The visitor considers the question.

“Not all at once. We have examined an ordinary relationship in the ground. We should not claim to have explained all mathematics or all human understanding because water flows downhill.”

“Then what have we earned?”

“That we can distinguish the occurrence of a relationship from the occasion on which someone recognizes it. Our recognition may be later, but it can still answer to what happened.”

The child, satisfied that the puddle is draining, plants the first seed in the formerly empty patch.

“Now there is something else,” the child says.

“What?”

“We know what to do here.”

The first speaker looks at the dark soil.

“And when someone asks how we found out?”

“We can tell them about the rain,” says the child. “Even though we were asleep when it came.”

The visitor looks toward the open path.

“We have been speaking of what can be understood again. Now we have reached something that does not need to have been understood the first time in order to become understandable afterward.”

“And that,” says the second speaker, “raises a more difficult question.”

“What question?”

“How shall we tell when different explanations preserve the very relationship they claim to describe?”

The child presses soil gently over the seed.

“One question at a time,” the child says.

This time no one objects.

## Chapter XXIV — The Corner That Stayed Right

The carpenter is repairing a small table beside the shelter.

One leg has become loose, and the table rocks when a cup is placed upon it.

The child brings the carpenter a piece of wood cut into a triangle.

“This will keep it from wobbling,” the child says.

“Where shall it go?” asks the carpenter.

“Between the leg and the underside of the table.”

The carpenter holds the piece against the corner. It does not fit. One angle is too wide.

The visitor, who has come from the garden, watches the child turn the wood over.

“Does turning it change its angles?”

“No,” the child says. “I was hoping it would fit the other way.”

The carpenter places a square against the table leg.

“Look. This is the corner we must fit.”

The child compares the piece of wood with the square.

“So this piece is wrong.”

“For this corner,” says the carpenter.

The first speaker arrives carrying two lengths of string.

“Before you cut another piece, may we try something?”

She lays the strings along the two edges of the carpenter's square and marks their lengths. The carpenter draws a right-angled triangle on a scrap of board.

“Now suppose we know the lengths of the two sides that meet at the corner,” she says. “Could we find how long the side across from that corner must be?”

The child measures the drawing.

“By measuring it.”

“Yes. But what if the triangle is not drawn yet, or is much too large to measure?”

“Then I wouldn't know.”

The visitor asks, “Could there be a relationship between the lengths, whether or not we have drawn that particular triangle?”

The carpenter takes a piece of chalk and makes three squares along the sides of the drawing.

The child counts the little units in the squares.

“The two smaller ones together have the same area as the big one.”

“Only in this drawing?” asks the visitor.

“I don't know.”

“Good,” says the carpenter. “Don't answer before looking.”

They draw another right-angled triangle, with different side lengths, and compare the squares again.

The child takes longer this time. One of the squares crosses the edge of the board. They fetch another piece and continue.

“I can see the same relationship,” the child says at last.

“Does the second triangle have the same lengths as the first?” asks the visitor.

“No.”

“Does it have the same appearance?”

“Not really.”

“What is the same?”

“The way the square on the long side is related to the other two.”

The carpenter nods.

“And if we built a triangle with sides that did not meet at a right angle?”

The child studies the square again.

“Then I couldn't just use what we found without checking.”

“Why not?”

“Because the right corner mattered.”

The visitor turns to the first speaker.

“We have spoken before of this mathematical relationship. But now we have encountered the difference between remembering its words and knowing what lets us use it.”

The first speaker takes the chalk.

“Suppose I explain the relationship to you without drawing any squares. And suppose the carpenter shows you a different proof with pieces of wood. Must our explanations be identical?”

“No,” says the child.

“Could one explanation be mistaken?”

“Yes. It might measure badly, or use a triangle that isn't right-angled.”

“Then how would we discover the mistake?”

“By checking what it was trying to establish.”

The visitor asks the child another question.

“Imagine someone who speaks a language you do not know. They draw the same kinds of triangles and show you the areas. You understand what the drawing demonstrates. Have you learned their language?”

“No.”

“Have you understood something through their drawing?”

“Yes.”

“Would the relationship begin to be true only when you found words for it in your language?”

The child is busy comparing the two drawings.

“No. The squares would still fit.”

The carpenter interjects.

“Careful. We do not want to say that every rough wooden square gives a perfect demonstration. The pieces may be cut badly.”

“But a badly cut piece can be wrong about the shape,” the child says, “not make the relationship wrong.”

“That is the distinction,” says the carpenter. “A drawing helps us see. The relationship tells us what a correct drawing or proof must answer to.”

The visitor looks toward the table.

“So we have something more exact than an ancient sentence handed from one person to another.”

“What?” asks the first speaker.

“A relationship that different people can try to establish, express, test, and use. Their acts of understanding differ. Their diagrams differ. But they need not discover different relationships.”

“Does that mean every comparison of two examples reveals an invariant?”

“No. We had to identify what remained: the relation among the areas, under the right-angle condition. Merely saying the triangles look alike would not have been enough.”

The child interrupts.

“Are we going to fix the table?”

The carpenter laughs. “That is still our undertaking.”

He measures the corner again, marks a new piece of wood, and cuts it. This time the triangular brace fits closely. He fastens it to the leg, presses a hand against the tabletop, and then sets the cup down.

The table stands without rocking.

“Did the drawing repair the table?” asks the visitor.

“No,” says the child. “We had to cut and put the piece in.”

“Then was the drawing useless?”

“No. We knew what length would fit before we cut it.”

The first speaker wipes chalk from her fingers.

“The mathematical achievement and the practical repair are not the same completion. One can help bring about the other.”

The carpenter tests the table once more.

“And if we had cut the piece wrongly?”

“The relationship would not be to blame,” says the child. “Our work would need correction.”

The visitor looks at the two triangles on the scrap board.

“Today we have learned something about how an achievement crosses between people. It is not enough that one repeats a statement, nor that two drawings have the same subject. We must be able to recognize what relationship is claimed and what would make an attempted demonstration faithful or mistaken.”

The second speaker joins them.

“Does that mean the world has given us one expression that all people must use?”

“No.”

“One history they must repeat?”

“No.”

“One method they must always prefer?”

“No.”

“Then what have they in common?”

The child puts a finger on the square drawn against the longest side.

“Something they can all be right or wrong about.”

No one answers immediately.

The table is steady. The chalk lines remain on the board. The child takes the cup away without spilling it.

The visitor says quietly, “If people can be answerable to the same relationship without sharing its first expression, perhaps they can also share a question before they share an answer.”

“Then let us find a case,” says the first speaker, “in which they do not agree.”

## Chapter XXV — The Crossing We Disagreed About

The wooden footbridge near the garden has been damaged by rain.

A plank has lifted at one end. Its fastening still holds, but the board bends when the carpenter presses on it.

Two neighbours have stopped beside the bridge.

“We can cross,” says the first. “It held me yesterday.”

“No one should cross until it is fixed,” says the second.

The visitor, approaching with the child, hears the disagreement.

“What are you arguing about?” she asks.

“Whether the bridge is safe,” says the first neighbour.

“Whether we ought to let children use it,” says the second.

The visitor looks from one to the other.

“Those may be different questions.”

“They are the same bridge,” says the first neighbour.

“Yes,” says the visitor. “But are you asking exactly the same thing of it?”

The first neighbour tries again.

“It carried my weight yesterday. I believe it can carry someone across today.”

The second points to the lifted plank.

“I am saying we should not send the children onto it unless we know it will hold them.”

“So you disagree,” says the visitor. “But perhaps not yet in the same respect.”

The carpenter, who has followed them from the shelter, squats beside the plank.

“Yesterday's crossing tells us something,” he says. “It tells us the bridge carried one person under yesterday's conditions. It does not tell us everything we need to know about this fastening after the rain.”

The first neighbour frowns.

“So my experience counts for nothing?”

“No,” says the carpenter. “It counts for what it established.”

The second neighbour is still impatient.

“And while you discuss it, someone may fall.”

“Then keep everyone off the bridge for now,” the carpenter says. “We can examine it without crossing.”

The child helps lay a branch across the entrance as a warning.

The first neighbour watches the work.

“I thought you were saying the bridge had already failed.”

“I wasn't,” says the second. “I was saying we don't know enough to let the children use it.”

“And I thought yesterday's success was enough.”

“It was enough to show you crossed yesterday,” the visitor says. “Now we need to ask what would be enough to establish the bridge's condition today.”

The first neighbour comes closer.

“What do you need to see?”

The carpenter shows them the loose fastening and the cracked timber beneath it.

“Look here. We can find out whether the plank is still supported, and whether the part holding the fastening has split.”

They examine it together. The first neighbour notices a second crack that was hidden by a strip of mud.

“I did not see that yesterday.”

“Perhaps it was hidden,” says the carpenter. “Perhaps it has widened since. We don't yet know which.”

The second neighbour points beneath the plank.

“Would that be enough to tell us not to cross?”

“It is enough to stop us treating the bridge as ready for use,” says the carpenter. “Whether the whole plank must be replaced is another matter.”

The visitor asks, “Have the neighbours reached agreement?”

“About not crossing for now,” says the child.

“Has their agreement repaired the bridge?”

“No.”

“Have they discovered everything about the damage?”

“No.”

“But have they found something they can investigate together?”

The first neighbour answers before the child can.

“Yes. We can look at the same fastening and ask whether it will hold.”

The second neighbour adds, “And we can disagree about what the answer is without pretending the fastening means something entirely different to each of us.”

The visitor nods.

“Then shared understanding is not the same as agreeing.”

“Nor is agreement the same as being right,” says the carpenter.

They carry tools from the shelter. The carpenter loosens the plank, exposing the timber beneath.

The first neighbour holds the board steady while the second removes a bent nail.

A new support must be cut and fitted. They measure it twice. The first measurement is wrong because the ruler's end has broken away.

“Now that,” says the first neighbour, “was a mistake.”

“What was?” asks the child.

“Our measurement. We treated the broken end of the ruler as though it marked the beginning.”

“And yesterday's crossing?” asks the visitor.

“Not a mistake. I really crossed. I mistook what that success entitled me to say about today.”

The carpenter sets aside the ruler.

“That's worth remembering.”

After the new support is fitted, the carpenter tests it carefully. The first neighbour asks to cross; the carpenter makes them wait while he checks the other side.

When the examination is complete, the barrier is moved aside.

The second neighbour crosses first, slowly, and comes back.

“Well?” says the first.

“It holds under the test we made. I would let the children cross now.”

The visitor smiles.

“Has the second neighbour changed her mind?”

“Yes.”

“Was her earlier refusal wrong?”

“No,” says the first neighbour. “Not when the fastening was cracked and we hadn't checked it.”

The child steps onto the bridge, then stops halfway.

“Why are you smiling?”

“Because you understand what changed,” says the visitor.

“I understand that we fixed it. And that they were arguing about different things at first.”

“Can you say what brought them together?”

The child thinks.

“They both wanted the bridge to work. But they had to look at it and find what was wrong.”

“Would wanting the same thing have been enough?”

“No.”

“Would using the same words have been enough?”

“No. They said safe and meant different things by it.”

“So what did they come to share?”

The child taps the plank with a foot.

“What would count as finding out.”

The visitor turns toward the neighbours.

“A shared world is not a world in which everybody sees the same things, believes the same things, or has the same past. These two began from different experiences and different questions. They were able to make their disagreement more exact because the bridge could answer to an inquiry neither could settle by assertion alone.”

The first neighbour looks over the side.

“The bridge did not vote for either of us.”

The second laughs. “And I hope it never will.”

They walk across together.

At the far end the child looks back.

“Someone built this bridge before I was born,” the child says. “I didn't have to learn how to build a whole bridge before I could cross it.”

The carpenter looks at the new support.

“No. But when it failed, we needed to find out what could still be trusted, and what needed doing again.”

The visitor remains beside the bridge.

“Then there is a further question.”

“About the bridge?” asks the child.

“About all the things we use without having discovered them ourselves. A path someone cleared. A rule someone taught. A measurement someone established. A way of building that outlives its builder.”

“Can't we just use them?” asks the child.

“Often we can.”

“And when we can't?”

“We need to know what was established, what still holds, and how to examine it.”

The child glances toward the garden.

“Then someone else has already begun our next question.”

The visitor looks at the bridge, the repaired plank, and the people crossing it.

“Yes,” she says. “Many people have.”


## Chapter XXVI — What Nobody Has to Discover Again

The next day, the child returns to the bridge with a folded piece of paper.

“Look what I found,” the child says.

It is a drawing, somewhat faded, of the bridge as it was first built. There are numbers beside the planks and lines showing where the supports belong.

“Where did you find it?” asks the carpenter.

“In the box of old tools. Someone put it beneath the lid.”

The carpenter unfolds the drawing across a dry board.

“I remember this,” he says. “The person who built the bridge made it.”

“Are all the numbers right?” asks the child.

“They tell us what was planned. We still have to see whether the bridge was built that way and whether the parts have changed.”

“Then why keep it?”

“It may help us find out.”

The visitor comes closer.

“Suppose the builder had left no drawing. Would we know nothing about how the bridge stands?”

“We could examine it,” says the carpenter. “We could measure its supports and work out what they do.”

“Would we be making the original bridge again?”

“No. We would be discovering how this one was made.”

“And if we followed the drawing without examining the bridge?”

“We might trust a support that has rotted away.”

The child points to a line at the end of the page.

“What does that say?”

The carpenter reads it aloud. It explains why the outer support was set beyond the bank rather than directly above the water.

“I didn't know that,” says the child.

“Neither did I,” says the carpenter. “I had noticed where the support stood, but not why the builder chose that place.”

The visitor considers the drawing.

“Has the person who made it returned to explain?”

“No.”

“Yet we have understood something of what that person understood?”

“Yes.”

“Is the paper itself the understanding?”

The child shakes their head.

“It's only a drawing.”

“Then why is it so useful?”

“Because we can see what the marks mean. And ask whether they are right.”

The carpenter shows the child how the measurements belong together. One gives a length. Another shows the distance between supports. The line at the riverbank gives the reason for a placement that had at first seemed strange.

For a while they say nothing. The child traces the marks with one finger, then compares them with the bridge.

“Here,” the child says. “The drawing shows another support underneath, but I can't see it.”

“Come around the bank,” says the carpenter.

They crouch beside the water. A broad beam stands partly hidden by grass.

“It is there,” says the child.

“Does the drawing tell us whether it is still sound?” asks the visitor.

“No. We have to look.”

The carpenter tests the beam. Its surface is weathered, but the wood beneath is firm.

The child stands up.

“So the drawing lets us start with something someone else already learned. But it doesn't do all our work.”

“That seems right,” says the carpenter.

The second speaker has joined them.

“Would you say that we have inherited the bridge?”

“Of course,” says the child.

“And the drawing?”

“Yes.”

“And the builder's understanding?”

The child hesitates.

“Some of it, maybe. We don't know everything the builder knew.”

“What makes you say *some of it*?”

“We know why that support was placed there. But we don't know why every other thing was done.”

The visitor smiles.

“Then inheritance is not simply having an old object in our hands.”

“No,” says the child. “We have to find out what it tells us.”

The carpenter folds the drawing again.

“Sometimes we can use a thing without knowing how it was discovered. I crossed this bridge for years without seeing the drawing.”

“And today?”

“Today I can explain one choice in its design. If that support ever needs replacing, the explanation may matter.”

The visitor asks, “Which matters more: the bridge, the drawing, or the explanation?”

The carpenter looks at the water.

“I would not answer without knowing what we are trying to do. If we need to cross, the bridge must hold. If we need to repair it, the drawing may guide us. If we need to decide whether the drawing is trustworthy, we must examine what was actually built and how it works.”

“Then one thing cannot do every other thing's work.”

“No.”

They carry the paper back to the shelter. An older neighbour is mending a net beside the entrance.

“Do you remember the person who made the bridge?” asks the child.

“Yes.”

“Did you help?”

“I brought some of the stones. I remember the builder sending us back for larger ones.”

“Why larger?”

“The bank was softer than expected. The first stones sank.”

The carpenter unfolds the drawing.

“Here is the outer support. This note explains why it stands beyond the bank.”

The neighbour studies the page and nods.

“That was after the stones sank. The first drawing would have looked different.”

The visitor looks up.

“Then the page records a choice made after another attempt failed.”

“Yes,” says the neighbour. “We learned something from the ground.”

“And was the failed attempt of no use?”

“It wasn't a sound foundation. But it taught us what would not hold.”

The child takes this seriously.

“So they did not know everything before they began.”

“Not at all,” says the neighbour.

“But we don't have to try the small stones again just to know what happened?”

“We need not repeat that failure. We can examine why it failed.”

“And if the bank has changed?”

“Then we must examine that too.”

The visitor puts a hand on the drawing.

“Here is a curious thing. The builder's work did not finish the work of all later builders. Yet it made something available to them. The bridge can be used. The drawing can be read. The old decision can be examined. The account of the failed stones may prevent someone from repeating a mistake.”

“Unless they don't listen,” says the child.

“Yes,” says the neighbour, laughing. “That can happen.”

The first speaker, who has been quiet, asks, “Does the understanding become less real because someone ignores it?”

“Not necessarily,” says the visitor. “But it may cease to guide what they do.”

“Then what do we mean when we say that an achievement remains available?”

“We mean more than that someone once thought it. We can encounter its result, recover its relation to the undertaking, or use what it has made possible. Sometimes the result survives when the explanation is lost. Sometimes the explanation survives when the original object is gone.”

The child looks toward the bridge.

“If the bridge fell down tomorrow, would the drawing be useless?”

“It might help build another,” says the carpenter. “Or help us learn why this one fell. But not unless we understood what it showed.”

The older neighbour ties off the net.

“And if the drawing were lost?”

“We could still examine the bridge,” says the child. “And we could ask you.”

“For now,” says the neighbour.

The child looks puzzled.

“Why for now?”

“Because I will not always be here.”

No one speaks for a moment.

The visitor asks the neighbour, “What would you want the child to keep from this conversation?”

“Not just that we used larger stones. I would want the child to remember why.”

“And if the child discovers a better way?”

“Then I hope the child uses it.”

The visitor turns to the child.

“Do you see? What was done before you were born can help you, even though you cannot return to the moment when it was done. And what you do may help someone who is not here yet.”

The child looks at the old drawing, then takes a fresh sheet of paper.

“I'll draw where we repaired the plank.”

“Why?” asks the carpenter.

“Because whoever comes next might need to know.”

The child starts drawing, stops, and adds the crack beside the fastening.

“Should I draw the part that broke too?”

“Yes,” says the carpenter. “Otherwise someone might not understand why we replaced it.”

The visitor watches the child at work.

“A bridge passes from one generation to another. So do words, methods, tools, and discoveries. But their passing is not enough by itself. Someone must still be able to recognize what was achieved and where it applies.”

The child finishes the drawing and places it beside the older one.

“Now there are two.”

“Will the next person have to read both?”

“Perhaps, if they want to understand what changed.”

“And if they only need to cross?”

“They can cross, if the bridge is safe.”

The visitor looks at the two pages.

“We have been trying to understand how a completed achievement can become the beginning of another. Here it has happened across lives that do not overlap in every moment.”

The older neighbour rises to put away the net.

“What is so surprising about that?” the neighbour asks.

The visitor considers.

“Perhaps the surprising thing is how ordinary it is.”

The neighbour smiles.

“Then perhaps you are beginning to understand what a village is.”

## Chapter XXVII — The World We Can Understand

In the morning the visitor walks alone to the garden.

The bridge is quiet. The path is dry. Beyond the stones, the rows have begun to show small green shoots.

At the far end, the child is trying to open the gate.

The gate catches against the ground. The child pushes once, then stops, kneels, and looks beneath it.

The visitor waits.

The child picks a small stone out of the way. The gate opens.

“You didn't push harder,” says the visitor.

“No. It was catching on the stone.”

The visitor smiles.

“A door again.”

“What?”

“When we began, we asked what it meant for a door to close. Now you have opened a gate.”

The child shrugs.

“It was only a stone.”

“Only a stone,” the visitor repeats.

The first speaker comes along the path carrying the old drawing of the garden.

“Are you speaking about stones again?”

“We seem unable to escape them,” says the visitor.

“Perhaps because we keep leaving them in useful places,” says the child.

They sit beside the gate. The second speaker arrives with a bowl of water for the young plants.

The visitor asks, “Have we ever found the answer to our first question?”

“Which first question?” asks the second speaker.

“What has happened when something becomes clear?”

The child points at the gate.

“I found the stone. I moved it. The gate opened.”

“And if you had not found it?”

“I might have pushed until something broke.”

“Did discovering the stone and opening the gate happen as one thing?”

“Not quite. First I understood what was stopping it.”

“And that understanding made the next action possible.”

“Yes.”

The first speaker sets the drawing on the bench.

“Could you have understood what stopped the gate without saying anything?”

“I did.”

“Could the visitor have understood it if you showed the stone?”

“Yes.”

“And if another child came tomorrow, unable to speak our language?”

The child picks up the stone and moves it back toward the gate, then takes it away again.

“I could show them.”

The visitor watches the movement.

“Then we have come a long way and returned to something very near where we began.”

The second speaker pours water slowly beside a row of plants.

“Not quite the same place,” she says.

“Why not?”

“Because now we understand what made some of our earlier questions worth asking. We know the difference between what happened and what was understood about it. We know an answer can be sufficient without being the last answer. And we know what was achieved can become available again.”

The child watches the water soak into the earth.

“We also know not to put too much water there.”

“Because of the rain?” asks the visitor.

“Because we watched where it collected. And we changed the ground.”

The first speaker points to the drawing.

“Could someone understand what we discovered if they had never seen the rain?”

“Yes. If we showed them what happened and what we learned from it.”

“Could they understand it in different words?”

“Yes.”

“Could they find that part of our explanation was wrong?”

“Yes.”

“And if they did?”

“We would have to examine what we got wrong, not pretend we never learned anything.”

The visitor looks out toward the bridge.

“Yesterday two neighbours disagreed about whether the bridge could be used. Did their disagreement mean that they inhabited different worlds?”

The child laughs.

“They were both standing beside the same bridge.”

“But they did not think the same thing.”

“No.”

“What made it possible for them to find out together?”

“They looked at the fastening.”

“And after that?”

“They fixed what needed fixing. Then they checked it.”

The second speaker sets down the bowl.

“Could they have disagreed even after examining it?”

“Of course,” says the visitor. “They might have read the evidence differently or wanted different standards of safety.”

“Then what would remain between them?”

“A question they could try to make more exact. Reasons they could examine. Conditions to which their judgments must answer.”

“Would that guarantee agreement?”

“No.”

“Would it make disagreement meaningless?”

“No. It would make it possible to discover what the disagreement was about.”

The child has gone to gather the tools.

The first speaker watches.

“I wonder whether we have been asking the wrong question about what binds people.”

“What question have we been asking?” says the visitor.

“What must people possess in common before they can understand one another?”

“Is that wrong?”

“Not wrong. But perhaps it sends us first to their words, customs, and beliefs. Suppose two people share none of those completely. Must they be unable to discover anything together?”

The visitor thinks of the islanders who once had no common language.

“They can point, show, try, fail, correct, and begin to recognize what matters to a shared undertaking.”

“Do they have to become the same person?”

“No.”

“Have the same experience?”

“No.”

“Agree about everything?”

“No.”

“Then what must be possible?”

The visitor answers carefully.

“Something must be able to become intelligible between them. A distinction one recognizes must be available, somehow, for another to recognize or examine. A question must have conditions by which an answer could be enough. An achievement must be capable of being taken up without the first moment happening again.”

The second speaker asks, “Is this what binds them more deeply than their language?”

“In one respect, perhaps. Language can help them share understanding. But the possibility of understanding something together cannot be explained solely by their already possessing the same words.”

“Does that make language unimportant?”

“Not at all. Think of what we could not have done without it: ask why, explain a mistake, tell someone absent, preserve a discovery. Our language is one of the great things we have made from the possibility of understanding.”

“And a village?” asks the first speaker.

“The village depends on more than understanding. People must care for one another, work, disagree, make promises, and keep them. But without something that can become intelligible between them, how would they know what they were promising, or recognize that a task had been done?”

The child returns, dragging a small sack of soil.

“Are you going to help me, or are you going to understand the garden until nightfall?”

They help.

The sack is heavier than it looks. One person holds it while another loosens the cord. The child tips it and spills soil beside the new plants.

“That was too much,” says the child.

“Can we put some back?” asks the first speaker.

They do.

For a while there is no further discussion.

When the last of the soil has been spread, the visitor sits on the edge of the path.

“We have been speaking of things that can be understood before anyone gives them a name. We have seen a relationship discovered through geometry, a bridge tested through disagreement, and a builder's decision made available long after the builder was gone.”

The second speaker joins her.

“Do those all concern the same subject?”

“No. A gate, a triangle, a bridge, and a village are different things.”

“Then why have they belonged to one inquiry?”

“Because each has allowed us to ask what became clear, what made it sufficient, and what remained available for someone else.”

The first speaker says, “And have they all been completed in exactly the same way?”

“No. Sometimes one discovery is decisive. Sometimes a whole arrangement must hold. Sometimes a former obstacle ceases to determine the outcome. What remains can be a recognized result, a relationship on which that result depends, or a capacity that has become available.”

“Can we now claim to have counted every way in which anything can become sufficiently understood?”

The visitor shakes her head.

“No. We have learned how to look for such differences. The counting requires another kind of patient examination.”

“Then have we failed to finish our inquiry?”

“Not this undertaking.”

“What have we finished?”

“We have found a way to recognize why completed understanding matters beyond any single moment. An answer can end one question without ending our ability to ask. What is understood can become available to another person. That person may preserve it, question it, correct it, or discover something further.”

The child sits down beside them.

“Does that mean I will know how to open every gate?”

“No,” says the visitor.

“Then what have I got?”

“You know what stopped this one. And you know to look before you push harder.”

The child considers that.

“That seems useful.”

“It is more than useful,” says the first speaker. “It gives you something from which another understanding can begin.”

The visitor looks toward the bridge, where someone she does not recognize is crossing.

“Who made the first path through this place?” she asks.

“No one here remembers,” says the second speaker.

“And who will walk it after us?”

“We do not know.”

“Yet the path can be followed. The bridge can be examined. The garden can be tended. What someone has discovered can become available to someone who has never met them.”

“Is that what you mean by a common world?” asks the first speaker.

“Partly. Not that we all see it from the same place. Not that we always understand it correctly. But that there is something we can meet, examine, learn from, and make more intelligible together.”

“And if a person arrives tomorrow and asks a question we have never thought of?”

The visitor watches the child carry the empty sack toward the shelter.

“Then perhaps something we have understood will help us hear the question.”

The child calls from the gate.

“Are you coming?”

“Yes,” says the visitor.

The child holds the gate open.

Nothing more needs explaining before they can pass through it.

The visitor goes first, then the others.

Behind them the garden remains, changed by their work and open to further change.

Ahead of them there are things they do not yet understand.

They go together.
