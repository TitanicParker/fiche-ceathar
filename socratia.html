SOCRATIA

A Socratic journey into the heart of intelligibility

Book I — Before the Word

Chapter I — The Door

A door closes.

A child standing nearby turns at the sound. A moment later, another person enters the room and sees the door shut.

“What happened?” asks the visitor.

“The door closed,” says the child.

The person who has just entered looks puzzled. “How do you know? I only found it closed.”

The visitor looks from one to the other.

“Do you know different things?”

“I saw it move,” says the child.

“And I know how it is now,” says the newcomer. “Someone might have shut it earlier.”

“So the movement and the condition it leaves are not the same,” says the visitor.

“Of course not,” says the child. “One happens. The other is what you find afterward.”

They stand for a moment before the door.

“Must the child have said anything for the closing to happen?” asks the visitor.

“No.”

“And must the newcomer have witnessed the movement to recognize the condition?”

“No,” says the newcomer. “But I would have been wrong to say I saw it close.”

“Would that sentence be difficult to understand?”

“Not at all. You'd know exactly what I claimed. It simply wouldn't be something I could honestly claim to have seen.”

The child opens the door again.

“Now you can both watch.”

It closes once more, this time gently.

“Did we make yesterday's closing happen again?” asks the visitor.

“No,” says the child. “That was another time.”

“Yet we can recognize the kind of difference again.”

The newcomer nods. “I understand what you meant before. You were telling me about an occurrence, and I was telling you about the condition I found.”

The visitor looks at the door.

“A small distinction. But consider what it allows us to notice. There is what happens. There is what remains the case afterward. There is what one person witnesses, what another discovers later, and what either can say about it. We must not confuse them merely because they concern one door.”

The child has begun to leave.

“Do we need a special word for every part of it?” the visitor asks.

“We already have words,” the child says. “But I knew it closed before you asked me.”

And the child goes out.

The visitor follows, carrying a question that the door itself did not ask:

What can be recognized before it has been put into words?

Chapter II — Before a Common Language

Along a path, two travellers come upon a fallen tree.

They have never met. They speak different languages.

One traveller points toward the tree and makes a sound. The other looks beyond it, then steps towards the far end of the path.

The first traveller hurries forward and waves an arm.

The second stops, uncertain.

“What has gone wrong?” asks the visitor, who has been watching them.

“Perhaps the second traveller thought the first was pointing out the way ahead,” says the child.

“Was the first traveller doing that?”

“Perhaps not. The tree is in the way.”

“Then the gesture has not been enough to make its purpose clear.”

The first traveller bends, touches the trunk, and tries to lift it. It does not move. Then the traveller points towards a narrow track around the trees.

The second traveller watches, looks at the trunk, and tries the new track. After a few steps, both travellers nod.

“They've understood,” says the child.

“What have they understood?”

“That the path is blocked, and there may be another way.”

“Have they learned one another's languages?”

“No.”

“Do we know they understand every detail in the same way?”

The child considers this.

“No. One may only want to get past, while the other wants to show why they cannot go straight ahead.”

“Yet something has become sufficient for what they are doing.”

They follow the track until it joins the main path again.

The visitor asks, “What made the second gesture better than the first?”

“It helped the other traveller see what mattered.”

“And how did the first traveller know there was something wrong with the first attempt?”

“The other one started walking the wrong way.”

“So a misunderstanding could be recognized before the travellers had agreed on words for describing it.”

The child watches the travellers disappear.

“Maybe pointing and gesturing are a kind of language.”

“They may be. Calling them that does not settle the matter we are asking about.”

“What matter?”

“What allows an expression, whether spoken or gestured, to be suitable or unsuitable for what someone is trying to make clear?”

The child points towards the fallen tree.

“The tree is still there.”

“Yes.”

“And it would still be in the way even if nobody understood the pointing.”

The visitor smiles.

“Then the obstacle does not depend upon their agreement. And their communication succeeds only as it comes to answer to something about the situation and their undertaking.”

They continue along the path, where the two travellers' footprints meet again in the mud.

No shared dictionary has been made.

But a way forward has been found.

Chapter III — The Translation That Failed

Later, the visitor listens to two people trying to pass a message between languages.

One says, “The storehouse door is closed.”

The interpreter turns to a third person and says something in another language.

The third person looks alarmed.

“What did you tell her?” asks the visitor.

“That the door closed.”

The first speaker shakes her head.

“That isn't quite what I said.”

“But the door is closed,” says the interpreter. “So it must have closed.”

“Perhaps. But I didn't say when it happened, or claim to have seen it. I told you its condition.”

The interpreter thinks for a moment.

“Then you could have found it closed when you arrived.”

“Yes.”

“And by saying it closed, I made it sound as though you were telling us about an event.”

“Exactly.”

The interpreter offers a different sentence. The third person relaxes and asks whether anyone has the key.

“Now she wants to know how to get in,” says the visitor.

“Which is another question,” says the first speaker.

The interpreter smiles ruefully. “I thought I had translated correctly because every word I used was ordinary and the sentence made sense.”

“Could your first sentence have been true?” asks the visitor.

“Yes.”

“Could it have been perfectly understandable?”

“Yes.”

“Then what made it a poor translation?”

“It did not preserve the difference the first speaker was making.”

The visitor looks at the two speakers.

“They do not need identical words or identical grammar. But there must be something by which they can tell whether the message has arrived faithfully.”

The interpreter pauses.

“And even after a faithful translation, the listener may have another question.”

“Certainly.”

“Then translating the statement and answering the next question are not one task.”

The first speaker opens the storehouse door and invites them inside.

The interpreter laughs.

“Now I can translate that as ‘the door opened,’ because I saw it happen.”

The visitor does not laugh at once.

“What is remarkable,” she says, “is that the mistake was recognizable. There was something to preserve, and something that could be lost, even while every sentence remained intelligible.”

The interpreter looks back at the door.

“Then perhaps I need to understand more than the words before I can translate them well.”

“Perhaps,” says the first speaker. “And perhaps understanding more clearly is what lets you notice when the words have failed.”

Chapter IV — The Relation That Did Not Age

The carpenter draws a right-angled triangle on a piece of board.

The visitor has asked why a measurement made from the drawing can guide work that has not yet been done.

“Here is the right angle,” says the carpenter. “Suppose we know the lengths of the two sides that meet there. A particular relationship tells us the length of the side across from it.”

The child looks at the drawing.

“Even if the triangle is much bigger?”

“Yes, provided it is still a right triangle. The lengths change, but the relationship holds.”

“Who first found that out?” asks the child.

“Many people investigated such relationships long before we were born,” says the carpenter. “We call this one the Pythagorean theorem.”

“Did they use those words?” asks the visitor.

“Not all of them. They used different words, drawings, numbers, and methods.”

The child looks at the chalk.

“Would a triangle know what name we gave it?”

The carpenter laughs. “It wouldn't know any name at all.”

“Then how can we tell that people using different explanations have understood the same thing?” the visitor asks.

“By seeing what their explanations establish,” says the carpenter. “An attempted demonstration can be different from another while reaching for the same relationship. An explanation may also be mistaken.”

“What would make it mistaken?”

“If it claimed the relationship held when the relevant conditions were not met, or if the reasoning did not establish what it claimed.”

The child takes the chalk and draws a lopsided triangle without a right angle.

“Would it work for this one?”

“We cannot simply assume it would,” says the carpenter. “The right angle was part of what mattered.”

The visitor studies the two drawings.

“Then there is the triangle we have drawn, the relationship we are trying to establish, and our different attempts to understand it.”

“Yes.”

“And when one explanation fails, we can criticize it by reference to something more than whether its words sound familiar.”

“Yes.”

“Could people who never met the original discoverer recognize the same relation?”

“That is what happens when someone studies a sound proof.”

The visitor asks, “Does the relation become new each time a child learns it?”

“Not in the way a new drawing becomes new,” says the carpenter. “The child's act of understanding is new. The relationship it establishes need not be.”

For a while they watch the child redraw the right angle carefully.

“Mathematics makes this difference easy to see,” says the visitor. “But perhaps it does not belong to mathematics alone. A warning can be faithfully translated, or mistranslated. A promise can be understood across different words. A practical explanation can be recognized as right or wrong even when its speaker is long gone.”

“Those are not all mathematical relations,” says the carpenter.

“No. We have only begun to see what might connect them.”

The child puts down the chalk.

“Are we finished with the triangle?”

“For today,” says the carpenter.

“And is everything about triangles now understood?”

“Not remotely.”

The child seems pleased by this answer.

The visitor looks at the board.

“So a particular understanding may be enough without becoming the last thing anyone will ever understand about its subject.”

The child carries the board into the shelter, where it will be needed again.

Chapter V — The Island

“Imagine an aircraft coming down near an island,” says the visitor. “Its passengers have come from many different countries. They reach shore alive, but there is no language all of them share.”

The child looks alarmed. “Can they find water?”

“They must. And shelter, and food.”

“Then they'll have to talk.”

“They will try. But at first, when one person speaks, many of the others do not understand.”

The child thinks about this.

“They could show one another things.”

“Such as?”

“One person could find a stream and take the others to it.”

“And if someone drank from the stream and became ill?”

“They would need to warn the others.”

“Suppose they had no word in common for dangerous.”

The child points sharply towards the stream, then shakes their head.

The visitor smiles. “Would everyone understand that?”

“Maybe not. Someone might think I wanted them to look at the water.”

“Then what?”

“I'd try to show what happened to the sick person.”

“And if the other person understood?”

“They wouldn't drink from it.”

“So what made the warning successful?”

“It helped them know what mattered.”

The first speaker, who has been listening, draws a line in the sand.

“Now suppose several people build a shelter. One wall falls during the night. Another remains standing. Do the survivors need an agreed word for standing before they can encounter the difference?”

“No,” says the child. “They would see which one fell.”

“Would knowing how to repair the fallen wall be the same as knowing which wall fell?”

“No. They would have to find out why.”

“And if they learned how to keep the next wall upright?”

“They could show others how.”

The visitor follows the line in the sand.

“Imagine their children growing up on the island. Over the years the families borrow sounds, invent signs, correct misunderstandings and gradually form ways of speaking together.”

“Then the children would have a language,” says the child.

“Yes. But consider something strange. The people were already recognizing which water made them ill, which shelter stood, which path was safe, and which work remained unfinished. Their developing language helps them explain these things more clearly. Did it create the difference between safe water and unsafe water?”

“No.”

“Could it help them discover differences they hadn't noticed?”

“Of course.”

“Then language changes what they can understand without having to be the source of every difference they understand.”

The child studies the sand drawing.

“Maybe they invent a word because something keeps happening and they need to tell people.”

“That is a possibility worth taking seriously,” says the first speaker. “The need to make something clear may help a language develop.”

“Then people don't have to know the words before they can discover why they need them.”

“Exactly.”

The visitor looks towards the path.

“What begins as pointing may become explanation. What begins as a failed warning may become a shared expression. And the expression can be corrected because the speakers are trying to make something definite available to one another.”

“Is that how all languages began?” asks the child.

“We have not shown that,” says the first speaker. “This island is an imagined situation, not a history of language. But it helps us see a possibility: people can already live among intelligible differences while they are still struggling to find common words.”

The child wipes away the line in the sand.

“The water would still flow, even if the drawing was gone.”

“Yes.”

“And someone could still get sick from it, even if nobody knew the right warning.”

“Yes.”

“Then there's a lot to learn before you can say it well.”

“And,” says the visitor, “sometimes learning to say it well is one of the ways you learn more.”

Chapter VI — The Mandate

On the island, a small boat has been sent to look for supplies.

People wait on the shore. The boat is overdue.

“Has it returned?” one person asks.

A neighbour replies, “It will return.”

The child looks out at the water.

“That wasn't what they asked.”

“What did they ask?” says the visitor.

“Whether it has come back.”

“Could the neighbour's answer be encouraging?”

“Yes.”

“Could the neighbour sincerely believe it?”

“Yes. But that doesn't tell them whether the boat has returned.”

Another person approaches from the lookout and says, “Not yet.”

The waiting people seem disappointed, but they know what they needed to know.

“Was that a better answer?” asks the visitor.

“Yes,” says the child. “Even though nobody liked it.”

“And if the neighbour had replied, ‘I hope so’?”

“That would tell us how the neighbour felt, not whether the boat was there.”

The first speaker joins them by the shore.

“Why does one answer fit and another fail?”

“Because of what the question asks,” says the child.

“Did anyone have to invent an extra rule that a question about whether the boat has returned should be answered by saying whether it has returned?”

“It seems to come with the question.”

“Then something in the undertaking makes demands upon an answer.”

The visitor picks up a rope lying beside the landing place.

“Suppose this rope is frayed, and someone is asked to repair it. They tie a neat knot and say they are finished.”

“Would they be?”

“It depends,” says the child. “Would the rope hold?”

“And if it didn't?”

“The knot might be neat, but the repair hadn't worked.”

“Now suppose someone promises to keep watch for the boat and goes home before their turn begins.”

“They haven't kept the promise.”

“Even if they say they have?”

“Yes.”

The first speaker sets down the rope.

“Here we have different undertakings: answering the question, repairing the rope, keeping the promise. Each makes a different demand upon what would count as finished.”

The child glances towards the sea.

“Are we allowed to stop talking about the boat while it's still out there?”

“Yes,” says the visitor. “Talking more won't bring it back.”

“Then a conversation can stop without its question being answered.”

The visitor nods.

“And the boat may return without anyone being present to say it has returned. The event and the answer about the event are not identical.”

A sail appears far out against the light.

The child is the first to see it.

“Is that the boat?”

The others look, wait, and recognize its markings.

“Now,” says the lookout, “we can answer.”

The neighbour who had promised its return does not look triumphant.

“I hoped I was right,” the neighbour says. “But until we could see it, hope was not the answer.”

The first speaker smiles.

“So we can understand why some attempts fail before we know how to make them succeed. A mistaken translation can be recognized as mistaken. A rope can be found still unfit for its purpose. An answer can be recognized as missing its question.”

“And this is how we improve them?” asks the child.

“Often. Their failure tells us something about what they were meant to accomplish.”

The boat draws near.

The visitor looks from the sail to the child.

“A successful answer does not need to make us happy. It needs to be enough for what was asked. And sometimes, as we have just seen, what makes an answer enough can be examined in the world before us.”

Chapter VII — Becoming Fluent

A new person has joined the work on the island.

For weeks she has been learning the shared language that has begun to grow among its people.

One morning a neighbour calls to her from the shelter. She pauses, silently finds the corresponding words in the language she learned as a child, and then brings the requested tool.

The visitor watches.

“What made you pause?”

“I had to turn the neighbour's words into my own before I knew what was wanted.”

“And do you always need to do that?”

“For now.”

Months pass.

The same neighbour calls from the shelter. This time the learner reaches for the tool before the neighbour has finished speaking.

The child laughs. “She understood without stopping to translate.”

The learner smiles.

“I heard what was wanted. I didn't think about my old words at all.”

“Does that mean you have forgotten your first language?” asks the visitor.

“Of course not.”

“Did you have to discover for the first time what it means to ask for help?”

“No. But I had to learn how this language makes a request, and when the same words can mean something different.”

“Can you now explain everything you understand in the new language?”

The learner thinks.

“Not everything. Sometimes I know what a person means but cannot find a good way to say it in the language I knew first.”

“Then understanding an expression and translating it are different tasks.”

“Yes.”

The child asks, “Can somebody speak a language very quickly without understanding what they're saying?”

“Certainly,” says the learner. “I could repeat phrases before I knew when to use them.”

“And could you understand a word but still use it badly?”

“Yes. People have had to correct me.”

The visitor asks, “What helped when you were corrected?”

“The person showed me why the phrase didn't fit the occasion. Sometimes we tried three different examples before I saw it.”

“Did the examples make you learn only a new sound?”

“No. They helped me notice a difference that I had been missing.”

The first speaker, coming into the shelter, asks the learner about a word that has no comfortable equivalent in the first language.

“Can you tell us what it means?”

“I can tell you where people use it,” she replies. “And I can give examples. But I wouldn't want to pretend there is one word in my first language that does exactly the same work.”

“So fluency is not merely finding a faster way to exchange one word for another,” says the visitor.

“No. It is being able to live and understand in the language itself.”

The child picks up the requested tool and hands it to the neighbour.

“Did the tool change because the learner became fluent?”

“No,” says the visitor.

“Did the language change?”

“It may have, as its speakers used it and learned from one another.”

“And did the learner change?”

“Certainly.”

“Then how can we say she understood the same request as before?”

“Because we can examine what was requested and whether she recognized it. We need not confuse the changing path of her understanding with every thing she comes to understand.”

The learner looks at the tool in the neighbour's hand.

“At first, my first language gave me a way into this one. Now understanding this one gives me new things to ask about both.”

The visitor nods.

“An acquired language is not a prison into which all intelligibility must fit. It can be another way of participating in a world already offering things to discover.”

Chapter VIII — The Question We Have Earned

In the evening the visitors sit beside the shelter.

From somewhere nearby comes the sound of a door closing.

The child looks up.

“We have talked about that before.”

“About a door?” asks the first speaker.

“About the difference between closing and being closed.”

“And why did that matter?”

“Because someone who only finds a door closed shouldn't say they watched it close.”

The visitor remembers the travellers beside the fallen tree.

“And when people without a shared language tried to show one another that a path was blocked?”

“They could understand some of it, get it wrong, and try again.”

“And the translation?”

“The interpreter changed a report about a condition into a report about an event.”

“And what helped put it right?”

“Knowing which difference the first person was trying to preserve.”

The carpenter, passing by with a board, stops to listen.

“What about the triangle?”

“The words and drawings could change, but there was a relationship that a correct explanation had to establish.”

“And the people waiting for the boat?” asks the first speaker.

“They needed an answer to their question, not just something encouraging to hear.”

The visitor looks around at them.

“We have met words, gestures, repairs, mathematical relationships, promises, and practical undertakings. Do these all concern one kind of object?”

“No,” says the child.

“Then why have they led us through one inquiry?”

The child thinks for a long time.

“Because we keep asking what was made clear, or what someone was trying to do.”

The first speaker says, “And whether the attempt became enough.”

“And whether what was understood could be used or understood again,” adds the learner.

The visitor nods.

“We have not yet found an account of every kind of completion. But our encounters have given us a way to look for one.”

The carpenter asks, “What would you look for first?”

“What changed between the difficulty and the achievement?”

“And next?”

“What made the change sufficient for the undertaking?”

“And after that?”

“What remained available as achieved?”

The child raises a hand.

“Does the order of the questions mean the things happen in that order?”

“Not necessarily,” says the visitor. “We ask them one after another because we cannot say everything at once. But in a completed achievement, what changed, what made it enough, and what remains achieved belong together.”

The child looks at the closed door.

“So knowing that it is closed is not the same as knowing why it closed.”

“No.”

“And being able to open it isn't the same as knowing whether it is closed.”

“No.”

“And a person could know all three things?”

“Yes, because one undertaking can lead into another.”

The first speaker says, “Then perhaps the next part of our inquiry should begin where the things themselves lead us. Not with a table of names, but with a question that is hard to answer well unless we notice what a particular achievement requires.”

“What question?” asks the child.

The visitor points towards the shelter door.

“What would make an answer enough?”

The child considers this, then stands.

“I can show you one thing. The door is closed.”

“Is that enough?”

“If that was what you wanted to know.”

They go inside.

The first question has been answered.

The next one is waiting.

Chapter IX — What Makes an Answer Enough?

The door to the shelter shuts in a gust of wind.

The child, sitting beside a basket of tools, looks up.

“Is it closed?” asks the visitor.

“Yes,” says the child.

“Why did it close?”

The child shrugs. “I only saw it close. I don't know why.”

The visitor turns to the first speaker.

“Was the first answer wrong?”

“No. It answered whether the door was closed.”

“But it was no use for the second question.”

“It wasn't an answer to the second question,” says the child, a little defensively. “You asked something new.”

The visitor smiles. “Then perhaps you have made the point more clearly than I could.”

Outside, the wind moves the branches against the roof.

“Maybe the wind did it,” says the child.

“Is that a guess or something you know?”

“A guess. The wind is strong. But I didn't see what pushed the door.”

They examine the door. Its latch is loose, and a new gust presses it against the frame.

“That seems to explain it,” says the child.

“Does it tell us who built the door?” asks the first speaker.

“No.”

“Does it tell us whether the door needs repairing?”

“Not entirely.”

“Then should we say our inquiry has accomplished nothing because those questions remain?”

The child looks exasperated. “We found why it closed. You can ask about the latch later.”

The carpenter, entering from the garden, has heard the last exchange.

“Someone asked me yesterday whether the bridge could carry a cart,” he says. “I could have answered, ‘Yes, it looks sturdy,’ and perhaps the person would have gone away satisfied. Would that have made the bridge safe?”

“No,” says the child.

“Then the end of a conversation is not necessarily the completion of the undertaking.”

“That's different from the door,” says the child. “We saw the wind close it.”

“Exactly,” says the carpenter. “Different questions require different ways of finding out.”

The visitor points along a corridor with three doors.

“Suppose I ask, ‘Which door is closed?’”

The child points to the middle one.

“And if I ask, ‘Can we pass through?’”

“I'd try the handle. Or look for another way.”

“Would the same pointing answer both questions?”

“No. In one case you only wanted to know which door. In the other you wanted to get through.”

The visitor falls quiet.

“I think I have sometimes confused two things,” she says. “The fact that I can ask another question, and the idea that the first answer wasn't enough.”

“You can always ask another question,” says the carpenter. “But you cannot fairly demand that the first answer accomplish what nobody asked it to do.”

“Nor can I call an answer sufficient simply because I am tired of asking.”

“No,” he says. “There has to be something about the question and the world that the answer gets right.”

The child tries the middle handle. The door opens onto a small storage room.

“There,” the child says. “Now you know which one was shut, why one door closed in the wind, and whether we can get into this room.”

“Three achievements?”

“Three questions,” says the child. “At least.”

The visitor looks back towards the door in the wind.

“What makes an answer enough?”

The child thinks for a moment.

“What it was supposed to answer.”

“And how do we tell that it really has?”

“We look at what it claims, and what the thing itself lets us find out.”

The carpenter closes the storage-room door gently.

“Then let's try a harder case,” he says. “What if we know a door can open, but the way beyond it is still blocked?”

They go to see.

Chapter X — The Door That Can Be Opened

The carpenter leads them to the back entrance of the storehouse.

The handle turns, but the door stays in its frame.

“Something's holding it,” says the child.

The visitor tries the handle. “Could we push harder?”

“We could break it,” says the carpenter. “Let's find out what holds it first.”

They examine the edges. At the bottom, partly hidden in the shadow, is a small bolt.

The child bends down, slides it clear, and pulls.

The door opens.

“That was easy,” the child says.

“Was the bolt the whole door?” asks the visitor.

“No. But it was what stopped the door opening.”

“And if the undertaking was simply to open this door?”

“Then moving the bolt did it.”

The child walks through and immediately stops.

The passage beyond the door is crowded with fallen timber. A storm has brought part of the roof down.

“Well,” says the carpenter. “Can we reach the storehouse yard?”

“Not through here,” says the child.

“But we opened the door,” says the visitor.

“Yes. And there's a lot more in the way.”

The carpenter lifts one branch aside. The child tries to squeeze past, but a heavy beam still blocks the passage.

“Is moving that branch enough?” asks the visitor.

“Not if we need a path through all of it,” says the child.

“Then the question has changed.”

“No,” says the child. “We wanted to get through all along. We just thought opening the door would be enough.”

The carpenter pauses.

“That's an important correction. We should not pretend our undertaking was narrower than it really was.”

Together they move the lighter pieces. The carpenter checks the beam before they disturb it. Only when the whole passage is clear can they carry a basket from one end to the other.

The visitor rests against the wall.

“One difficulty was answered at one decisive place: the bolt. The other required a continuous route. Fixing one place did not make the whole path usable.”

The carpenter nods. “A door and a passage can belong to the same journey and still ask different things of us.”

As they gather their tools, a neighbour arrives.

“Why did you spend the morning clearing this side?” she asks. “The eastern entrance is open. I came in that way.”

The child stares at her.

“There's another entrance?”

“Of course. We started using it after the storm.”

The visitor looks at the cleared timber.

“Would the old passage have needed clearing if our only undertaking had been to reach the yard?”

“Not if the other entrance was known and usable,” says the carpenter.

“Then what would have happened to the blockage?”

“It would still have been there,” says the child. “But we could have gone around it.”

“Would that have repaired the damaged passage?”

“No.”

“Would it have made entering the yard possible?”

“Yes.”

The neighbour laughs. “You sound as though you'd rather have discovered my entrance than repaired the passage.”

“Perhaps,” says the visitor. “But the work we've done may be needed for another reason. We shouldn't say it was useless merely because a second way exists.”

The carpenter sets a basket in the cleared passage.

“Both entrances may matter when supplies arrive. A task can be properly completed even when there was another way to meet the original need.”

The visitor looks at the two routes.

“Let's see whether we can describe the differences without making them merely differences of size. Moving the bolt was enough because one particular obstruction was decisive. Clearing the passage was enough only when the connected route was passable. Finding the other entrance would have meant the old blockage no longer decided whether we could reach the yard.”

The child considers them.

“One place. A whole way. Or a problem that stops deciding.”

“Three different accounts of what made a result enough,” says the first speaker, who has joined them.

“Do we have to give them names?” asks the child.

“Only if the names help us keep the differences clear.”

The child points to the fallen timber.

“I'd like a name that doesn't make me carry it again.”

The carpenter laughs, then becomes serious.

“There's another thing to notice. We've finished the clearing. Tomorrow someone can use this route without repeating the work.”

“And with the eastern entrance?” asks the visitor.

“The old obstruction may remain irrelevant to tomorrow's journey.”

“Those achievements do not simply vanish when we stop working,” says the visitor. “But do they remain in the same way?”

The child hoists the basket.

“Let's find out tomorrow. I'm finished carrying wood today.”

No one argues with that answer.

Chapter XI — What Remains After the Answer?

The next morning, someone arrives at the storehouse carrying two keys.

“One of these opens the food room,” the keeper says. “I can't remember which.”

The child tries the first. It will not turn. The second opens the lock.

“There,” says the child, holding it up.

“Must you open the door again tomorrow to know which key worked today?” asks the visitor.

“Not if I remember. I could mark it.”

The child ties a short piece of string to the second key.

“Now someone else can find it.”

“Does the string make the key work?” asks the keeper.

“No. It helps us recognize the key we found.”

The visitor picks up both keys.

“Yesterday's turning has ended. But the distinction we achieved can remain available.”

“Until someone changes the lock,” says the keeper.

“Then we would need to examine what still holds,” the visitor agrees.

Later, the keeper asks the people who distribute food to agree on who will hold the key.

“Mara will keep it,” they decide. “When someone needs supplies, they will ask her.”

They leave the storehouse.

“Has the agreement ended?” asks the visitor.

“The speaking has ended,” says the child. “Mara still has to keep the key.”

“And when another person comes for supplies?”

“What we agreed tells them whom to ask.”

“Does this remain like the little string on the key?”

The keeper considers.

“Not quite. The string helps identify an object. The agreement makes sense of what Mara and the others are responsible for. If that relationship changes, the arrangement changes.”

They walk towards the spring along the path cleared earlier.

People are carrying water in both directions.

“Who is working on the path now?” asks the visitor.

“Nobody,” says the child. “It's already clear.”

“Has the achievement therefore disappeared?”

“Look at all the people using it.”

The first speaker bends to pick up a fallen branch that has landed beside the path.

“Three things have remained after their decisive moments,” she says. “We know which key opened the lock. The agreement about the key continues to bear upon what the people do. And the cleared path lets people carry water.”

“Could we say that the key can also be used,” asks the visitor, “or that the cleared path is an established fact?”

“Of course,” says the keeper. “We're not pretending every object does only one thing.”

“Then what are we distinguishing?”

The child answers slowly.

“How each particular achievement remains useful—no, not just useful. How it remains what we accomplished.”

They stop at the path through the trees.

“Suppose the old storehouse entrance stays blocked,” says the first speaker, “but the eastern entrance remains open. Do we need to forget the blockage to use the new way?”

“No,” says the child.

“Must we deny that it mattered yesterday?”

“No. It may matter again if the other entrance is shut.”

“Yet it need not govern how we enter today.”

The visitor looks towards the spring.

“What a completed achievement leaves behind is not always a thing we can hold. Sometimes a fact stands for later recognition. Sometimes a relationship continues to matter. Sometimes an accomplished capacity changes what can be done.”

The keeper lifts the marked key.

“And these don't belong to three successive mornings that every discovery must go through.”

“No,” says the visitor. “They are differences in how the achieved result remains available.”

The child runs ahead, then returns.

“You said yesterday that you had two questions. What were they?”

“What makes the undertaking complete, and how what has been completed remains?”

“Are they the same question?”

The child thinks of the bolt, the cleared passage, and the second entrance.

“No. You could know that we finished the work without noticing what it gives us afterward.”

“Or notice that people use the passage without knowing what had to be completed to make it usable.”

They reach the spring.

The child fills a jug, lifts it, and starts home.

“The path still works,” the child says.

“Then our earlier work has arrived here before us,” says the first speaker.

The child glances back.

“Not the work. What the work made possible.”

The visitor nods.

They have found a difference that will matter again.

Chapter XII — A True Sentence, an Unanswered Question

The visitor arrives at the storehouse and finds the keeper sorting jars along a shelf.

“Where is the key to the food room?” she asks.

“The door is locked,” says the keeper.

“I know. That's why I need the key.”

“It was built last month,” the keeper adds, looking towards the doorway.

The visitor laughs. “Are you going to tell me everything about the storehouse except where the key is?”

The keeper turns, surprised.

“I thought you were asking about the door.”

“I asked where the key was.”

“Oh. Mara has it. She took it home.”

“Thank you. That answers my question.”

The child, who has accompanied the visitor, looks at the keeper.

“Was it wrong to say the door was locked?”

“No,” says the keeper. “It is locked.”

“Or that the storehouse was built last month?”

“That is true too.”

“Then why did she keep asking?”

“Because neither was an answer to where the key was,” says the visitor.

The child considers the shelves.

“If she'd asked whether the door was locked, the first answer would have done.”

“Yes.”

“And if she'd asked when it was built, the second would have done.”

“Exactly.”

The keeper closes a jar.

“So the same words can be enough in one conversation and not in another.”

“Not merely because people are pleased or displeased,” says the visitor. “The question gives them something to answer to.”

They leave for Mara's home, but after a few steps the visitor stops.

“Wait. When you heard that Mara had the key, what did you understand?”

“That we should go and find her,” says the child.

“Where?”

“Here, near the storehouse, I suppose.”

“Why near the storehouse?”

“Because the keeper said the key was with Mara.”

“But the keeper also said Mara took it home.”

The child looks embarrassed. “I wasn't listening to the last part.”

“Then what happened?”

“I heard the answer, but I did not understand it properly.”

“Did the key move when you noticed that?”

“No.”

“Did the keeper need to change what happened to the key?”

“No. I needed to change what I understood.”

The first speaker, meeting them on the path, asks why they have turned around.

“We were going to search near the storehouse,” says the child. “But the key is at Mara's house. I understood the first part and missed the rest.”

“Then is a successful explanation always the same thing as its listener's successful understanding?” asks the speaker.

“Not if the listener hears it badly.”

“Could a clear sentence also say something false?”

“Of course.”

“And could someone understand that false sentence perfectly?”

“Yes. They would know what was being claimed, even if it wasn't true.”

The visitor watches the child walk ahead.

“We have been noticing three things that easily get tangled: what a sentence says, whether what it says is true, and what the listener actually understands.”

“Would a longer explanation always help?” asks the first speaker.

“No. It might merely give us more to misunderstand.”

“Then what helps?”

“A clarification that removes the particular mistake.”

At Mara's house the child asks for the key.

Mara retrieves it from a hook beside the door.

“Was the keeper right?” asks the visitor.

“Yes,” says the child, taking the key. “And now I know what with Mara meant in that conversation.”

They return to the storehouse and open the food room.

“Finding the key has helped us do something else,” says the visitor. “But that does not make answering its whereabouts, understanding the answer, and opening the door one and the same achievement.”

The child hangs the key on its hook.

“Can we now get the food?”

“That,” says the keeper, “is a new and much better question.”

The visitor smiles.

“If words can be true without answering, and understood without being true, then perhaps we should not look only to sentences when we ask what it means for something to become intelligibly complete.”

“Where else?” asks the child.

“Let's look at someone who cannot yet say what they have recognized.”

Chapter XIII — Before the Sentence Is Ready

Two baskets sit outside the shelter.

The younger child has helped gather fruit but has not yet learned the number words the adults use.

One basket holds three oranges. The other holds two.

The younger child looks from one basket to the other, then moves an orange from the first basket into the second.

The visitor, who has been watching, asks, “What did that tell us?”

The older child starts to answer.

“It means—”

“Perhaps we should be careful,” says the first speaker. “We can see what was moved. We can see what the younger child attended to. Do we know exactly what the child was thinking?”

The older child looks at the baskets.

“No. Maybe they wanted the second one to have more.”

“And maybe they were comparing the two,” says the visitor. “We cannot tell everything from the action.”

The younger child reaches towards one basket, then stops as someone takes an orange from it.

The visitor offers the orange back. The younger child smiles and returns to the basket.

“Whatever the child has understood, must a sentence have been spoken first for there to be a difference between the arrangements?”

“No,” says the older child. “We can count them even if the younger one cannot.”

“Could the younger child notice something about having more or fewer without possessing the adult number words?”

“It seems possible. But we mustn't pretend we know how far that recognition goes.”

The first speaker nods.

“Then this is neither a reason to deny the younger child any understanding nor a reason to credit the child with every distinction the adults can state.”

The older child sits beside the baskets.

“What happens when we teach the words?”

“Try,” says the visitor.

They begin naming the numbers, pairing oranges with fingers and sounds. The younger child repeats a sound, points to an orange, and laughs when everyone else laughs.

“Has saying a sound settled whether the child knows its use?” asks the visitor.

“Not yet. We'd need to see what the child can do with it.”

“Would learning numbers merely put labels on an understanding already fully present?”

“No,” says the older child. “The words might help the child compare more things and tell someone who can't see the baskets.”

The first speaker picks up one basket.

“Suppose I take this away. Can the younger child now learn to tell us how many oranges were inside it?”

“That would be a new ability,” says the older child.

“And could a question that was difficult before become possible?”

“Yes.”

“Then language can help understanding grow. We need not say it creates every distinction to recognize how much it changes what can be made clear.”

The younger child holds an orange towards the visitor and says a word nobody quite recognizes.

“Was that the right word?” asks the older child.

“We don't yet know what was intended,” says the visitor. “Let's look at what the child is trying to do.”

The younger child offers the orange again. The visitor takes it and says thank you. The younger child reaches for another.

“Perhaps the child was asking you to take it,” says the first speaker.

“Perhaps. And now we have an occasion to test that understanding.”

The older child looks at the two baskets.

“I thought learning language was just getting better at saying things.”

“What do you think now?” asks the visitor.

“That it also helps you notice what you need to say, even when you don't have the words for it.”

The first speaker smiles.

“An expression may be missing while a difficulty is already recognizable. And finding the expression may itself teach us a difference we had not properly seen before.”

The younger child puts an orange in each of the visitor's hands.

The visitor looks down, trying to decide whether it is an invitation, a game, or an instruction.

“I think,” she says, “we have another little question to ask before we can be sure we've understood.”

The older child laughs.

“Then the younger one isn't the only person still learning.”

Chapter XIV — One Answer, Three Questions

Rain has blown against the storehouse door all morning.

The carpenter is examining a dark line of dampness beneath it when the child arrives.

“Is the door closed?” asks the child.

“Yes,” says the carpenter.

“Then why is there water inside?”

“Because being closed doesn't necessarily make it watertight.”

The visitor joins them.

“Can the door be closed and still fail to keep the rain out?”

“Look here,” says the carpenter, pointing to a narrow gap beside the frame. “The water comes through.”

“And can we open it from inside?” asks the child.

The carpenter reaches for the latch. It is too high for the youngest people who use the storehouse.

“Some of us can,” he says. “Not everyone.”

The visitor looks at the damp floor.

“So someone could truthfully tell us the door is shut while leaving two other undertakings unfinished.”

“Keeping out the rain,” says the child.

“And allowing everyone who needs it to open the door from inside,” says the carpenter.

They press a strip of cloth into the gap. During the next shower the floor remains dry.

“Now the first trouble is repaired,” says the child.

“Which trouble?” asks the visitor.

“The leak.”

“And the latch?”

“Still too high.”

“Then the fact that another requirement remains does not undo the weatherproofing.”

The carpenter nods.

He moves the latch lower. The child can now reach it easily, but on a damp morning the swollen frame holds fast.

“I thought I'd fixed it,” the carpenter says.

“You made it reachable,” says the child. “But it still won't open.”

“Then I answered one demand and missed another.”

The visitor examines the edge of the door.

“If the undertaking is reliable opening in wet and dry weather, would one adjustment necessarily be enough?”

“Not if the frame swells,” says the carpenter. “I need to examine the clearance and the movement of the whole door.”

They work through the afternoon. The carpenter tests the latch and the frame, then waits until the wood is damp again before calling the work finished.

The child is impatient.

“Why test it twice?”

“Because the trouble doesn't happen only when the wood is dry.”

“So what makes this repair enough depends upon the conditions in which the door has to work.”

“Yes.”

Later a neighbour arrives carrying a basket of grain.

“Are you still working on the western door?” she asks.

“We've made it work,” says the child.

“Good. After the storm we were using the eastern entrance. The fallen tree made the western way impossible.”

“Did you move the tree?” asks the visitor.

“No. It still lies across the old approach. We went around the other side.”

The carpenter sets down his tools.

“So while this door was being repaired, the people were already able to enter by another way.”

“For the purpose of delivering grain,” says the neighbour, “the western blockage had ceased to decide whether we could get in.”

“Yet it might still matter if we needed to bring in a very wide cart,” the carpenter says.

“Then the old obstruction could become relevant to a new undertaking,” says the visitor. “We should not confuse its no longer governing one question with its ceasing to exist.”

The child looks at the two entrances.

“We had one job that could be put right at the gap, another that needed the whole door to move reliably, and another where we didn't have to fix the old way at all.”

The carpenter smiles.

“And those differences are not just how much time each job took.”

“No,” says the visitor. “They concern what makes the undertaking sufficient.”

The child taps the newly lowered latch.

“Did someone have to speak before the latch became reachable?”

“No.”

“But if we wanted to tell someone who wasn't here what had changed?”

“We would need to explain which achievement we meant,” says the carpenter. “A door may be shut, weatherproof, and easy to open. Those are different things to establish.”

The visitor watches the child open the door.

“Perhaps this is why we must not describe every kind of success in the same way. The world, the words describing it, and a person's understanding of those words can be connected without becoming one event.”

The child steps outside.

“Right now I just need the door to open.”

“And it does,” says the carpenter.

For that undertaking, the answer is enough.

Chapter XV — What We Cannot Yet Say

The younger child has begun carrying water to the shelter.

Two containers stand beside the path. One has a fine crack near the bottom. The child pours water into it, watches a thin stream escape, then transfers the remaining water to the other container.

The visitor has seen the whole thing.

“What have we learned?” she asks the older child.

“That the little one noticed the leak.”

“Can we be certain what the younger child understood?”

“Not every part of it. But the water was going out, and the child changed containers.”

The first speaker comes from the shelter.

“Which one would you use to carry water?” she asks the younger child.

A small finger points to the uncracked container.

“Does that amount to a complete explanation?” asks the visitor.

“No,” says the older child. “But it isn't nothing.”

“And what would be wrong with saying we know everything the younger one understands?”

“We'd be guessing.”

“So we can acknowledge a difference made available through action without claiming an explanation the child has not given.”

The first speaker sets the broken container aside.

The next day the younger child learns a new word for leaking. The word delights the child, who repeats it while pointing at the crack.

“Does that new word repair the container?” the older child asks.

“No,” says the visitor.

“Does it make the crack appear?”

“No.”

“But it means the younger one can tell someone about a leak without bringing the container along.”

“And perhaps notice another leak somewhere else,” says the first speaker. “The new word may help the child make a comparison that was difficult yesterday.”

The older child considers this.

“So the word doesn't merely dress up what the child already understood. Learning it may allow more understanding.”

The visitor nods.

Later a neighbour returns from the spring. The younger child announces, with evident pride, that the container leaked yesterday but is good now.

The neighbour picks it up and looks doubtful.

“Did someone mend it?”

The older child checks. A small patch of resin covers the crack.

“Someone did.”

“And is it certain to hold when the container is full?”

The carpenter fills it and leaves it standing. A drop forms beneath the patch.

“Not yet,” he says.

The younger child watches the drop fall, then looks at the visitor.

“The word was right,” says the older child. “But the claim that it holds water now was wrong.”

“And could the neighbour understand that claim clearly even though it was mistaken?”

“Yes.”

“Could the sentence have been perfectly well formed?”

“Yes.”

“Then learning to say something, saying something true, and making another person understand it are different accomplishments.”

The first speaker hands the container back to the carpenter.

“What must we correct now?”

“The repair,” says the child. “And what we said about it.”

“Are those the same work?”

“No. One is done with resin and the container. The other is done by telling people what is actually the case.”

The carpenter presses the patch away and begins again.

The visitor looks towards the younger child, who is still repeating the new word.

“There is something heartening in all this. We do not have to deny a child's earlier recognition because the words came later. But we must not imagine the learning of words adds nothing. It can bring new questions and new distinctions within reach.”

The older child looks at the half-mended container.

“And even once we know the word, we still have to find out whether we've fixed the thing.”

“Yes.”

The carpenter finishes and leaves the container full until morning.

When it holds, the younger child runs to show everyone.

“What has become complete now?” asks the visitor.

“The repair,” says the older child. “And the child has learned a way to tell us about it. Tomorrow we may need to check it again, but that doesn't mean nothing was achieved today.”

The visitor smiles.

“We should allow each accomplishment its own measure. A word can be learned without completing an education. A repair can succeed without preventing every future failure. A true account can be given without answering every question about its subject.”

“Do we know all the ways this can happen?” asks the older child.

“Not yet. Naming a number of forms would not show why those differences belong in it.”

“Then what shall we do?”

“Take an achievement we can watch closely. Ask what was necessary, what became enough, and what remained when the activity ended.”

The younger child has gone to the sand with the dry container and a stick.

“There's your next achievement,” says the first speaker.

They turn to see what the child is drawing.

Chapter XVI — The Mark That Remains

A child draws a circle in the sand beside the shelter.

The visitor watches the small hand complete the line and lift away.

“Is the drawing finished?” she asks.

“Yes,” says the child.

“Is the movement of your hand still happening?”

“No.”

“But the circle is still there.”

The child looks pleased. “You can see it.”

A second child traces the line with another stick.

“Has the first child's drawing happened again?” asks the visitor.

“No,” says the older child. “This is another movement. But it follows the line already made.”

The younger child has a purpose for the mark.

“Stones go there,” the child says, pointing around the circle.

The carpenter, passing with a basket of small stones, stops to watch.

“So the circle isn't only a picture,” he says. “It tells us where to make the garden boundary.”

They place the stones carefully.

The child rubs away a little of the circle while arranging them.

“Will the boundary fail if the sand mark disappears?” asks the visitor.

“No,” says the carpenter. “If the stones are in their places, they can still mark the edge.”

“And if the children forget where they meant the stones to go before setting them?”

“Then the mark may be important,” he says. “We should not declare that a plan remains available when nothing can still guide its use.”

They finish the boundary and step outside it.

The visitor looks at the partly erased circle.

“What has survived from the drawing?”

The older child answers, “The stones are where it showed them to go.”

“Is that the same as remembering every movement of the little one's hand?”

“No.”

“Then an achieved arrangement can depend upon an earlier mark without needing the original moment to continue.”

A few days later, shoots appear inside the boundary.

One stone has tilted. The older child straightens it.

“Have you repaired the whole boundary?” asks the visitor.

“I fixed this stone. The others were already where they should be.”

The carpenter points towards the far side, where the sloping ground has drawn several stones out of line.

“Over there we may have a different problem.”

“Moving one stone won't settle it,” says the child. “We have to look at the line across that whole side.”

They examine the slope, reset several stones, and check the extent of the boundary.

The visitor waits until they have finished.

“What made the first adjustment enough?”

“One crooked stone was the problem.”

“And the second?”

“The whole stretch had to mark the edge.”

“Then the difference isn't merely that one job took longer.”

“No,” says the carpenter. “What counted as finished was different.”

Some weeks later the plants have grown thick enough to mark the garden's edge by themselves.

The younger child asks whether the stones must remain.

“Perhaps not,” says the first speaker. “We can see the boundary in the planting.”

“Would taking the stones away destroy the earlier achievement?” asks the visitor.

“Not the fact that they marked the garden when we needed them,” says the older child. “But the garden doesn't need them in the same way now.”

“Could the stones remain where they are without still being necessary?”

“Yes.”

“Then something can stop deciding how the garden must be marked without ceasing to have mattered.”

The carpenter takes up one stone.

“We could use these around the new bed.”

The child looks at the first boundary.

“So a completed thing can make another thing possible. But that doesn't mean the new bed was already finished when we placed the first stones.”

The visitor nods.

“Nor does it mean the first boundary was incomplete because another one can now be made.”

They gather the stones that are no longer needed.

As they work, the younger child says, “Now we can plant the new place.”

The older child looks at the soil.

“Not quite. We still have to prepare it.”

“What have you done, then?” asks the first speaker.

“We've made the space available.”

“And what remains from that achievement?”

“A place we can begin to prepare,” says the child.

The visitor looks at the sand, the plants, and the stones in the basket.

“The mark, the arrangement, the changing boundary, the new opening: these are connected. But they do not all remain in the same way, nor do they all answer the same undertaking.”

The child sets the last stone down.

“Do we have to say all that before we plant?”

“No,” says the visitor.

“Good.”

The children begin preparing the bed.

What was finished has given them something else to do.

Chapter XVII — The Word That Has No Equivalent

The two speakers stand in the garden after the day's work.

The young plants have been watered. The tools are clean. Neither speaker is ready to leave.

The first says a word from her childhood language.

The second looks up.

“What does that mean?”

The first thinks.

“It's a feeling I used to have when the work was done and everything was ready, but I wanted to stay a little longer.”

“Is it happiness?”

“Sometimes. But not simply happiness.”

“Pride?”

“Not quite.”

The second speaker laughs. “You are giving me a very poor dictionary.”

“I know. I don't think there's one word in your language that does quite the same work.”

“Then show me.”

The first speaker looks at the completed beds.

“Think of a day when you've finished something with people you care about. Nothing else is required just now. You don't want to change anything. You only want to remain a little while with what has been made.”

The second is quiet.

“I know something like that.”

“Would you use the word here?”

“I might. But I want to understand when you would use it.”

The first smiles.

“That is a better question.”

Over the next few days the second speaker tries the word in different situations.

One morning, after washing a single cup, she uses it proudly.

The first speaker hesitates.

“I understand why you tried it. But it sounds strange there.”

“Why? The work is finished.”

“Because it isn't only the ending of a task. It's something about wanting to remain with what has come to rest. It can happen after very small work, but it isn't merely any moment when the work stops.”

The second looks at the clean cup.

“Then I recognized part of it, but not enough to use it well.”

“Perhaps. Can you think of another occasion?”

That evening they sit beside the garden after helping the neighbours repair a path. No one has left yet. One person is quietly putting away a tool; another is looking at the work without speaking.

The second speaker feels the new word arrive before she says it.

“Would it fit now?”

The first does not answer immediately.

“Try saying it.”

The second says the word softly.

The first smiles. “Yes. That is how I would say it.”

“Then now I have learned the word?”

“You have learned something about using it. Tomorrow we may find another situation that teaches you more.”

The second considers this.

“When I understood your example on the first day, I didn't yet know how to use the word.”

“No.”

“And when I used it badly, I had not failed to understand everything.”

“No. There was something you had grasped, and something you still needed to learn.”

“Could I understand the word well and still find it difficult to translate into my first language?”

“Certainly.”

The visitor, who has been nearby, joins them.

“Then explanation, recognition, appropriate use, and translation can succeed or fail in different respects.”

The second speaker laughs.

“Do you always need to divide a happy moment into four undertakings?”

“Not always,” says the visitor. “But if a learner has difficulty, the differences can help us understand what needs attention.”

The first speaker turns to her.

“I think the learner understood why the first example mattered before being able to give a faithful sentence. The mistake with the cup helped make the missing distinction clearer.”

“Then an unsuccessful attempt can be intelligible even before the person knows exactly how to correct it,” says the visitor.

“Yes,” says the second. “I knew the cup example was wrong as soon as I heard you hesitate. I didn't know yet what would be right.”

They stand and look across the beds.

The visitor asks, “Have the two of you found an exact equivalent between your languages?”

“No,” says the first speaker.

“Has that prevented the second from learning to use the word directly?”

“No.”

“Then what has been shared?”

“Not a matching word,” says the second. “Something I can recognize, and a way of saying it that belongs to another language.”

The first speaker looks pleased.

“Will you always need to remember my explanation before you say it?”

“Not now. When the situation is right, I think I can use it without translating.”

The visitor begins to collect the remaining tools.

“Then a particular achievement is complete. The whole language has not been mastered; every possible use has not been settled. But the new expression can now participate in your conversation.”

The second speaker watches the young plants.

“There's something else I want to ask,” she says.

“What?”

“Now that I can use this word, what else can we discover together?”

The first speaker points towards the stones bordering the garden.

“Let's begin there tomorrow.”

The second says the newly learned word once more, without translating it.

The first understands.

They remain a little longer before leaving.

Chapter XVIII — What the Answer Makes Possible

The two speakers are still in the garden.

The second has used the word she learned from the first. For once, she has not needed to search for an equivalent in her own language. They stand looking at what they have made.

“What is this?” asks the first speaker.

“A garden.”

“And what else?”

The second turns towards her. “Are you suggesting it isn't a garden?”

“Not at all. I wondered what you saw when you answered.”

“A boundary, the beds, the little path between them. And a great deal of work.”

The first laughs. “You answered my question. You have also given me several others.”

“Such as?”

She points to one of the stones.

“Who placed that there?”

“You did.”

“When?”

“Yesterday. Before the rain.”

“Why?”

“To keep the earth from slipping out of the bed.”

They look down. A thin thread of soil lies beyond the boundary.

“Did it work?” asks the first.

“Not entirely.”

The second kneels. She had meant to straighten the stone; instead she brushes the soil away and finds a narrow space beneath it.

“That is odd. I thought the stone had merely shifted.”

“What do you think now?”

“I don't know whether it shifted at all. The water may have carried the soil beneath it.”

“Can we tell from what we have seen?”

“Not yet.”

The second moves along the edge. Beneath two more stones the earth has been worn away.

“Then perhaps it is not one stone's fault,” she says.

The first comes closer.

“Was your answer about who placed the first stone wrong?”

“No. You placed it.”

“And the answer about why?”

“I said why you intended to place it. I didn't say your intention had succeeded.”

“So the first answers can remain right while a later question discovers a difficulty they did not settle.”

The second rubs the damp earth between her fingers.

“I knew who placed the stone. Then I knew why. But I only thought to ask whether the boundary held when I saw the soil outside it.”

“Could you have asked the question earlier?”

“Of course. But I had no reason to look there until we noticed the gap.”

“Then some questions are not merely left waiting in a queue,” says the first. “One answer can give another question its point.”

The second looks across the bed.

“And the new question may change the way I see the first stone. I thought I was dealing with one crooked piece. Now I want to know how water travels along the whole edge.”

“Will setting one stone upright answer that?”

“No. Nor do I know whether setting all of them upright would help.”

She begins to lift one.

The first holds out a hand.

“Before we change anything, what would we need to find out?”

“Where the water comes from, and where it goes.”

“Can we discover that while the rain has stopped?”

The second thinks of the jug near the shelter.

“We could bring water tomorrow and watch.”

She puts the stone back.

“We've not repaired the boundary,” she says.

“No.”

“Yet this wasn't a useless morning.”

“What has been accomplished?”

“We know a better question. And we know why that question matters to the work.”

The first sits beside the bed.

“I had thought we were only speaking of the word you learned. Then we spoke of the garden, then one stone, then yesterday, and now the movement of water. We haven't travelled far.”

“We haven't needed to. Each thing we understood showed us something else worth attending to.”

“Did the questions make the water move?”

“No. It could wash the soil away whether or not we understood it.”

“Then discovering the right question is our achievement, not the creation of the difficulty.”

The second takes the empty jug from beneath the bench.

“Shall I fill this now?”

“For tomorrow?”

“Yes. If we return, we'll know what to look for.”

The first gets up.

“We have finished something today without finishing everything.”

“That's becoming a familiar distinction.”

“Is it becoming tiresome?”

The second smiles. “Only when you ask it instead of helping carry the jug.”

They leave the stones as they found them.

The boundary still needs attention. The next visit has a purpose it did not have that morning.

Chapter XIX — What Tomorrow Receives

The next morning one speaker brings the jug and the other a small stick for marking the soil.

“What are we trying to find out?” asks the first.

“Where the water goes when it reaches these stones,” says the second.

“Yesterday is over. Why have we come back?”

The second looks at her.

“Because the difficulty is still here. And because we worked out what to investigate.”

“Must we remember every word we used yesterday?”

“No. We need to know what we found and what remained unsettled.”

She pours a little water above the nearest stone. It disappears into the ground. Beside the next stone it travels along the surface, carrying a fine line of soil.

The first kneels to watch.

“That's different.”

“Yes. I thought the water might be going beneath the whole row in the same way.”

“And now?”

“It doesn't seem to. Here it soaks in; there it runs along the edge.”

“Did we know that yesterday?”

“No. We knew only that soil had escaped in several places. Now we have begun to see how.”

They repeat the pouring. The result is similar, though not identical. The second marks the two places with the stick.

“Will the mark tell someone exactly what we saw?” asks the first.

“Not by itself. I should tell them what it marks.”

“Would the mark be the discovery?”

“No. It helps us find the places where we made it.”

They sit beside the damp earth.

“Suppose someone came here to water the plants,” says the first. “They might pour the jug in the same place. Would they have carried out our investigation?”

“Not necessarily. They might only be watering.”

“So what made our pouring an inquiry?”

“We were looking for an answer to what happened beneath the boundary.”

The first thinks for a while.

“Then we have carried something from yesterday into today. Not yesterday itself, and not necessarily its very words.”

“The question and what we already learned,” says the second. “Yesterday showed us where to look.”

“And today's observation?”

“It tells us something new. The slope seems to matter.”

She points towards the higher end of the bed.

“Do you think the water would follow the same route if we changed the ground there?”

“We would have to try.”

“Then the question about repairing the boundary is still open.”

“Yes. But the question about where the water went in these places has received an answer.”

The first picks up a small clod of earth.

“Could another person come tomorrow and understand what we found?”

“We could show them the marks and pour water again.”

“Must they have watched yesterday's rain?”

“No.”

“Would their act of seeing be your act of seeing, occurring a second time?”

The second shakes her head.

“They would see it for themselves. And perhaps notice something we missed.”

“Could they correct us?”

“I hope they would, if we were wrong.”

“What would make their account a correction instead of merely another description?”

“They would have to show which part of what we claimed was mistaken.”

The first sets down the clod.

“I want to distinguish something. Yesterday's event has passed. Today's observation is another event. But what we found yesterday can still help us understand today's.”

“Isn't that obvious?”

“It is when you carry the jug. But consider what happens when the people who discovered a thing are no longer here. If what was understood depended entirely on the first moment of recognition, what could anyone else inherit?”

The second looks at the stick marks.

“Not much.”

“Yet you have not repeated yesterday's recognition. You have done something with it.”

“Yes. And the marks may help someone do something further when we are gone.”

“Does that mean an understanding must be an instant, a point on the clock?”

“Our understanding took time yesterday. It took time again today.”

“Then what shall we mean when we speak of a moment of understanding?”

The second considers.

“An occasion in which something comes to be clear enough for what we're doing. It need not happen in one second.”

“And what remains?”

“Not the whole occasion. Something achieved in it can remain available.”

She lifts the stick and examines the marks.

“Perhaps tomorrow we can alter the slope a little and see whether the soil stays.”

“Will that be the same undertaking as watching the water?”

“It's connected, but not the same. We have seen a route the water takes; we have not yet put the difficulty right.”

“Could someone repair the boundary without knowing every detail of the water's movement?”

“Possibly. But we'd still have to find out whether the repair held.”

They rise, leaving the marks in place.

The first asks, “If another person came tomorrow, what would have to remain the same for them to understand what we discovered?”

“The words?”

“Must they?”

“No. We could draw it, or show them the water.”

“The stones?”

“Not necessarily. Another garden might have the same trouble.”

“Then what must they recognize?”

The second looks back at the wet line beneath the stones.

“I don't know how to answer that exactly.”

They walk home with the empty jug.

The question is not answered. But it is no longer vague: they want to know how someone who was absent could recognize the very determination their investigation has made available.

Chapter XX — The Same Thing, Understood Again

The following morning, a third person visits the garden.

She has not seen the rain or the water poured along the boundary. One speaker shows her the marks, now faint, and the spaces beneath several stones.

“We discovered that water was carrying soil through here,” she explains.

The visitor crouches down.

“I can see soil outside the bed. How do you know the water carried it? Someone might have moved it with a tool.”

“We saw where the water ran when we poured it yesterday,” says the second speaker. “Would you like us to show you?”

They fetch the jug and pour.

The visitor watches the water follow the slope.

“Now I see what you mean. It passes beneath this stone and carries loose soil out.”

The first speaker smiles.

“Do you understand what we discovered?”

“In part. But you told me something I hadn't known: the water moved this way. And I've noticed something else. The soil here is looser than the soil farther along.”

“We hadn't compared those places,” says the second.

“Then have I failed to understand your finding because I have seen something more?”

The first considers.

“No. You could understand what we found and discover something we missed.”

“And if I simply repeated your words without looking?”

“You might still imagine the soil had been pushed out by somebody digging,” says the second. “The words alone wouldn't tell us whether you understood what we meant.”

The visitor presses the soil between her fingers.

“I see. The subject isn't enough either. A person could stand in this garden all day and investigate the insects rather than the movement of the water.”

“What is it we need to preserve, then?” asks the first.

“The particular finding: the water crossed beneath the stones and carried soil with it. We may learn more about why it followed that route without losing the finding.”

She takes a stone from the path and taps it against another.

“This makes a hollow sound. I suppose it is hollow.”

The second takes it.

“Perhaps. But the sound could have another cause.”

They inspect the stone. It is solid.

“I was wrong,” says the visitor.

“Was it wrong that you heard the sound?” asks the first.

“No. I heard it. I was wrong about what produced it.”

“Would it help to say that your later understanding merely added a further fact?”

“No. It corrected what I inferred.”

They put the stone back.

The visitor turns to the garden.

“Then there are two different ways later understanding can change the earlier. I can keep what was established and learn something more. Or I can discover that I claimed something the evidence did not support.”

“And if you meet another person,” says the second, “how can you tell whether their account preserves our finding?”

“Not by its having the same words. I'd ask what it claims about the soil, and what would show that claim to be true or false.”

The first draws a quick sketch in the dirt. It shows water travelling over the tops of the stones.

“Would this be a faithful drawing?”

“No,” says the visitor. “It puts the water where we did not observe it.”

She draws another, showing the water beneath the stones but rising towards higher ground.

“And this?”

“That changes its direction.”

“Yet neither drawing uses a sentence.”

“The difference still matters,” says the visitor. “There is something the drawings are answerable to.”

For a while they watch the water settle.

“Then what would it mean,” the first asks, “for somebody elsewhere to discover the same thing?”

The visitor hesitates.

“Perhaps we should be careful. Somebody might discover water taking soil from beneath another boundary. That would not be the same particular rain or the same garden. Yet it could make the same kind of relationship clear.”

“And suppose their explanation used a language none of us knew?”

“The relationship wouldn't become a different one simply because the words were different.”

“Could the explanation nevertheless be mistaken?”

“Certainly. We would have to find a way to examine what it claims.”

The second looks at the channel.

“So we have not explained every kind of sameness. There is the particular occurrence, what we found about it, and a relationship that might be recognized elsewhere.”

“But we have learned something,” says the visitor. “Faithfulness to an earlier understanding is not the repetition of its sounds, nor merely seeing the same object. It means remaining answerable to what was actually established.”

The first begins to gather the tools.

“And when a new discovery proves an earlier claim wrong?”

“We should correct that claim and preserve what was truly learned.”

They stand at the edge of the bed. The water has not needed their words to follow the slope, yet their words have helped them examine one another's understanding of it.

The visitor looks at the path.

“Tomorrow I may come back with a different question. I hope you won't make me repeat this whole conversation.”

“Only if you forget what we have established,” says the second.

“Then what shall we ask next?”

“Perhaps when a condition was once rightly governing a choice,” says the first, “but no longer is.”

The visitor looks towards the soft ground where they carried water.

“That,” she says, “may be worth finding out before someone steps there.”

Chapter XXI — What No Longer Decides

The following morning, the three return to the garden.

A child has placed a small board across the damp ground.

“Don't step there,” the child says. “The soil is too soft.”

The visitor steps back.

“Was the child right?”

The first speaker kneels and presses a hand against the ground.

“Yesterday, certainly. We could scarcely stand here without leaving deep marks.”

“And now?”

“The surface still yields. We should go around.”

They carry the water along the longer path.

Later, after the sun has warmed the garden, the child returns. The board is still there, but the soil beside it is firm.

“Can we cross now?” the visitor asks.

The child removes the board, presses a heel into the ground and crosses without sinking.

“Yesterday you told us not to step there,” the visitor says.

“I know.”

“Were you mistaken?”

The child looks at the dry earth, then at the visitor.

“No. It was wet yesterday.”

“And are you mistaken to cross it now?”

“No. It isn't wet like that now.”

The visitor turns to the others.

“We have heard two directions from the same child: do not cross, and now you may cross. Must one of them be wrong?”

“Only if they were given for the same ground under the same conditions,” says the second speaker.

“So the earlier direction can remain correct?”

“Yes.”

“Yet it no longer tells us what to do?”

“Not today.”

They watch the child carry the board to the edge of the garden.

“I have been calling such a change a correction,” says the visitor. “But that cannot be quite right.”

“What would you correct?”

“Perhaps the earlier instruction.”

“Would you tell the child that yesterday the ground was not soft?”

“No. We saw it ourselves.”

“Would you tell the child it was wrong to warn us?”

“No.”

“Then what has changed?”

“The ground. And with it, what the warning requires of us.”

The first speaker picks up a stone and lays it beside the path.

“Suppose we kept the board there even after the ground had dried, merely because the warning was once needed. What would we be doing?”

“Treating the earlier condition as though it still governed the crossing.”

“And if we threw the board away, declaring that the warning had always been foolish?”

“We would lose the reason for it.”

“Can we do neither?”

“Yes. We can remember why the crossing was unsafe and recognize why that reason no longer decides this crossing.”

The child returns with a handful of seeds.

“May I plant here?”

The first speaker examines the soil.

“Not too close to the stones. The water passed beneath them yesterday.”

The child stops.

“Will it always pass there?”

“That is what we are still learning.”

The visitor smiles.

“So we have not finished every question about this place.”

“No.”

“But we have finished the question of whether we may cross it now?”

“For the conditions before us, yes.”

“And yesterday's warning has not been erased by today's answer?”

“No.”

The visitor is quiet for a while.

“I think I see something that troubled me when we spoke of the same understanding passing from one moment to another. I imagined that remaining faithful meant continuing to obey every earlier conclusion.”

“Can an earlier conclusion remain faithful to its own occasion without commanding every later occasion?”

“Here it can.”

“And how do you know?”

“Because the warning answered to the softness of the ground. That was what made it appropriate. When the ground changed, the warning's reason ceased to decide what we should do. The reason did not cease to have been real.”

The second speaker sets down the jug.

“Now suppose someone had told us yesterday that the ground was soft when it was actually dry. Would today's crossing be the same sort of change?”

“No. We would have discovered that the earlier warning was mistaken.”

“What if yesterday the ground was soft, but today a bridge had been built above it?”

“The old difficulty would still be there beneath the bridge, but it might no longer prevent us from passing.”

“Must the obstacle disappear for the undertaking to be complete?”

“No.”

“And if we call all three cases a correction?”

“We lose the difference between a false judgment, a changed condition, and a way forward that makes the old obstacle no longer decisive.”

The first speaker nods toward the child, who has started arranging seeds in small groups.

“Can this distinction help us understand more than paths?”

The visitor thinks of the untranslatable word they learned in the garden.

“A word may be suitable in one exchange and unsuitable in another, even when the earlier use was good.”

“And a rule?”

“It may be needed for one undertaking but not another.”

“And a discovery?”

“It can remain established while a new question demands more than it established.”

“Then what must we preserve if we want to understand yesterday faithfully?”

“Not merely the instruction. We must preserve what it was answering to.”

“And what must we examine before carrying it into today?”

“Whether those conditions still matter to what we are doing now.”

The child calls out.

“The small seeds should go nearer the fence. The others need more space.”

“Why?” the visitor asks.

“Because those will grow wider.”

“Has the child answered every question about the garden?”

“No.”

“Has the child discovered something that may govern the planting?”

“Yes.”

“Will it govern every planting, in every garden?”

“Not without looking at what is being planted and where.”

The visitor takes up the jug.

“Yesterday's understanding has not vanished. Nor must we surrender today's observation to yesterday's instruction. Perhaps to remain faithful is to know what an achievement was enough for, and what it is enough for now.”

The first speaker looks at the path where the board had been.

“Then we have found something more exact than either repetition or rejection.”

“What?”

“An earlier condition may stop governing without having been false. And what it once made intelligible can remain available to us.”

They cross the path together.

The crossing does not repair yesterday.

It does not make yesterday happen again.

It belongs to today, and yesterday has helped them understand why.

Chapter XXII — What We Can Begin From

The garden is nearly planted.

One patch near the low boundary remains empty. The child has left the seeds in a folded corner of cloth beside it.

“Why not plant there?” asks the visitor.

“Because the water might gather there again. I want to see what happens in another rain.”

“Is the rest of the garden unfinished because of that patch?”

“No. Those rows are planted.”

The visitor looks along the beds. She had been about to say that the work was not complete, but the child has made her hesitate.

“Then there is something you have done, and something you have decided not yet to do.”

“Yes. And it matters which is which.”

The second speaker takes out the drawing made after they poured water along the stones. The arrows show the places they observed it flowing.

“Could this help someone who came tomorrow?” she asks.

The child studies the drawing.

“It shows where the water went when we tried it.”

“Does it tell us everything the next rain will do?”

“No. It may rain harder. The ground might change.”

“And if someone asked how we knew the water took that route?”

“I'd have to tell them what we watched. Or show them again.”

The visitor sits beside the empty patch.

“So there is a difference between possessing a drawing of a result and knowing how the result was established.”

“And between what the drawing says and what tomorrow will bring,” says the first speaker.

The child picks up a few seeds.

“Couldn't a new person just trust us?”

“For some things, perhaps,” says the visitor. “But if the person needed to decide whether to build a wall here, might they need to know more?”

“They'd want to see where the water goes.”

“And if they discovered that we had read the marks wrongly?”

“We should change what we say,” says the child.

“Would that make all the plants we have already set in the ground disappear?”

“Of course not.”

The first speaker looks along the rows.

“Then we need not treat every unfinished question as a failure of the work that has been completed. Nor need we defend an old explanation when new evidence shows it was mistaken.”

The visitor rolls the drawing carefully.

“I used to think clarity would mean no more questions. Now I can say what we found, how we found it, and what we still need to discover. That seems a better kind of clarity.”

The child returns the seeds to their cloth.

“We'll watch this place when it rains,” the child says.

The second speaker stands.

“And suppose it rains tonight, while everyone sleeps?”

“Then in the morning we'll see what it has done,” says the child.

“Would the water have waited for us to watch?”

“No.”

“Could we still learn something from what remained?”

“We could try.”

The visitor looks towards the darkening sky.

“Then tomorrow may give us a question that goes beyond remembering what we already saw.”

The child gathers the tools.

“Good. We have enough questions for today.”

They leave the unplanted patch as it is.

The planted rows are finished for now. The empty patch has a reason for remaining empty. And the drawing, folded into a pocket, may yet help someone understand what the next rain reveals.

Chapter XXIII — Before Anyone Said It

The rain comes in the night.

By morning the garden path is scattered with leaves. A shallow channel has formed beside the stones, and the unplanted patch is filled with water.

The child arrives first.

“I thought it might happen,” the child says.

The visitor, coming behind, takes a look.

“What did you know?”

“That the water would gather here.”

“You suspected it,” says the visitor. “And now?”

“Now I can see it.”

The first speaker arrives with a spade. She stands at the edge of the water and sees the channel running from the higher ground to the unplanted patch.

“No one was here when the rain began,” she says.

“Not even the child?” asks the visitor.

“I was asleep,” the child answers. “But the puddle came anyway.”

“Then perhaps we should ask something,” the visitor says. “When did the water find its way down here?”

“During the night.”

“When did we discover the way it took?”

“This morning.”

“Are those the same event?”

“No.”

The visitor kneels to examine the channel. There are places where the water has carried fine soil, leaving the larger stones exposed. A small twig has lodged against one of them.

“How can we tell which way the water went?”

The child points from the higher ground to the lower patch.

“It came from there. It left the little stones behind and brought the loose soil here.”

“Is that all we know?”

The first speaker shakes her head. “We can see where soil was displaced. We have good reason to think the water came along this channel. We did not watch every drop.”

The child looks up. “But you can still know something about it.”

“Yes,” says the speaker. “We can know what the marks are enough to tell us, and leave aside what they do not tell us.”

The visitor lifts the twig.

“Suppose the channel had formed in a part of the garden no one ever visited. Would the water have needed us in order to run down the slope?”

“No.”

“And if a person came a year later and found the marks?”

“They might work out what happened,” says the child.

“Would their discovery make the water run at that earlier time?”

The child laughs. “It would be rather late for that.”

The visitor smiles, but does not let the matter rest.

“Then we must distinguish the event, the traces it leaves, and the understanding someone may later achieve.”

“We have distinguished those before,” says the first speaker.

“We have. But there is something further here. We speak as though a relationship could be understood long after the occurrence from which we learn of it.”

The second speaker, who has been watching the little pool, answers.

“Sometimes it can. But we must not pretend every explanation found afterward was already waiting in the stones.”

“What do you mean?”

“We can say the ground sloped, the rain fell, and water moved downhill. The movement did not wait for our account. But the words we use to describe it, the questions we choose, and the explanation we put together are our work.”

“Does that make the movement depend upon the explanation?”

“No. Nor does the movement tell us everything we might wish to explain.”

The child begins clearing leaves from the lower end of the channel.

“Wait,” says the first speaker. “What are you trying to do?”

“Let the puddle drain.”

“Then you have begun a new undertaking. Has the investigation of the water's earlier movement finished?”

“Enough to know where to make a way out,” says the child.

“Is the way out made?”

“Not yet.”

The visitor points to the child's muddy hands.

“A moment ago you were looking at traces. Now you are trying to change the ground. Did the earlier discovery itself drain the water?”

“No.”

“Did it help you choose where to work?”

“Yes.”

“So what was achieved this morning?”

The child pauses. “We found where the water went. And now we know where it might go if we clear the leaves.”

“Those are two things,” says the visitor.

“And neither is the rain itself,” adds the second speaker.

The child looks from one adult to another. “Are we going to talk about it all day, or can I move the leaves?”

“You can move the leaves,” says the first speaker, and picks up the spade.

They work for a while.

The water begins to trickle into a shallow bed beyond the stones. The puddle falls, slowly enough that they can watch the wet line withdraw.

The visitor stands.

“What we discovered was not produced by the words we found for it,” she says. “Yet without understanding what mattered in the marks, we might have dug in the wrong place.”

“And without the words?” asks the first speaker.

“We might still have noticed and acted. But the words have let us show the child what we noticed, ask whether we were right, and explain it to someone absent.”

The second speaker takes up the twig and sets it beside the channel.

“Then let us keep the distinction. Something may happen without being understood. Something may be understood without being spoken. And something spoken may still be mistaken.”

“Yes.”

“Does the same follow for every thing we might call intelligible?”

The visitor considers the question.

“Not all at once. We have examined an ordinary relationship in the ground. We should not claim to have explained all mathematics or all human understanding because water flows downhill.”

“Then what have we earned?”

“That we can distinguish the occurrence of a relationship from the occasion on which someone recognizes it. Our recognition may be later, but it can still answer to what happened.”

The child, satisfied that the puddle is draining, plants the first seed in the formerly empty patch.

“Now there is something else,” the child says.

“What?”

“We know what to do here.”

The first speaker looks at the dark soil.

“And when someone asks how we found out?”

“We can tell them about the rain,” says the child. “Even though we were asleep when it came.”

The visitor looks toward the open path.

“We have been speaking of what can be understood again. Now we have reached something that does not need to have been understood the first time in order to become understandable afterward.”

“And that,” says the second speaker, “raises a more difficult question.”

“What question?”

“How shall we tell when different explanations preserve the very relationship they claim to describe?”

The child presses soil gently over the seed.

“One question at a time,” the child says.

This time no one objects.

Chapter XXIV — The Corner That Stayed Right

The carpenter is repairing a small table beside the shelter.

One leg has become loose, and the table rocks when a cup is placed upon it.

The child brings the carpenter a piece of wood cut into a triangle.

“This will keep it from wobbling,” the child says.

“Where shall it go?” asks the carpenter.

“Between the leg and the underside of the table.”

The carpenter holds the piece against the corner. It does not fit. One angle is too wide.

The visitor, who has come from the garden, watches the child turn the wood over.

“Does turning it change its angles?”

“No,” the child says. “I was hoping it would fit the other way.”

The carpenter places a square against the table leg.

“Look. This is the corner we must fit.”

The child compares the piece of wood with the square.

“So this piece is wrong.”

“For this corner,” says the carpenter.

The first speaker arrives carrying two lengths of string.

“Before you cut another piece, may we try something?”

She lays the strings along the two edges of the carpenter's square and marks their lengths. The carpenter draws a right-angled triangle on a scrap of board.

“Now suppose we know the lengths of the two sides that meet at the corner,” she says. “Could we find how long the side across from that corner must be?”

The child measures the drawing.

“By measuring it.”

“Yes. But what if the triangle is not drawn yet, or is much too large to measure?”

“Then I wouldn't know.”

The visitor asks, “Could there be a relationship between the lengths, whether or not we have drawn that particular triangle?”

The carpenter takes a piece of chalk and makes three squares along the sides of the drawing.

The child counts the little units in the squares.

“In this drawing, the two smaller squares together have the same area as the big one.”

“Only in this drawing?” asks the visitor.

“I don't know.”

“Good,” says the carpenter. “Don't answer before looking.”

They draw another right-angled triangle, with different side lengths, and compare the squares again.

The child takes longer this time. One of the squares crosses the edge of the board. They fetch another piece and continue.

“It seems to happen again,” the child says at last.

“The drawings suggest a pattern,” says the carpenter, “but two examples are not a proof that it holds for every right triangle. We would still need to establish why it must hold.”

“Does the second triangle have the same lengths as the first?” asks the visitor.

“No.”

“Does it have the same appearance?”

“Not really.”

“What is the same?”

“The way the square on the long side is related to the other two.”

The carpenter nods.

“And if we built a triangle with sides that did not meet at a right angle?”

The child studies the square again.

“Then I couldn't just use what we found without checking.”

“Why not?”

“Because the right corner mattered.”

The visitor turns to the first speaker.

“We have spoken before of this mathematical relationship. But now we have encountered the difference between remembering its words and knowing what lets us use it.”

The first speaker takes the chalk.

“Suppose I explain the relationship to you without drawing any squares. And suppose the carpenter shows you a different proof with pieces of wood. Must our explanations be identical?”

“No,” says the child.

“Could one explanation be mistaken?”

“Yes. It might measure badly, or use a triangle that isn't right-angled.”

“Then how would we discover the mistake?”

“By checking what it was trying to establish.”

The visitor asks the child another question.

“Imagine someone who speaks a language you do not know. They draw the same kinds of triangles and show you the areas. You understand what the drawing demonstrates. Have you learned their language?”

“No.”

“Have you understood something through their drawing?”

“Yes.”

“Would the relationship begin to be true only when you found words for it in your language?”

The child is busy comparing the two drawings.

“No. The squares would still fit.”

The carpenter interjects.

“Careful. We do not want to say that every rough wooden square gives a perfect demonstration. The pieces may be cut badly.”

“But a badly cut piece can be wrong about the shape,” the child says, “not make the relationship wrong.”

“That is the distinction,” says the carpenter. “A drawing helps us see. The relationship tells us what a correct drawing or proof must answer to.”

The visitor looks toward the table.

“So we have something more exact than an ancient sentence handed from one person to another.”

“What?” asks the first speaker.

“A relationship that different people can try to establish, express, test, and use. Their acts of understanding differ. Their diagrams differ. But they need not discover different relationships.”

“Does that mean every comparison of two examples reveals an invariant?”

“No. We had to identify what remained: the relation among the areas, under the right-angle condition. Merely saying the triangles look alike would not have been enough.”

The child interrupts.

“Are we going to fix the table?”

The carpenter laughs. “That is still our undertaking.”

He measures the corner again, marks a new piece of wood, and cuts it. This time the triangular brace fits closely. He fastens it to the leg, presses a hand against the tabletop, and then sets the cup down.

The table stands without rocking.

“Did the drawing repair the table?” asks the visitor.

“No,” says the child. “We had to cut and put the piece in.”

“Then was the drawing useless?”

“No. We knew what length would fit before we cut it.”

The first speaker wipes chalk from her fingers.

“The mathematical achievement and the practical repair are not the same completion. One can help bring about the other.”

The carpenter tests the table once more.

“And if we had cut the piece wrongly?”

“A correct geometrical relation would not make our cutting accurate,” says the child. “Our work would need correction.”

The visitor looks at the two triangles on the scrap board.

“Today we have learned something about how an achievement crosses between people. It is not enough that one repeats a statement, nor that two drawings have the same subject. We must be able to recognize what relationship is claimed and what would make an attempted demonstration faithful or mistaken.”

The second speaker joins them.

“Does that mean the world has given us one expression that all people must use?”

“No.”

“One history they must repeat?”

“No.”

“One method they must always prefer?”

“No.”

“Then what have they in common?”

The child puts a finger on the square drawn against the longest side.

“Something they can all be right or wrong about.”

No one answers immediately.

The table is steady. The chalk lines remain on the board. The child takes the cup away without spilling it.

The visitor says quietly, “If people can be answerable to the same relationship without sharing its first expression, perhaps they can also share a question before they share an answer.”

“Then let us find a case,” says the first speaker, “in which they do not agree.”

Chapter XXV — The Crossing We Disagreed About

The wooden footbridge near the garden has been damaged by rain.

A plank has lifted at one end. Its fastening still holds, but the board bends when the carpenter presses on it.

Two neighbours have stopped beside the bridge.

“We can cross,” says the first. “It held me yesterday.”

“No one should cross until it is fixed,” says the second.

The visitor, approaching with the child, hears the disagreement.

“What are you arguing about?” she asks.

“Whether the bridge is safe,” says the first neighbour.

“Whether we ought to let children use it,” says the second.

The visitor looks from one to the other.

“Those may be different questions.”

“They are the same bridge,” says the first neighbour.

“Yes,” says the visitor. “But are you asking exactly the same thing of it?”

The first neighbour tries again.

“It carried my weight yesterday. I believe it can carry someone across today.”

The second points to the lifted plank.

“I am saying we should not send the children onto it unless we know it will hold them.”

“So you disagree,” says the visitor. “But perhaps not yet in the same respect.”

The carpenter, who has followed them from the shelter, squats beside the plank.

“Yesterday's crossing tells us something,” he says. “It tells us the bridge carried one person under yesterday's conditions. It does not tell us everything we need to know about this fastening after the rain.”

The first neighbour frowns.

“So my experience counts for nothing?”

“No,” says the carpenter. “It counts for what it established.”

The second neighbour is still impatient.

“And while you discuss it, someone may fall.”

“Then keep everyone off the bridge for now,” the carpenter says. “We can examine it without crossing.”

The child helps lay a branch across the entrance as a warning.

The first neighbour watches the work.

“I thought you were saying the bridge had already failed.”

“I wasn't,” says the second. “I was saying we don't know enough to let the children use it.”

“And I thought yesterday's success was enough.”

“It was enough to show you crossed yesterday,” the visitor says. “Now we need to ask what would be enough to establish the bridge's condition today.”

The first neighbour comes closer.

“What do you need to see?”

The carpenter shows them the loose fastening and the cracked timber beneath it.

“Look here. We can find out whether the plank is still supported, and whether the part holding the fastening has split.”

They examine it together. The first neighbour notices a second crack that was hidden by a strip of mud.

“I did not see that yesterday.”

“Perhaps it was hidden,” says the carpenter. “Perhaps it has widened since. We don't yet know which.”

The second neighbour points beneath the plank.

“Would that be enough to tell us not to cross?”

“It is enough to stop us treating the bridge as ready for use,” says the carpenter. “Whether the whole plank must be replaced is another matter.”

The visitor asks, “Have the neighbours reached agreement?”

“About not crossing for now,” says the child.

“Has their agreement repaired the bridge?”

“No.”

“Have they discovered everything about the damage?”

“No.”

“But have they found something they can investigate together?”

The first neighbour answers before the child can.

“Yes. We can look at the same fastening and ask whether it will hold.”

The second neighbour adds, “And we can disagree about what the answer is without pretending the fastening means something entirely different to each of us.”

The visitor nods.

“Then shared understanding is not the same as agreeing.”

“Nor is agreement the same as being right,” says the carpenter.

They carry tools from the shelter. The carpenter loosens the plank, exposing the timber beneath.

The first neighbour holds the board steady while the second removes a bent nail.

A new support must be cut and fitted. They measure it twice. The first measurement is wrong because the ruler's end has broken away.

“Now that,” says the first neighbour, “was a mistake.”

“What was?” asks the child.

“Our measurement. We treated the broken end of the ruler as though it marked the beginning.”

“And yesterday's crossing?” asks the visitor.

“Not a mistake. I really crossed. I mistook what that success entitled me to say about today.”

The carpenter sets aside the ruler.

“That's worth remembering.”

After the new support is fitted, the carpenter tests it carefully. The first neighbour asks to cross; the carpenter makes them wait while he checks the other side.

When the examination is complete, the barrier is moved aside.

The second neighbour crosses first, slowly, and comes back.

“Well?” says the first.

“It holds under the test we made. I would let the children cross now.”

The visitor smiles.

“Has the second neighbour changed her mind?”

“Yes.”

“Was her earlier refusal wrong?”

“No,” says the first neighbour. “Not when the fastening was cracked and we hadn't checked it.”

The child steps onto the bridge, then stops halfway.

“Why are you smiling?”

“Because you understand what changed,” says the visitor.

“I understand that we fixed it. And that they were arguing about different things at first.”

“Can you say what brought them together?”

The child thinks.

“They both wanted the bridge to work. But they had to look at it and find what was wrong.”

“Would wanting the same thing have been enough?”

“No.”

“Would using the same words have been enough?”

“No. They said safe and meant different things by it.”

“So what did they come to share?”

The child taps the plank with a foot.

“What would count as finding out.”

The visitor turns toward the neighbours.

“A shared world is not a world in which everybody sees the same things, believes the same things, or has the same past. These two began from different experiences and different questions. They were able to make their disagreement more exact because the bridge could answer to an inquiry neither could settle by assertion alone.”

The first neighbour looks over the side.

“The bridge did not vote for either of us.”

The second laughs. “And I hope it never will.”

They walk across together.

At the far end the child looks back.

“Someone built this bridge before I was born,” the child says. “I didn't have to learn how to build a whole bridge before I could cross it.”

The carpenter looks at the new support.

“No. But when it failed, we needed to find out what could still be trusted, and what needed doing again.”

The visitor remains beside the bridge.

“Then there is a further question.”

“About the bridge?” asks the child.

“About all the things we use without having discovered them ourselves. A path someone cleared. A rule someone taught. A measurement someone established. A way of building that outlives its builder.”

“Can't we just use them?” asks the child.

“Often we can.”

“And when we can't?”

“We need to know what was established, what still holds, and how to examine it.”

The child glances toward the garden.

“Then someone else has already begun our next question.”

The visitor looks at the bridge, the repaired plank, and the people crossing it.

“Yes,” she says. “Many people have.”

Chapter XXVI — What Nobody Has to Discover Again

The next day, the child returns to the bridge with a folded piece of paper.

“Look what I found,” the child says.

It is a drawing, somewhat faded, of the bridge as it was first built. There are numbers beside the planks and lines showing where the supports belong.

“Where did you find it?” asks the carpenter.

“In the box of old tools. Someone put it beneath the lid.”

The carpenter unfolds the drawing across a dry board.

“I remember this,” he says. “The person who built the bridge made it.”

“Are all the numbers right?” asks the child.

“They tell us what was planned. We still have to see whether the bridge was built that way and whether the parts have changed.”

“Then why keep it?”

“It may help us find out.”

The visitor comes closer.

“Suppose the builder had left no drawing. Would we know nothing about how the bridge stands?”

“We could examine it,” says the carpenter. “We could measure its supports and work out what they do.”

“Would we be making the original bridge again?”

“No. We would be discovering how this one was made.”

“And if we followed the drawing without examining the bridge?”

“We might trust a support that has rotted away.”

The child points to a line at the end of the page.

“What does that say?”

The carpenter reads it aloud. It explains why the outer support was set beyond the bank rather than directly above the water.

“I didn't know that,” says the child.

“Neither did I,” says the carpenter. “I had noticed where the support stood, but not why the builder chose that place.”

The visitor considers the drawing.

“Has the person who made it returned to explain?”

“No.”

“Yet we have understood something of what that person understood?”

“Yes.”

“Is the paper itself the understanding?”

The child shakes their head.

“It's only a drawing.”

“Then why is it so useful?”

“Because we can see what the marks mean. And ask whether they are right.”

The carpenter shows the child how the measurements belong together. One gives a length. Another shows the distance between supports. The line at the riverbank gives the reason for a placement that had at first seemed strange.

For a while they say nothing. The child traces the marks with one finger, then compares them with the bridge.

“Here,” the child says. “The drawing shows another support underneath, but I can't see it.”

“Come around the bank,” says the carpenter.

They crouch beside the water. A broad beam stands partly hidden by grass.

“It is there,” says the child.

“Does the drawing tell us whether it is still sound?” asks the visitor.

“No. We have to look.”

The carpenter tests the beam. Its surface is weathered, but the wood beneath is firm.

The child stands up.

“So the drawing lets us start with something someone else already learned. But it doesn't do all our work.”

“That seems right,” says the carpenter.

The second speaker has joined them.

“Would you say that we have inherited the bridge?”

“Of course,” says the child.

“And the drawing?”

“Yes.”

“And the builder's understanding?”

The child hesitates.

“Some of it, maybe. We don't know everything the builder knew.”

“What makes you say some of it?”

“We know why that support was placed there. But we don't know why every other thing was done.”

The visitor smiles.

“Then inheritance is not simply having an old object in our hands.”

“No,” says the child. “We have to find out what it tells us.”

The carpenter folds the drawing again.

“Sometimes we can use a thing without knowing how it was discovered. I crossed this bridge for years without seeing the drawing.”

“And today?”

“Today I can explain one choice in its design. If that support ever needs replacing, the explanation may matter.”

The visitor asks, “Which matters more: the bridge, the drawing, or the explanation?”

The carpenter looks at the water.

“I would not answer without knowing what we are trying to do. If we need to cross, the bridge must hold. If we need to repair it, the drawing may guide us. If we need to decide whether the drawing is trustworthy, we must examine what was actually built and how it works.”

“Then one thing cannot do every other thing's work.”

“No.”

They carry the paper back to the shelter. An older neighbour is mending a net beside the entrance.

“Do you remember the person who made the bridge?” asks the child.

“Yes.”

“Did you help?”

“I brought some of the stones. I remember the builder sending us back for larger ones.”

“Why larger?”

“The bank was softer than expected. The first stones sank.”

The carpenter unfolds the drawing.

“Here is the outer support. This note explains why it stands beyond the bank.”

The neighbour studies the page and nods.

“That was after the stones sank. The first drawing would have looked different.”

The visitor looks up.

“Then the page records a choice made after another attempt failed.”

“Yes,” says the neighbour. “We learned something from the ground.”

“And was the failed attempt of no use?”

“It wasn't a sound foundation. But it taught us what would not hold.”

The child takes this seriously.

“So they did not know everything before they began.”

“Not at all,” says the neighbour.

“But we don't have to try the small stones again just to know what happened?”

“We need not repeat that failure. We can examine why it failed.”

“And if the bank has changed?”

“Then we must examine that too.”

The visitor puts a hand on the drawing.

“Here is a curious thing. The builder's work did not finish the work of all later builders. Yet it made something available to them. The bridge can be used. The drawing can be read. The old decision can be examined. The account of the failed stones may prevent someone from repeating a mistake.”

“Unless they don't listen,” says the child.

“Yes,” says the neighbour, laughing. “That can happen.”

The first speaker, who has been quiet, asks, “Does the understanding become less real because someone ignores it?”

“Not necessarily,” says the visitor. “But it may cease to guide what they do.”

“Then what do we mean when we say that an achievement remains available?”

“We mean more than that someone once thought it. We can encounter its result, recover its relation to the undertaking, or use what it has made possible. Sometimes the result survives when the explanation is lost. Sometimes the explanation survives when the original object is gone.”

The child looks toward the bridge.

“If the bridge fell down tomorrow, would the drawing be useless?”

“It might help build another,” says the carpenter. “Or help us learn why this one fell. But not unless we understood what it showed.”

The older neighbour ties off the net.

“And if the drawing were lost?”

“We could still examine the bridge,” says the child. “And we could ask you.”

“For now,” says the neighbour.

The child looks puzzled.

“Why for now?”

“Because I will not always be here.”

No one speaks for a moment.

The visitor asks the neighbour, “What would you want the child to keep from this conversation?”

“Not just that we used larger stones. I would want the child to remember why.”

“And if the child discovers a better way?”

“Then I hope the child uses it.”

The visitor turns to the child.

“Do you see? What was done before you were born can help you, even though you cannot return to the moment when it was done. And what you do may help someone who is not here yet.”

The child looks at the old drawing, then takes a fresh sheet of paper.

“I'll draw where we repaired the plank.”

“Why?” asks the carpenter.

“Because whoever comes next might need to know.”

The child starts drawing, stops, and adds the crack beside the fastening.

“Should I draw the part that broke too?”

“Yes,” says the carpenter. “Otherwise someone might not understand why we replaced it.”

The visitor watches the child at work.

“A bridge passes from one generation to another. So do words, methods, tools, and discoveries. But their passing is not enough by itself. Someone must still be able to recognize what was achieved and where it applies.”

The child finishes the drawing and places it beside the older one.

“Now there are two.”

“Will the next person have to read both?”

“Perhaps, if they want to understand what changed.”

“And if they only need to cross?”

“They can cross, if the bridge is safe.”

The visitor looks at the two pages.

“We have been trying to understand how a completed achievement can become the beginning of another. Here it has happened across lives that do not overlap in every moment.”

The older neighbour rises to put away the net.

“What is so surprising about that?” the neighbour asks.

The visitor considers.

“Perhaps the surprising thing is how ordinary it is.”

The neighbour smiles.

“Then perhaps you are beginning to understand what a village is.”

Chapter XXVII — The World We Can Understand

In the morning the visitor walks alone to the garden.

The bridge is quiet. The path is dry. Beyond the stones, the rows have begun to show small green shoots.

At the far end, the child is trying to open the gate.

The gate catches against the ground. The child pushes once, then stops, kneels, and looks beneath it.

The visitor waits.

The child picks a small stone out of the way. The gate opens.

“You didn't push harder,” says the visitor.

“No. It was catching on the stone.”

The visitor smiles.

“A door again.”

“What?”

“When we began, we asked what it meant for a door to close. Now you have opened a gate.”

The child shrugs.

“It was only a stone.”

“Only a stone,” the visitor repeats.

The first speaker comes along the path carrying the old drawing of the garden.

“Are you speaking about stones again?”

“We seem unable to escape them,” says the visitor.

“Perhaps because we keep leaving them in useful places,” says the child.

They sit beside the gate. The second speaker arrives with a bowl of water for the young plants.

The visitor asks, “Have we ever found the answer to our first question?”

“Which first question?” asks the second speaker.

“What has happened when something becomes clear?”

The child points at the gate.

“I found the stone. I moved it. The gate opened.”

“And if you had not found it?”

“I might have pushed until something broke.”

“Did discovering the stone and opening the gate happen as one thing?”

“Not quite. First I understood what was stopping it.”

“And that understanding made the next action possible.”

“Yes.”

The first speaker sets the drawing on the bench.

“Could you have understood what stopped the gate without saying anything?”

“I did.”

“Could the visitor have understood it if you showed the stone?”

“Yes.”

“And if another child came tomorrow, unable to speak our language?”

The child picks up the stone and moves it back toward the gate, then takes it away again.

“I could show them.”

The visitor watches the movement.

“Then we have come a long way and returned to something very near where we began.”

The second speaker pours water slowly beside a row of plants.

“Not quite the same place,” she says.

“Why not?”

“Because now we understand what made some of our earlier questions worth asking. We know the difference between what happened and what was understood about it. We know an answer can be sufficient without being the last answer. And we know what was achieved can become available again.”

The child watches the water soak into the earth.

“We also know not to put too much water there.”

“Because of the rain?” asks the visitor.

“Because we watched where it collected. And we changed the ground.”

The first speaker points to the drawing.

“Could someone understand what we discovered if they had never seen the rain?”

“Yes. If we showed them what happened and what we learned from it.”

“Could they understand it in different words?”

“Yes.”

“Could they find that part of our explanation was wrong?”

“Yes.”

“And if they did?”

“We would have to examine what we got wrong, not pretend we never learned anything.”

The visitor looks out toward the bridge.

“Yesterday two neighbours disagreed about whether the bridge could be used. Did their disagreement mean that they inhabited different worlds?”

The child laughs.

“They were both standing beside the same bridge.”

“But they did not think the same thing.”

“No.”

“What made it possible for them to find out together?”

“They looked at the fastening.”

“And after that?”

“They fixed what needed fixing. Then they checked it.”

The second speaker sets down the bowl.

“Could they have disagreed even after examining it?”

“Of course,” says the visitor. “They might have read the evidence differently or wanted different standards of safety.”

“Then what would remain between them?”

“A question they could try to make more exact. Reasons they could examine. Conditions to which their judgments must answer.”

“Would that guarantee agreement?”

“No.”

“Would it make disagreement meaningless?”

“No. It would make it possible to discover what the disagreement was about.”

The child has gone to gather the tools.

The first speaker watches.

“I wonder whether we have been asking the wrong question about what binds people.”

“What question have we been asking?” says the visitor.

“What must people possess in common before they can understand one another?”

“Is that wrong?”

“Not wrong. But perhaps it sends us first to their words, customs, and beliefs. Suppose two people share none of those completely. Must they be unable to discover anything together?”

The visitor thinks of the islanders who once had no common language.

“They can point, show, try, fail, correct, and begin to recognize what matters to a shared undertaking.”

“Do they have to become the same person?”

“No.”

“Have the same experience?”

“No.”

“Agree about everything?”

“No.”

“Then what must be possible?”

The visitor answers carefully.

“Something must be able to become intelligible between them. A distinction one recognizes must be available, somehow, for another to recognize or examine. A question must have conditions by which an answer could be enough. An achievement must be capable of being taken up without the first moment happening again.”

The second speaker asks, “Is this what binds them more deeply than their language?”

“In one respect, perhaps. Language can help them share understanding. But the possibility of understanding something together cannot be explained solely by their already possessing the same words.”

“Does that make language unimportant?”

“Not at all. Think of what we could not have done without it: ask why, explain a mistake, tell someone absent, preserve a discovery. Our language is one of the great things we have made from the possibility of understanding.”

“And a village?” asks the first speaker.

“The village depends on more than understanding. People must care for one another, work, disagree, make promises, and keep them. But without something that can become intelligible between them, how would they know what they were promising, or recognize that a task had been done?”

The child returns, dragging a small sack of soil.

“Are you going to help me, or are you going to understand the garden until nightfall?”

They help.

The sack is heavier than it looks. One person holds it while another loosens the cord. The child tips it and spills soil beside the new plants.

“That was too much,” says the child.

“Can we put some back?” asks the first speaker.

They do.

For a while there is no further discussion.

When the last of the soil has been spread, the visitor sits on the edge of the path.

“We have been speaking of things that can be understood before anyone gives them a name. We have seen a relationship discovered through geometry, a bridge tested through disagreement, and a builder's decision made available long after the builder was gone.”

The second speaker joins her.

“Do those all concern the same subject?”

“No. A gate, a triangle, a bridge, and a village are different things.”

“Then why have they belonged to one inquiry?”

“Because each has allowed us to ask what became clear, what made it sufficient, and what remained available for someone else.”

The first speaker says, “And have they all been completed in exactly the same way?”

“No. Sometimes one discovery is decisive. Sometimes a whole arrangement must hold. Sometimes a former obstacle ceases to determine the outcome. What remains can be a recognized result, a relationship on which that result depends, or a capacity that has become available.”

“Can we now claim to have counted every way in which anything can become sufficiently understood?”

The visitor shakes her head.

“No. We have learned how to look for such differences. The counting requires another kind of patient examination.”

“Then have we failed to finish our inquiry?”

“Not this undertaking.”

“What have we finished?”

“We have found a way to recognize why completed understanding matters beyond any single moment. An answer can end one question without ending our ability to ask. What is understood can become available to another person. That person may preserve it, question it, correct it, or discover something further.”

The child sits down beside them.

“Does that mean I will know how to open every gate?”

“No,” says the visitor.

“Then what have I got?”

“You know what stopped this one. And you know to look before you push harder.”

The child considers that.

“That seems useful.”

“It is more than useful,” says the first speaker. “It gives you something from which another understanding can begin.”

The visitor looks toward the bridge, where someone she does not recognize is crossing.

“Who made the first path through this place?” she asks.

“No one here remembers,” says the second speaker.

“And who will walk it after us?”

“We do not know.”

“Yet the path can be followed. The bridge can be examined. The garden can be tended. What someone has discovered can become available to someone who has never met them.”

“Is that what you mean by a common world?” asks the first speaker.

“Partly. Not that we all see it from the same place. Not that we always understand it correctly. But that there is something we can meet, examine, learn from, and make more intelligible together.”

“And if a person arrives tomorrow and asks a question we have never thought of?”

The visitor watches the child carry the empty sack toward the shelter.

“Then perhaps something we have understood will help us hear the question.”

The child calls from the gate.

“Are you coming?”

“Yes,” says the visitor.

The child holds the gate open.

Nothing more needs explaining before they can pass through it.

The visitor goes first, then the others.

Behind them the garden remains, changed by their work and open to further change.

Ahead of them there are things they do not yet understand.

They go together.
