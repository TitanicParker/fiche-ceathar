The centre-first method

A sentence means something, but it also performs or presents one intelligible achievement. We locate that achievement before asking which of the 54 forms may describe it. The full research protocol distinguishes ordinary guided readings from frozen blind assays.

  1. Mark the actual event. Are we examining the sentence's own move, or the event it describes? Do not switch unnoticed.
  2. Ask what changed. What has become distinguishable, possible, related, repaired, integrated, or settled?
  3. Ask what made it sufficient. Is completion focal, distributed, or dependent on a former constraint losing force?
  4. Ask how it remains. Does the completion stand as achieved, depend on an ongoing bearing, or persist as an operative capacity?
  5. Test the nearest rival. Name the genuinely competing reading; do not assign a cell from a keyword.
  6. Apply the completion gate. No completed intelligible event, no coordinate. In a formal test, preserve the independent assay and decoder outputs.

The fifteen definitions and Dimensional Conjunction govern all canonical forms. These blog readings teach recognition and are not presented as blind-test results.

A practical distinction

A Centre is not just a paraphrase

The Centre is not everything the sentence tells us. It is what holds everything the sentence tells us together.

A paraphrase restates the content. An Operative Centre identifies the minimal governing determination that makes that content one achieved intelligibility. It should be precise enough to stand alone and narrow enough that changing the decisive relationship would change the achievement.

How to find it

  1. Read the whole sentence first. Notice its assertion, qualification, contrast, condition or metaphor; do not hunt for a structural label.
  2. What changed? Say what has become intelligibly determinate by the end of the sentence.
  3. What made it sufficient? Identify the relation doing the work: for example, temporal priority, dependence, opposition, equality or an enabling condition.
  4. How does it remain achieved? Name the specific understanding now established, without inventing a physical event the sentence does not assert.
  5. State the Centre in one sentence. Keep the governing relation, including its modality: can is not must; may is not did.
  6. Try to break it. Remove or replace the decisive words. If the achievement remains the same, your proposed Centre may be too broad; if the change alters its identity, you have located a useful diagnostic.
Example A-009 · Ordinary life
We built clocks to measure time, then arranged our lives around them.

True but insufficient paraphrase: People invented clocks and use them to organise their schedules.

What the paraphrase misses: The movement from a tool made for a human purpose to a tool that organises its makers. The word then holds that reversal together.

Operative Centre: The establishment of a reversal in practical dependence whereby instruments created to measure time become structures around which human activity is organised.

Diagnostic: Remove “then arranged our lives around them” and the reversal disappears. A mere account of invention is a different achievement.

Example A-111 · Scientific language
At chemical equilibrium, the forward and reverse reactions occur at equal rates.

True but insufficient paraphrase: The sentence describes what happens in a chemical system at equilibrium.

What the paraphrase misses: Equilibrium is a dynamic equality of two ongoing reaction rates, not an absence of reaction.

Operative Centre: At chemical equilibrium, forward and reverse reactions continue at the same rate.

Diagnostic: Replace equal rates with zero rates. The statement now describes a different condition and loses the achievement of dynamic balance.

What stays the same across both examples? Neither reading depends on a keyword or a 54-cell assignment. In one, the governing relation is a reversal of practical dependence; in the other, it is equality of ongoing processes. Both are recognised by the same three questions and tested by changing what makes the sentence sufficient.

A diagnostic is a test of the reading, not a mechanical proof of uniqueness. Where two genuinely different Centres remain defensible, record the leading one and the strongest alternative for further examination. From ordinary recognition to scientific precision

The written Resolve record now contains 163 consecutive Centre readings. The first hundred show the method across everyday language; the next sixty-three introduce scientific conditions, definitions, mechanisms and quantitative invariants. Here are four lessons drawn from the actual corpus, with the original wording retained.

A-101 · Conditional law
An object moving at constant velocity continues in a straight line unless a net external force acts on it.

The decisive word is unless. The sentence does not assert that an object's velocity can never change; it identifies the condition under which the stated persistence holds.

Operative Centre

An object continues at constant velocity in a straight line unless acted upon by a net external force.

Diagnostic. Deleting “unless” would make persistence unconditional and alter the stated law.

A-111 · Dynamic equilibrium
At chemical equilibrium, the forward and reverse reactions occur at equal rates.

Equilibrium is not the cessation of reaction. The equal rates of two continuing processes constitute the achievement.

Operative Centre

At chemical equilibrium, forward and reverse reactions continue at the same rate.

Diagnostic. Replacing “equal” with “zero” would mistake dynamic equilibrium for no reaction.

A-150 · Evidential inference
The age of some rocks can be estimated from the measured proportions of radioactive parent isotopes and their decay products.

The expression makes an inferential route intelligible. It does not supply the age of a particular rock, and “some” and “can” constrain the reach of the claim.

Operative Centre

The age of certain rocks can be estimated from the measured ratio of radioactive parent isotopes to their decay products.

Diagnostic. Replacing “some” with “all” removes the specified limitation.

A-161 · Mathematical invariant
In a right-angled triangle, the square of the hypotenuse's length equals the sum of the squares of the other two side lengths.

Unlike a possible ecological outcome, this is an exact relationship within a defined geometric class. The right-angle condition is constitutive, not incidental.

Operative Centre

In a right triangle, the hypotenuse length squared equals the sum of the squares of the other two side lengths.

Diagnostic. Removing “right-angled” falsely extends the equality to arbitrary triangles.

INSTALLATION 40The Promise and the EventRead essay + diagnostics ↘

Sentence Craft · Resolve Corpus I · Cinderella 244–253 · written Centre records ↗

A fairy tale advances through promises, warnings and actions. Readers often know where those words will lead. But a sentence that issues a command has not yet narrated obedience, and a sentence that announces a future event has not made that event occur.

The ten sentences here move from a midnight restriction to Cinderella's journey and the prince's attention, and end in a striking difference between seeing someone and recognising her. Each makes a distinct achievement. Their relation is narratively continuous, but continuity must not erase their separate force.

244 · A command without content

The fairy godmother gave her one command.

The command is given, but its content is not yet here. “One” limits its number, and “command” makes it directive rather than friendly advice. A reader who imports the following midnight warning has read ahead.

Operative Centre · 244

The narrative establishment of a single command given by the fairy godmother to Cinderella.

Diagnostic. Replace “one command” with “several suggestions.” The singular obligatory character disappears.

245 · The requirement specified

“You must leave the palace before midnight,” she said.

The instruction now receives its content. “Must” makes departure obligatory; “before midnight” states the deadline. The sentence does not yet explain why the deadline matters, nor does it say Cinderella obeys.

Operative Centre · 245

The establishment of the fairy godmother's command requiring Cinderella to leave the palace before midnight.

Diagnostic. Replace “before midnight” with “whenever you wish.” The time restriction disappears.

246 · The predicted ending

“At the stroke of twelve, the magic will end.”

This is the explanation anticipated by the preceding command, but its achievement is distinct: it fixes a prospective endpoint for the magic. “Will end” is future tense, not a narrated event that has already happened.

Operative Centre · 246

The establishment of a spoken prediction that the magic will end at the stroke of twelve.

Diagnostic. Replace “will end” with “will continue unchanged.” The scheduled termination is reversed.

247 · A promise without fulfilment

Cinderella promised that she would remember.

Here we obtain a commitment, not proof of its future fulfilment. A promise can be sincerely made and still forgotten. The sentence says nothing about later compliance with the deadline.

Operative Centre · 247

The narrative establishment of Cinderella's promise to remember the fairy godmother's warning.

Diagnostic. Replace “promised” with “forgot.” The undertaking disappears.

248 · Journey to the destination

The golden coach carried her to the palace.

The coach's earlier transformation now acquires practical use: it carries Cinderella to the palace. That completes a journey, but it does not yet establish her entry into the ballroom or whom she meets.

Operative Centre · 248

The narrative establishment of Cinderella's conveyance to the palace by the golden coach.

Diagnostic. Replace “to the palace” with “away from the palace.” The journey's destination is changed.

249 · The entrance and the gaze

When she entered the ballroom, everyone turned to look at her.

Entry and reaction are explicitly linked by “when.” The sentence makes collective attention the result of her arrival; it does not explain why all the guests look. “Everyone” establishes the scope within the scene.

Operative Centre · 249

The narrative establishment of Cinderella's ballroom entrance as drawing the gaze of everyone present.

Diagnostic. Replace “everyone turned to look at her” with “no one noticed her.” The entrance remains, but its collective effect vanishes.

250 · An approach with a purpose

The prince saw her and came at once to ask her to dance.

“Saw” is followed by an immediate approach. “To ask” makes the purpose explicit. An invitation intended is not yet an invitation accepted. The next sentence will determine whether the dance occurs.

Operative Centre · 250

The narrative establishment of the prince's immediate approach to Cinderella with the purpose of inviting her to dance.

Diagnostic. Replace the approach with “remained where he was.” Seeing would remain, but the immediate purposive action would not.

251 · The dance accomplished

Cinderella danced with the prince all evening.

Now the shared dance is actually stated. “With the prince” identifies her partner; “all evening” gives duration. Neither love nor an engagement is established. The reader must not let the tale's known conclusion overtake this sentence.

Operative Centre · 251

The narrative fulfilment of dancing between Cinderella and the prince throughout the evening.

Diagnostic. Replace “danced with” with “watched.” Their shared participation disappears.

252 · A judgement held by someone

He thought she was the loveliest and kindest lady he had ever met.

The key qualification is “He thought.” It locates the evaluation within the prince's perspective. The comparison extends over ladies he has met, not every person in existence. The narrator does not independently prove the judgement's truth.

Operative Centre · 252

The narrative establishment of the prince's personal judgement that Cinderella was the loveliest and kindest lady he had encountered.

Diagnostic. Replace “He thought” with “Everyone knew.” Personal assessment becomes collective certainty.

253 · Perception without recognition

Her stepsisters saw her too, but they did not know who she was.

The adversative “but” is decisive. They perceive her and yet fail to identify her. Seeing and recognition are not equivalent. Nor does this sentence tell us why recognition fails.

Operative Centre · 253

The establishment of a contrast between the stepsisters' seeing Cinderella and their failure to recognise her identity.

Diagnostic. Replace “did not know who she was” with “immediately recognised her.” The gap between perception and identity disappears.

The three distinctions that govern this passage

First, speech is not its fulfilment. The fairy godmother's command identifies a requirement; the announcement of midnight supplies a reason to respect it; Cinderella's promise establishes an undertaking. None yet tells us whether she leaves in time. Even the confident “will end” remains a prediction at the point it is uttered.

Second, purpose is not yet accomplished action. The prince approaches in order to ask Cinderella to dance. Only the following sentence establishes their dance. If we collapse the two, we lose the exact distinction between what he intends and what later happens.

Third, a person's perception is not the same as recognition or objective truth. The prince thinks Cinderella is the loveliest and kindest lady he has met; the judgement is his. The stepsisters see Cinderella but fail to identify her. These distinctions are not incidental. They are the governing achievements of their respective sentences.

Take this with you

In narrative reading, preserve who says, thinks, sees, promises and actually does. The same story can establish these different relations without allowing one to stand as a substitute for another. A Centre succeeds only when it respects the difference.

INSTALLATION 39The Touch and the TransformationRead essay + diagnostics ↘

Sentence Craft · Resolve Corpus I · Cinderella 234–243 · written Centre records ↗

In this passage of Cinderella, a pumpkin becomes a coach, animals become attendants, and worn cloth becomes a gown. A glass pair of slippers appears. The extraordinary events make the narrative memorable, but they also offer a difficult test of reading: the reader must not let the expected result arrive before the sentence that states it.

Magic does not cancel grammatical distinctions. A command is different from its fulfilment. An initiating touch is different from the transformation it precedes. “Appeared” need not name a cause, and “like” makes a comparison rather than an identity.

234 · An instruction

Her fairy godmother told her to fetch a pumpkin from the garden.

This is an instruction, not yet its fulfilment. “Told her to fetch” makes the act directive, while pumpkin and garden determine the specified task.

Operative Centre · 234

The narrative establishment of the fairy godmother's instruction that Cinderella fetch a pumpkin from the garden.

Diagnostic. Replace “told her to fetch” with “watched her admire.” The directive disappears.

235 · Its fulfilment

Cinderella brought the finest pumpkin she could find.

The next sentence reports an achieved act, not a hope or command. “Finest ... she could find” is limited by what she was able to locate. The reading must not turn that comparative into a claim about every pumpkin in the world.

Operative Centre · 235

The narrative fulfilment of the pumpkin-fetching instruction through Cinderella's bringing the finest pumpkin she could find.

Diagnostic. Replace “brought” with “searched for.” Completed delivery becomes an unfinished search.

236 · From pumpkin to coach

The fairy godmother touched it with her wand, and it became a golden coach.

The pronoun “it” carries the pumpkin forward. The wand-touch is narrated, and “became” establishes a changed thing rather than the appearance of a separate object. The story asserts transformation without requiring a naturalistic explanation.

Operative Centre · 236

The narrative transformation of the fetched pumpkin into a golden coach following the fairy godmother's wand-touch.

Diagnostic. Replace “became a golden coach” with “remained a pumpkin.” The central transformation is lost.

237 · Number retained, kind altered

Then she touched six little mice, and they became six fine horses.

Here number is preserved while kind changes. “Then” places the action after the coach's transformation. The sentence does not yet describe the horses being harnessed or moving the coach.

Operative Centre · 237

The narrative transformation of six little mice into six fine horses through the fairy godmother's subsequent touch.

Diagnostic. Replace the becoming with “ran away as mice.” The animals remain mice and the transformation disappears.

238 · A role created

She touched a rat, and he became a coachman.

A rat now becomes a figure with an occupational role. There is no narrated driving action. The Centre concerns the change, not the performance of the newly acquired role.

Operative Centre · 238

The narrative transformation of a rat into a coachman through the fairy godmother's touch.

Diagnostic. Replace “became a coachman” with “remained a rat.” The new identity and role vanish.

239 · An appearance and a role

She touched six lizards, and they became footmen in splendid coats.

The same six creatures pass into a new role and appearance. “Splendid coats” belongs to the resulting picture; it is not evidence of later service. The sentence establishes the transformation, not their conduct.

Operative Centre · 239

The narrative transformation of six lizards into splendidly coated footmen through the fairy godmother's touch.

Diagnostic. Replace “became footmen in splendid coats” with “remained lizards.” The resulting role and appearance disappear.

240 · The touch before the result

At last, the fairy godmother touched Cinderella’s worn dress with her wand.

This sentence is a diagnostic trap for readers who already know the next line. It says the worn dress was touched, not that it has yet become a gown. “At last” places the touch as the culmination of a sequence; the transformation belongs to what follows.

Operative Centre · 240

The narrative culmination of the fairy godmother's sequence of wand-touches in an action directed at Cinderella's worn dress.

Diagnostic. Replace “touched” with “admired.” The culminating wand-action is no longer stated.

241 · The result at last

The plain cloth became a beautiful gown that shimmered like moonlight.

Now the result is asserted. “Became” establishes transformation, while “like moonlight” compares the shimmer rather than converting the cloth into moonlight. The reading must preserve both the changed garment and the simile's status.

Operative Centre · 241

The narrative transformation of plain cloth into a beautiful gown whose shimmer is compared to moonlight.

Diagnostic. Replace “became a beautiful gown” with “remained plain cloth.” The new garment is no longer achieved.

242 · Appearance without a stated agent

On Cinderella’s feet appeared a pair of glass slippers.

The verb “appeared” establishes the slippers' arrival without specifying a separate action by Cinderella or the godmother. A reader may infer a magical cause from context, but the sentence itself narrates an appearance.

Operative Centre · 242

The narrative appearance of a pair of glass slippers on Cinderella's feet.

Diagnostic. Replace “appeared” with “were imagined.” The material arrival on her feet disappears.

243 · The scope of grace

Cinderella looked more graceful than anyone in the kingdom.

The claim concerns how Cinderella looks, not every aspect of her character or a contest she has won. “Anyone in the kingdom” determines the scope of the comparison. A familiar tale may invite praise, but the Centre must retain the specific appearance-based claim.

Operative Centre · 243

The narrative establishment of Cinderella's appearance as more graceful than that of anyone else in the kingdom.

Diagnostic. Replace the comparison with “as graceful as some people nearby.” The asserted comparative pre-eminence vanishes.

The most instructive pair

Sentences 240 and 241 must not be collapsed. First the fairy godmother touches Cinderella's worn dress with her wand. Then the plain cloth becomes a beautiful gown. Knowing the story might lead us to read the result backward into the touch, but the source establishes these as separate achievements. The diagnostic for the first tests the wand-action; the diagnostic for the second tests the transformation.

The same distinction appears at the opening of the batch. Cinderella is told to fetch a pumpkin; only in the next sentence does she bring one. An instruction is neither completed compliance nor evidence that compliance is impossible. Sentence Craft depends on recognising the exact point at which something becomes established.

There are further qualifications. The best pumpkin is the finest she could find, not necessarily the finest in existence. The six mice become six horses, preserving count through transformation. The shimmering gown is described through a simile, not literal moonlight. Cinderella's grace is asserted comparatively as an appearance, not as a judgement of moral worth.

Take this with you

Even in a fairy tale, what has happened and what has merely been prepared must remain distinct. The governing achievement appears where the sentence makes it actual. The reader's task is to discover that exact point, preserve the qualifications that accompany it and resist borrowing certainty from the next line.

INSTALLATION 38What Is Said Is Not Yet DoneRead essay + diagnostics ↘

Sentence Craft · Resolve Corpus I · Cinderella 224–233 · written Centre records ↗

Cinderella has completed her work. That fact might appear to settle the condition laid down by her stepmother. Yet the next ten sentences do not move directly from completion to fulfilment. They establish laughter, an exclusionary statement, a departure, solitude, tears, light, an unfamiliar visitor, a declaration of identity, an assurance and a wondering response.

This sequence is particularly demanding because readers know what the story is preparing. If we read ahead, the light already becomes magical, the old woman already becomes unquestionably a fairy, and the future assurance already becomes a completed journey. The craft asks us to resist precisely this acceleration.

224 · Laughter after completion

Then the stepsisters laughed at her.

“Then” matters because this response follows the completed work. Laughter directed “at her” is not neutral laughter in the abstract. Yet the sentence alone does not state the words or motive. A reader should not insert the next sentence's objection before examining this one.

Operative Centre · 224

The narrative establishment of the stepsisters' laughter directed at Cinderella immediately after her work was completed.

Diagnostic. Replace the laughter with “congratulated her.” The response's quality changes while its timing and recipients remain.

225 · A spoken exclusion is not a law

“You cannot go to the ball dressed like that,” they said.

The statement is an objection in direct speech, not a narrator's unconditional proof of impossibility. “Dressed like that” provides the stated grounds. We can read the cruelty of the moment without converting their words into a universal fact about Cinderella's future.

Operative Centre · 225

The establishment of the stepsisters' stated exclusion of Cinderella from the ball on the grounds of her present dress.

Diagnostic. Replace the reason with “because the ball was cancelled.” The refusal would no longer concern her clothing.

226 · Departure is not arrival

They climbed into their carriage with their mother and rode away to the palace.

A departure is now narrated. Carriage, companions and palace destination are specified. But leaving for the palace is not the same as arriving there, nor does this sentence explicitly narrate Cinderella's loneliness.

Operative Centre · 226

The narrative departure of the stepsisters and their mother together by carriage toward the palace.

Diagnostic. Replace “rode away to the palace” with “remained at the house.” The destination may remain mentioned, but the departure disappears.

227 · Solitude becomes explicit

Cinderella stood alone in the kitchen.

Now solitude is explicitly established, not merely inferred from the carriage's departure. The kitchen is her location; “alone” gives the condition of her presence. Neither sorrow nor a long duration is stated by this sentence.

Operative Centre · 227

The narrative establishment of Cinderella's solitary presence in the kitchen.

Diagnostic. Replace “alone” with “beside her stepsisters.” The scene no longer establishes solitude.

228 · A visible sign of sorrow

Her eyes filled with tears, and she sat beside the hearth.

Two elements are joined: tears and a movement to sit by the hearth. The tears supply visible evidence of distress; the hearth locates her. We should not confuse an external sign with a fully stated explanation of everything she thinks or feels.

Operative Centre · 228

The narrative establishment of Cinderella's tearful distress as she sits beside the hearth.

Diagnostic. Replace the tears with eyes “shining with laughter.” The sitting remains, but the tearful quality disappears.

229 · Light before its explanation

Then a soft light shone in the room.

This sentence is easy to overread because the familiar tale suggests magic. Yet it asserts only a new light, softly shining, after the prior scene. Its source is not named. “Then” gives a temporal turn, not a mechanism.

Operative Centre · 229

The narrative arrival of a soft light in the room after Cinderella's tearful moment.

Diagnostic. Replace the light with the statement that “the room remained dark.” The new luminous occurrence is removed.

230 · The visitor before her name

An old woman stood before her, dressed in a silver cloak and carrying a wand.

The woman now becomes present before Cinderella, and her appearance is given. A wand may invite speculation, but neither an identity nor a magical act has yet been asserted. The sentence establishes a figure's presence and description.

Operative Centre · 230

The narrative introduction of an old woman before Cinderella, identified in appearance by a silver cloak and a wand.

Diagnostic. Replace “stood before her” with “was remembered from long ago.” The same woman could be described, but the immediate encounter disappears.

231 · A claim of identity

“I am your fairy godmother,” said the old woman.

Direct speech introduces a new kind of fact: a claim about the speaker's identity. The sentence establishes that the woman makes that claim. It does not yet demonstrate any powers. To read her role as wholly verified by this sentence would exceed the evidence given here.

Operative Centre · 231

What has been established—and what has not

The ten sentences present a continuous scene. Their continuity matters, but the Centre of one sentence does not swallow the others. The stepsisters' spoken denial matters as a claim made by characters; it is not identical to the narrator determining that Cinderella can never go. The carriage's departure matters as an event; it is not yet the arrival at the palace. The luminous occurrence matters as a new event; it is not itself a disclosed cause or demonstration of magic.

The most delicate shift comes between the old woman's appearance and her declaration. A wand and silver cloak are descriptions. “I am your fairy godmother” is a self-identification through speech. “You shall go to the ball” is a prospective assurance. Treating those as one indisputable accomplished result would flatten three separate achievements.

Do not make a narrator's event out of a character's promise, or an explained cause out of a suggestive appearance. In a story, the difference between what is seen, what is said and what is accomplished is often the very substance of the reading.

Three neighbouring achievements

Three moments deserve to be read in deliberate contrast. First, “wished that she might go too” establishes a desire. Second, “You may go when all your work is finished” establishes permission under a condition. Third, “At last, the house was clean and the work was done” establishes fulfilment of the stated work condition. None of these sentences, alone, asserts that Cinderella has entered the ball.

The distinction can be missed because the reader recognises the story's narrative direction. But the exact record must keep desire, permission and completed work apart. To say “she was finally allowed to attend” at the first moment would erase uncertainty; to say she had attended at the last would invent an event.

The diagnostic must strike the point on which the particular achievement depends, rather than merely producing a new version of the plot.

In a sequence of narrative sentences, the future can appear obvious without yet being established. Let each sentence earn only its own event, condition, response or completed state. Continuity helps us resolve references, but it does not license anticipation as fact.

1. Finding something by removing possibilities

It is easy to paraphrase this as saying that binary search finds an item efficiently. But the sentence identifies the means through which that method can locate a target. Operative Centre

The establishment of repeated elimination of half the remaining search interval as the means by which binary search can locate a target in a sorted array.

The sorted array is an essential condition. The repeated narrowing is the stated means, and can locate is not a promise that every target is present. Encryption transforms readable information into a form that requires an appropriate key to recover the original information.

There is a transformation here, but transformation alone is not enough. Information changes from a readable form into another form. What matters is that recovery of the original now requires an appropriate key. The key is not merely an object associated with encryption; it is presented as required for recovering the information.

Operative Centre

The establishment of encryption as a transformation that makes recovery of the original readable information dependent on an appropriate key.

The establishment of a checksum's limited capacity to detect some accidental alterations in transmitted data.

Operative Centre · A-187

The establishment of an immune-signal-driven rise in the body's temperature set point during infection as a condition under which fever can occur.

The distinction between temperature and temperature set point is important.

The sentence is not simply describing a thermometer reading. It identifies a change in the body's regulatory setting.

It also says can occur, not must occur.

Replace "raise the body's temperature set point" with "leave the body's temperature set point unchanged."

The altered sentence would remove the regulatory change on which this particular account depends.

The words describing infection and immune signalling might remain, but they would no longer establish the same relationship.

This is a characteristic difficulty in scientific reading. A familiar outcome can distract the reader from the particular condition through which the sentence makes that outcome intelligible.

5. A negation must keep its exact reach

A-188 is brief:

Antibiotics that target bacteria do not directly kill viruses.

The sentence establishes a limit.

But what exactly is limited?

A careless reading might say that antibiotics cannot affect a viral illness in any way. Another might say simply that antibiotics are treatments for bacteria rather than viruses.

The second comes nearer the stated distinction, but the governing achievement is more precise.

The Centre is:

Operative Centre · A-188

The establishment that antibiotics directed against bacteria do not themselves directly kill viruses.

The word directly must remain.

Its presence restricts the negative claim to direct killing of viruses. The sentence does not settle every possible indirect effect that antibiotic use might have in circumstances involving a viral infection.

The diagnostic is simple: remove "directly."

The altered sentence would make a broader claim that is no longer explicitly limited to direct antiviral killing.

A small deletion would therefore change the scope of what the sentence denies.

This teaches a particularly demanding form of attention.

When a sentence establishes that something does not happen, the reader must discover exactly what is being denied. An unqualified negation can become a different achievement from a carefully restricted one.

6. A transformation directed toward excretion

A-189 reads:

The liver converts ammonia produced during amino acid metabolism into urea for excretion.

Here several relations belong together.

The ammonia arises during amino acid metabolism. The liver converts it into urea. The resulting substance is directed toward excretion.

A summary such as "the liver processes waste" is not necessarily false, but it leaves the governing transformation largely unspecified.

The Centre is:

Operative Centre · A-189

The establishment of hepatic conversion of ammonia arising from amino acid metabolism into urea as a transformation directed toward excretion.

The original sentence does not say merely that one chemical becomes another. The phrase for excretion gives the transformation an identified purpose.

Nor does it state that the liver itself performs the final act of excretion.

The distinction between preparing something for excretion and excreting it is important.

Replace "into urea for excretion" with "into another compound for storage."

The liver would still perform a conversion, but the product and its stated destination would be different.

The original achievement depends upon the particular transformation and its excretory direction.

The sentence shows how a process can be intelligible not only through what happens, but through what its product is for.

7. An improved capacity is not an immediate event

A-190 states:

Regular aerobic exercise can improve the heart's ability to deliver oxygenated blood during physical activity.

The sentence presents regular aerobic exercise as capable of improving a functional capacity.

This is not simply a claim that the heart beats faster during exercise.

Nor does it say that every exercise session necessarily produces an improvement.

The Centre is:

Operative Centre · A-190

The establishment of regular aerobic exercise as capable of enhancing the heart's capacity to deliver oxygenated blood during physical activity.

"Regular" qualifies the exercise.

"Can" preserves the possibility rather than promising a uniform result.

"Ability" identifies the improvement as a capacity of the heart, while the final phrase identifies the circumstances in which that capacity operates.

A diagnostic helps separate the governing achievement from a nearby reading.

Replace "the heart's ability to deliver oxygenated blood during physical activity" with "the heart's rate of beating during a single exercise session."

The altered statement would concern a particular measurement during a session. The original concerns the possibility of improving a functional capacity through regular exercise.

The difference is not merely one of duration. It is a difference between a capacity that can be enhanced and an event that can be observed.

The reader must identify which has actually been established.

8. A binary digit is determined by its alternatives

A-191 marks a change of subject:

A binary digit represents one of two possible values.

There is no physiological mechanism here.

The sentence identifies a representational restriction.

The Centre is:

Operative Centre · A-191

The establishment of a binary digit as a representation restricted to one of exactly two possible values.

The word binary might tempt a reader simply to think of zero and one.

Those are the usual symbols in binary notation, but the sentence's governing achievement is the two-valued representational structure.

One digit represents one value chosen from two possibilities.

It does not represent an unrestricted range of alternatives.

Replace "two possible values" with "three possible values."

The modified sentence would still describe a representational symbol, but the defining two-valued restriction would be absent.

Notice what this diagnostic teaches.

The Centre is not necessarily the most familiar example associated with the sentence. It is the relationship that makes the sentence's claim what it is.

9. Instructions govern operations

A-192 reads:

A computer program follows instructions that specify operations on data.

At first glance, we could say that a computer program processes data.

That description leaves something essential unresolved: how are its operations determined?

The sentence supplies an answer through instructions.

The Centre is:

Operative Centre · A-192

The establishment of specified instructions as governing the operations on data followed by a computer program.

"Follows instructions" states the program's relationship to its specification.

"That specify operations on data" identifies what the instructions determine.

The instructions are not incidental information that happens to accompany the program.

They govern the operations described.

Replace the central wording with "performs unspecified operations on data."

The program might still act upon data. But the specified relationship between instructions and operations would disappear.

This distinguishes an account of activity from an account of how that activity is governed.

A Centre must preserve the difference.

10. Arrangement is not yet sorting

The final sentence, A-193, reads:

A sorting algorithm arranges items according to a specified ordering rule.

This is particularly revealing because an inadequate reading can preserve almost every noun and verb.

We might say that a sorting algorithm rearranges items.

That is true to part of the sentence, but it misses what makes the arrangement an instance of sorting.

The arrangement is governed by a specified ordering rule.

The Centre is:

Operative Centre · A-193

The establishment of a specified ordering rule as the governing condition of the arrangement produced by a sorting algorithm.

The sentence does not require the rule to be alphabetical or numerical. It does not say that every sorting algorithm uses the same rule.

It establishes that the arrangement conforms to whatever ordering rule has been specified.

Consider the alteration:

A sorting algorithm arranges items in an arbitrary sequence.

The items would still be arranged. But the rule-governed achievement would be absent.

The word sorting would lose the relation through which the original sentence makes it intelligible.

Here the diagnostic reveals a distinction between doing something to items and doing something according to a determining standard.

A sorting algorithm does not merely produce a sequence. The sentence makes its work intelligible through the rule to which the sequence conforms.

11. What these ten readings teach

This batch does not yield one universal interpretation of functional language.

Vaccination prepares for a possible later response. Haemoglobin is established as the predominant carrier. Diaphragm contraction contributes to inhalation. Fever may occur through a change in temperature regulation.

Antibiotics are subject to a limit on direct action. The liver performs a transformation directed toward excretion. Regular aerobic exercise can enhance a capacity.

A binary digit is defined through two representational possibilities. A computer program follows specified operations. A sorting algorithm produces an arrangement governed by a rule.

These achievements are distinct.

The common discipline is not to assign them the same form. It is to ask what makes each sentence's particular claim sufficient.

Sometimes a single word carries the difference.

Most establishes predominance. Helps identifies contribution. Can preserves possibility. Directly restricts negation. For excretion gives a transformation its stated direction. According to makes an ordering rule governing.

A paraphrase can preserve the familiar activity while losing any one of these relationships.

An Operative Centre must find and preserve the relation on which the achievement depends.

Take this with you

A sentence may describe an action, but its governing achievement often lies in what determines, qualifies or directs that action.

Read beyond the familiar verb without leaving the actual sentence.

Ask what would remain if the decisive relationship were removed. If an activity survived but its specific intelligibility disappeared, the diagnostic has revealed something essential.

Do not make a contribution exclusive, a possibility certain, a predominant share total, or a specified rule arbitrary.

The craft advances through such distinctions.

The reader learns not merely to recognise what happens, but to discover what makes the sentence's particular achievement hold together.

That remains the work of Sentence Craft.

The previous instalment examined how a small qualification can govern the achievement of a scientific sentence. A word such as nonnegative, generally or can may determine the exact reach of a claim.

The next ten sentences bring a related difficulty into view.

They concern things that perform functions: parts of the brain, sensory pathways, sleep, the heart, insulin and the kidneys.

It is tempting to read such sentences as simple descriptions of what these things do. Yet a functional description may establish a means, a condition, an improvement, a contribution, a contrast or a coordinated set of activities.

These are not interchangeable achievements.

The discipline of reading is to distinguish them without adding an explanation that the sentence has not supplied.

1. Importance is not exclusivity

Consider A-174:

The hippocampus is important for forming many new episodic memories.

A reader who knows something about memory might immediately say that the hippocampus is the brain's memory centre.

That sounds informative, but it goes beyond the sentence.

The hippocampus is described as important, not as solely responsible. Its contribution concerns forming, not every aspect of storing or recalling memories. And the sentence says many new episodic memories, not all memories.

The governing achievement is therefore:

Operative Centre · A-174

The establishment of the hippocampus as an important contributor to the formation of many new episodic memories.

There is a subtle difference between identifying a contribution and assigning exclusive responsibility.

A contribution can be important without being the only contribution. It can apply to many instances without applying to every instance.

If we replace "forming" with "recalling", we alter the specified function while retaining the sentence's other qualifications.

The sentence's achievement disappears even though its general subject—brain activity and memory—remains.

This is the first lesson of the batch: an accurate Centre often depends on resisting the most familiar general description of a subject.

2. Finding the direction of a pathway

A-175 states:

Visual signals from the retina travel toward the brain through the optic nerve.

The sentence establishes a pathway.

But a pathway is not merely the name of something through which information passes. Its intelligibility depends on the relationship between origin, direction and route.

The visual signals originate in the retina. They travel toward the brain. The optic nerve is the specified pathway.

Our Centre identifies the optic nerve as the pathway carrying retina-originating visual signals toward the brain.

A weaker reading would say that vision depends on the connection between the eye and brain.

That may be a useful broader observation, but the sentence describes the transmission of signals. It does not describe visual experience as a whole or explain how the brain interprets what it receives.

Replace "through the optic nerve" with "without passing through the optic nerve," and the source and destination remain. What disappears is the pathway that the sentence specifically establishes.

The diagnostic shows why the nerve is not an incidental anatomical detail.

It is the means through which the sentence connects the signals' origin with their directed movement.

3. Improvement requires its stated means

Now read A-176:

Repeated practice can improve performance on a motor skill by changing neural processing.

There is a very plausible first reading: practice makes people better at skills.

That is familiar and, in many circumstances, true. But it is not exact enough.

The sentence establishes a possible improvement, and it identifies a particular means through which that improvement can occur.

The governing achievement is the establishment of neural-processing change as a means through which repeated practice can improve motor-skill performance.

Two short expressions hold that achievement together.

Can prevents us from claiming that repeated practice invariably improves performance.

By changing prevents us from treating neural processing as something merely associated with practice.

The distinction between by and while is especially useful.

A person might practise a skill while all sorts of things are happening. An accompanying occurrence does not necessarily explain the improvement.

But the sentence does not say merely that practice happens while neural processing changes. It says that repeated practice can improve performance by changing that processing.

If the mechanism is removed, the general possibility of improvement survives, but the sentence's particular explanatory achievement is lost.

4. Selectivity is not necessarily a trade-off

A-177 requires more careful discrimination:

Attention can improve the processing of selected information while other information receives less processing.

The sentence establishes a contrast.

Some information is selected for improved processing. Other information receives less processing.

It is tempting to explain this as a competition in which limited attention is taken away from one task and assigned to another.

That may describe some circumstances. But the sentence itself does not specify a limited pool of resources or assert that reducing other processing is the cause of the improvement.

Its governing achievement is more restrained:

Operative Centre · A-177

The establishment of attention's capacity to favour selected information through improved processing alongside reduced processing of other information.

The word alongside matters in this formulation. It preserves the joint occurrence without converting the contrast into an explicitly stated causal mechanism.

There is also a modality to preserve. Attention can improve processing. The sentence does not say that it must do so.

A useful diagnostic changes the second clause so that all other information receives equally improved processing.

The altered sentence would still describe improvement. But it would no longer establish the original pattern of unequal processing.

The governing achievement is not improvement alone. It is selective improvement in contrast with lesser processing elsewhere.

This sentence teaches us that a contrast is not automatically a causal explanation.

5. The quiet force of support

A-178 is short:

Sleep supports the consolidation of some forms of memory.

Its brevity makes it easy to overread.

We may know that sleep is involved in many aspects of learning and memory. We may want to describe how it affects neuronal connections or how sleep stages contribute to memory processing.

None of that is supplied here.

The sentence establishes a supporting relationship.

Its Centre is the establishment of sleep as a supporting condition for the consolidation of some forms of memory.

Three restrictions matter.

First, supports does not mean guarantees.

Second, consolidation is not simply the first formation of a memory or the subsequent act of recalling it.

Third, some forms prevents the statement from becoming a claim about every kind of memory.

The Centre must not make sleep the sole cause of consolidation or imply that all memories are consolidated through sleep.

There is a valuable distinction between a sentence that establishes the existence of a process and one that establishes something's contribution to that process.

Sleep is not described here as the whole explanation of memory consolidation. It is established as supporting that process within a qualified scope.

6. Combining information is more than receiving it

A-179 reads:

The brain combines information from multiple senses to estimate properties of the environment.

This sentence contains an achievement that may be missed if we focus only on the number of senses involved.

A reader might say that the brain receives information from many senses.

But reception is not combination.

The sentence states that the brain brings information from multiple senses together to estimate properties of the environment.

The Centre is:

Operative Centre · A-179

The establishment of multisensory information combination as a means by which the brain estimates properties of its environment.

There is a purposive or functional relationship in the words to estimate.

Information is not merely combined and then left without a stated function. The combination is directed toward an estimative achievement.

Nor should estimate be replaced by know perfectly.

An estimate can be useful while remaining uncertain or mistaken. The sentence does not guarantee the accuracy of the result.

Here is the diagnostic: replace "combines information from multiple senses" with "receives information separately from multiple senses."

The altered sentence would retain several sources of information. It would lose their combination as the stated means of estimation.

The strongest rival reading—that the brain receives information about the environment through different senses—is a faithful description of part of the content, but misses the governing relationship between combination and estimation.

The lesson is simple and demanding: having several inputs is not the same as establishing what is achieved by bringing them together.

7. Damage can reveal difference without establishing exclusivity

A-180 states:

Damage to particular brain regions can impair specific cognitive functions while leaving others relatively intact.

A reader might use this sentence to argue that particular brain regions have fixed and exclusive mental functions.

That is a substantially stronger claim than the sentence makes.

The sentence establishes that damage can have differentiated consequences. Some cognitive functions may be impaired while others remain relatively intact.

Its Centre is:

Operative Centre · A-180

The establishment that damage to particular brain regions can produce selective cognitive impairment, with some functions affected while others remain relatively intact.

Can preserves possibility.

Specific prevents the impairment from being described as necessarily general.

Relatively intact prevents the preservation from becoming a claim that the other functions are entirely unchanged.

Notice also the role of while. It joins impairment and comparative preservation into one intelligible pattern.

If we remove the preserved functions, we are left with the claim that brain damage can impair cognition. That is true to part of the sentence, but not to its distinctive achievement.

A stronger diagnostic replaces "while leaving others relatively intact" with "while impairing all other cognitive functions equally."

The altered sentence would no longer establish differentiated consequences.

The governing achievement depends on the possibility that some functions are affected more than others.

We should not infer from that possibility that each mental function belongs exclusively to a single region. The sentence has not established that further conclusion.

8. One organ, two reaches of circulation

A-181 turns from cognition to circulation:

The heart pumps blood through the lungs and the rest of the body.

At first, the Centre seems obvious: the heart pumps blood.

But that restatement omits the scope established by the sentence.

The heart's pumping function is presented as extending through both the lungs and the rest of the body.

The Centre is the establishment of the heart's pumping function as driving blood through both the lungs and the rest of the body.

The sentence does not detail the chambers of the heart or the sequence through which blood travels.

Those details belong to a fuller physiological explanation. They are not required to recognise the achievement here.

The distinction between the lungs and the rest of the body is important. Replace the final phrase with "through the lungs alone," and the broader circulatory reach is lost.

The lesson is not that every conjunction automatically creates the Centre. It is that, in this particular sentence, the two domains together determine the stated scope of the pumping function.

9. Helping does not mean being indispensable

Consider A-182:

Insulin helps many body cells take up glucose from the blood.

The ordinary verb helps is doing precise scientific work.

It establishes a facilitating contribution. It does not establish that insulin is the only thing involved, that every body cell depends upon it, or that its presence guarantees a particular amount of glucose uptake.

The Centre is:

Operative Centre · A-182

The establishment of insulin as a facilitator of glucose uptake from the blood by many body cells.

Two qualifications preserve its scope.

Helps identifies the character of insulin's contribution.

Many identifies the restricted range of cells concerned.

Compare this with the statement that insulin is required by every body cell before glucose can enter.

That would describe a universal necessary condition, not the qualified facilitation established in the original.

A reader who substitutes necessity for help may sound as though they are offering a stronger scientific explanation. In fact, they have changed the achievement.

This is one of the central disciplines of reading functional language: do not turn a contribution into an exclusive requirement.

10. Filtration is not the whole of regulation

The batch closes with A-183:

The kidneys filter blood and regulate the excretion of water and dissolved substances.

A quick paraphrase might say that the kidneys remove waste from the blood.

That is an intelligible account of part of kidney function, but it does not preserve what the sentence establishes.

The sentence names both filtration and regulation of excretion.

The first identifies an activity performed upon blood. The second identifies control over the excretion of water and dissolved substances.

The Centre is:

Operative Centre · A-183

The establishment of kidney function as combining blood filtration with regulation of the excretion of water and dissolved substances.

The word regulate is essential.

A filter that simply allowed everything it encountered to pass out would not establish the same function as an organ that regulates what is excreted.

Replace "regulate the excretion of water and dissolved substances" with "pass all filtered material directly out of the body."

The altered sentence would no longer preserve the regulatory achievement.

Here, as in the heart sentence, two functions are stated together. But their relationship differs. The heart sentence establishes the reach of one pumping activity across two parts of the body. The kidney sentence establishes a function through two related activities, filtration and regulation.

The craft does not impose one interpretation upon sentences because they share a grammatical conjunction.

It asks what the particular conjunction joins and why that joining matters.

11. What this batch teaches

These ten sentences reveal how difficult a familiar functional statement can be.

The hippocampus contributes to formation. The optic nerve supplies a pathway. Practice can improve performance through neural change. Attention can favour selected information. Sleep supports consolidation. The brain combines sensory information for estimation.

Elsewhere, damage produces differentiated consequences; the heart pumps blood through two circulatory domains; insulin facilitates uptake; and the kidneys both filter and regulate.

These are different achievements.

Some establish means. Some establish contributions. Some identify the scope of a function. Others establish a possibility, a contrast or the coordinated activities through which an organ's work becomes intelligible.

Their common scientific setting does not determine how they should be read.

A reader who knows the subject very well may be especially tempted to fill in the mechanisms and explanations that the sentence leaves unstated.

Knowledge is valuable. But the task here is not to produce the fullest available account of a phenomenon.

It is to discover exactly what the sentence itself establishes.

Take this with you

A function is not yet an Operative Centre. The Centre is the precise achievement that the sentence establishes through that function.

Ask what contribution, condition, means, contrast or reach has actually been made intelligible.

Then test it.

If changing helps to requires alters the claim, preserve the distinction. If changing by to while destroys the mechanism, preserve the means. If removing relatively intact loses selective preservation, do not reduce the sentence to impairment alone.

The craft advances when we learn not merely to recognise what a sentence is about, but to respect the exact limits of what it has achieved.

That is the responsibility of reading.

INSTALLATION 32When One Word Governs the WholeRead essay + diagnostics ↘

Sentence Craft · Resolve Corpus B · A-164–A-173 · approved Centre records ↗

There is a temptation, when reading a scientific sentence, to believe that understanding its subject is enough.

We recognise the derivative, the integral, probability, the neuron. We may even know the relevant mathematical formula or biological process. But familiarity with the subject does not yet tell us whether we have found the sentence's Operative Centre.

A sentence can be scientifically familiar and still be difficult to read exactly.

In these ten sentences, the difficulty often lies in a modest expression: instantaneous, nonnegative, independent, generally, by itself, can.

Such expressions may look like qualifications added to the main information. In fact, they may determine what the sentence is able to establish at all.

The reader's responsibility is to discover that determination.

1. The difference between change and its rate

Consider A-164:

The derivative of a differentiable function gives its instantaneous rate of change.

An immediate paraphrase is that derivatives tell us how functions change.

That is true, but it is too broad. A function's change could be described by comparing its values at two different points. The sentence makes a more specific mathematical achievement: it establishes a rate of change at an instant.

The Operative Centre is:

Operative Centre · A-164

The establishment of differentiation as determining a differentiable function's rate of change at an instant, rather than merely over an interval.

The word instantaneous is not an embellishment. Without it, we could confuse the derivative with an average rate of change over an interval.

Nor may we forget differentiable. The sentence does not say that every function has a derivative at every point.

Notice what the Centre does. It does not merely repeat that a derivative gives a rate. It identifies the determination that makes the statement mathematically distinctive: the passage from change considered across an interval to change measured at a point.

2. An equality held in place by its conditions

A-165 reads:

The definite integral of a nonnegative continuous function over an interval equals the area beneath its graph on that interval.

This looks like a familiar geometric description of integration. Its apparent simplicity can conceal the importance of one restriction.

Nonnegative is essential.

For the function described, the definite integral equals the ordinary geometric area beneath its graph over the specified interval.

But a definite integral can include negative contributions when the function takes negative values. Ordinary geometric area does not become negative merely because part of the graph lies below the horizontal axis.

Our proposed Centre is:

Operative Centre · A-165

The establishment of an exact equality between the definite integral of a nonnegative continuous function and the geometric area beneath its graph over the same interval.

The decisive diagnostic is to remove nonnegative.

The resulting assertion would claim the same ordinary-area equality for continuous functions that may take negative values. The original relationship would no longer hold generally.

This teaches an important discipline. A mathematical condition is not background information that can be discarded once the result is understood.

Sometimes the condition is part of what makes the result true.

3. A number can govern in different ways

A-166 and A-167 concern two familiar ways of describing numerical data.

The mean of a finite set of numbers equals their sum divided by the number of values.
The median of an ordered numerical dataset separates its lower and upper halves.

A reader might give both sentences the same general description: they explain measures of central tendency.

That description is useful as background knowledge, but it does not identify either Operative Centre.

The mean is established through a numerical operation upon the total and the count. Its Centre is the determination of the arithmetic mean by distributing the total numerical sum equally across the number of values.

The median is established through its position within ordered data. Its Centre is the central separating relationship between the lower and upper halves.

These are distinct mathematical achievements.

The mean's defining operation depends on summation and division by the number of values. The median's defining relationship depends on the ordering of observations.

Changing the mean's divisor from the number of values to the largest value destroys the operation that determines the arithmetic mean.

Removing the ordering from the median sentence, meanwhile, removes the explicit arrangement in which the lower and upper halves are identified by position. The underlying values may still have a median; the point is that the sentence's stated account depends on their order.

There is a second lesson here.

The median sentence does not tell us how to calculate the median for every possible dataset. In particular, it does not spell out the procedure for an even number of observations.

A reader may know that procedure. But possessing additional knowledge does not authorise us to insert it into the Centre.

The craft requires the sentence's achievement, not everything that can correctly be said about its subject.

4. The mathematical force of independence

A-168 gives us a particularly revealing condition:

Independent events have a joint probability equal to the product of their individual probabilities.

A plausible reading would be that the sentence explains how to calculate the probability of two events occurring together.

But that could apply to many probability problems. It misses the exact condition under which the multiplication rule stated here holds.

The Centre identifies independence as the condition under which joint probability equals the product of the individual probabilities.

Consider a diagnostic.

Replace Independent events with Mutually exclusive events.

The two expressions may be confused in ordinary conversation. Mathematically, however, they establish different relationships.

Mutually exclusive events cannot occur together. Their joint probability is zero, although their individual probabilities may both be positive.

The original product relationship would therefore no longer hold generally.

The diagnostic demonstrates something more important than the usefulness of precise terminology. It shows that the sentence's mathematical achievement depends on a particular relationship between events.

We must find that relationship, not merely recognise the subject of probability.

5. The difference between improvement and guarantee

A-169 introduces a more subtle difficulty:

A larger random sample generally reduces the sampling variability of a sample mean when observations are independent and have finite variance.

There are several ways to read this sentence badly while saying something that sounds reasonable.

We might say that larger samples always produce more accurate answers. We might say that the observations become less variable when the sample grows. Or we might say simply that more data is better.

None is the exact achievement.

The sentence concerns the sampling variability of the mean. That is not the same as the variability of the observations themselves.

It also says generally, not always.

And it specifies conditions: the observations are independent and have finite variance.

The proposed Centre establishes increased random sample size as generally reducing the sampling variability of the mean under those conditions.

The diagnostic changes sampling variability of a sample mean to variability of the individual observations.

What is lost is the distinction between the statistical behaviour of the estimator and the dispersion of the data from which it is calculated.

The importance of a qualifier is not always that it reduces certainty. Sometimes it identifies exactly which quantity the claim concerns.

6. What a sentence refuses to establish

A-170 reads:

A correlation between two variables does not by itself establish that either variable causes the other.

Here the achievement is not the establishment of a new cause. It is the establishment of a limit upon inference.

The phrase by itself governs the sentence.

Correlation alone is insufficient to demonstrate causation in either direction. But the sentence does not say that a causal relationship is absent.

There is an important difference between:

  • Having insufficient evidence to establish a causal relationship.
  • Having established that no causal relationship exists.

The first is the sentence's achievement. The second is not.

Our Centre identifies the inferential insufficiency of correlation alone to demonstrate causation in either direction between two variables.

A reading that says "correlation proves there is no causation" would reverse the epistemic force of the sentence.

This is a particularly important lesson for the craft. A sentence may achieve something by establishing the limits of what is known.

To preserve such an achievement, the reader must not fill the resulting space with an answer the sentence has deliberately left unestablished.

7. Means, thresholds and enhancement

The final three sentences turn from mathematical and statistical relations to neuronal communication.

A-171 states:

Neurons communicate across many synapses by releasing chemical neurotransmitters.

The crucial word is by.

It identifies neurotransmitter release as a means of communication. Replace by with while, and release becomes something that accompanies communication rather than necessarily serving as its means.

The Centre therefore concerns communication through chemical neurotransmitter release, not merely the coexistence of two activities.

A-172 states:

A neuron can generate an action potential when changes in its membrane voltage reach a sufficient threshold.

Here two expressions must be held together: can and sufficient threshold.

The sentence establishes a threshold-conditioned possibility. It does not assert that any membrane-voltage change will generate an action potential, or that reaching the threshold guarantees an action potential in every circumstance.

The Centre is the establishment of a sufficient membrane-voltage threshold as the condition under which an action potential can be generated.

The threshold defines the relevant condition. Can preserves the modality.

Finally, A-173 states:

Myelin increases the speed of electrical signal conduction along many nerve fibers.

A plausible but incorrect rival reading would say that myelin makes electrical conduction possible.

That is not what the sentence establishes.

It attributes an increase in conduction speed to myelin along many nerve fibres.

The difference between enhancing a process and making that process possible is decisive. A factor may improve the speed of something that can occur without it.

The word many also matters. The statement is not framed as a universal claim about every nerve fibre.

These three sentences teach different lessons. One concerns the means of communication; another concerns a threshold-conditioned possibility; the third concerns an enhancement of speed.

Their common subject matter does not give them a common Centre.

8. What this batch teaches

These ten readings make an exacting demand upon the reader.

The governing achievement may lie in an equality, but only under its conditions. It may lie in a positional relationship rather than an arithmetic operation. It may be a possibility rather than a certainty, a comparative tendency rather than a guarantee, or an explicit limit on inference rather than a positive causal conclusion.

The challenge is not to produce a more impressive description of the subject.

It is to find what the sentence has actually made intelligible and to discover why that achievement holds.

The diagnostic helps, but only when it tests something essential. Changing nonnegative tests an equality's domain. Changing by to while tests whether a mechanism has genuinely been stated. Confusing correlation with causation exposes a limit on inference.

A good diagnostic makes the reader see what would be lost.

A good Centre makes the reader see why that loss matters.

Take this with you

The smallest qualification may carry the largest responsibility.

Read the whole sentence. Find the relation that makes it sufficient. Preserve the conditions under which it remains true.

Then ask whether the Centre has identified that relation, or merely repeated the information surrounding it.

That question is the continuing discipline of Sentence Craft.

A craft lesson · drawn from the written Operative Centre readings of the Resolve corpus ↗

Reading is often treated as the extraction of information. We ask what a sentence says, and then give an account of its topic. But a sentence may tell us several true things while achieving one governing determination. To find that determination is a more exacting task. It is an art of reading.

Call it Sentence Craft. The name matters. A method can be written down as a sequence of instructions. A craft must be acquired by practising judgements that instructions alone cannot make. The three questions—What changed? What made it sufficient? How does it remain achieved?—discipline attention. The diagnostic disciplines discrimination. Neither produces the Centre mechanically.

Not everything true is the Centre

We built clocks to measure time, then arranged our lives around them.

It is true that clocks measure time. It is true that shared timekeeping helps coordinate daily activity. It is true that technology shapes social routines. These are substantial readings, not foolish ones. Yet the sentence's governing achievement is the reversal of practical dependence: instruments made for our purposes become structures around which we organise our lives. The word then makes the movement legible; the second clause completes it. Remove that clause and the reversal disappears.

Here lies the difficulty of the craft. A rival interpretation may be accurate about part of the sentence and still fail to identify what holds the whole together. A good exercise does not ask us to reject nonsense. It asks us to distinguish the governing reading from other intelligent readings.

There are no near misses

A Centre is not a score for semantic resemblance. Either the proposed reading identifies the governing intelligible achievement, or it identifies something else. Two different wordings may name the same Centre; one elegant and one awkward can be equally exact. Conversely, a polished explanation can remain a mistake if it substitutes a theme, a consequence, or a background cause for the decisive relation.

This does not mean that the reader is infallible. Readers can disagree, hesitate and revise. The claim is about what is being judged, not about our certainty: identity of achievement is not approximate identity. We learn by exposing where a rival reading changes the sentence's work.

The diagnostic is a craftsperson's test

At chemical equilibrium, the forward and reverse reactions occur at equal rates.

To say that equilibrium is stable may be relevant, but it is not yet the achievement named here. The sentence establishes the equality of two ongoing reaction rates. It does not say that both reactions stop. Replace equal rates with zero rates, and a different claim appears. The diagnostic shows precisely what must survive for this reading to remain the same reading.

Notice the character of this test. It is not a trick for manufacturing an answer. It is a way of checking workmanship after an answer has been proposed. We ask: which alteration would destroy the intelligible achievement I say I have found?

From worked pieces to apprenticeship

The written Centres in Resolve are worked pieces: attempts to read each original sentence in its own wording, to say what governs it, and to make the discrimination inspectable. They are editorial readings, not automatically certified final classifications. The 54-cell coordinates belong to a later and distinct inquiry.

Play Sentence Craft ↗ turns this apprenticeship into a game. Six levels begin with recognising a Centre rather than a subject and move toward contrasts, dependencies, conditions, scientific claims and exact qualifications. Each round offers four developed interpretations. Only one is the recorded Centre; the other three must be taken seriously enough to be rejected for a reason. The score has no partial credit, but the explanation teaches what the score cannot.

Take this with you

The Centre is not everything the sentence tells us. It is what holds everything the sentence tells us together. Read first. Form a judgement. Test its decisive relation. Compare a serious rival. Then do it again. The art of reading cannot be replaced by a formula, but it can be taught, practised, tested and refined. That is why it is a craft.
